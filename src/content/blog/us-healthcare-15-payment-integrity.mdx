# How US Healthcare Works, Part 15: Payment Integrity

[Part 13](/blog/us-healthcare-13-claim-adjudication) showed that adjudication runs fast and mostly without people, and [part 14](/blog/us-healthcare-14-the-claim-sop) showed how examiners handle what the machine can't. Some wrong payments still get through: a claim coded higher than the record supports, a service that wasn't necessary, a bill that another plan should have paid, and sometimes a bill for care that never happened. **Payment integrity** is the set of programs that find these, before payment if possible and after it if not.

## 1. How big the problem is

Medicare measures it carefully. Each year a contractor samples traditional Medicare claims, requests the medical records and checks whether each payment was correct. For fiscal year 2025, the [estimated improper payment rate](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/fiscal-year-2025-improper-payments-fact-sheet) was 6.55%, or \$28.83 billion, down from 7.66% the year before.

| Claim type | Improper payment rate, FY2025 |
|---|---|
| Hospital inpatient (paid by DRG) | 3.15% |
| Part B (physicians, outpatient services) | 8.44% |
| Other Part A providers (hospital outpatient, skilled nursing, home health, hospice and others) | 6.67% |
| Durable medical equipment | 24.12% |

An improper payment isn't the same as fraud. Most of these are **documentation errors**: the record didn't support what was billed, or a required form was missing. Some care was probably appropriate but not properly documented. CMS says so itself. But the number shows the scale: even a careful payer pays roughly one claim dollar in fifteen wrongly.

## 2. Fraud, waste and abuse

The industry splits wrong payments into three kinds, and the response differs:

| Kind | Meaning | Example | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fraud | Deliberate deception for payment | Billing for visits that never happened; paying kickbacks for referrals | Investigation, referral to law enforcement, criminal and civil penalties |
| Abuse | Practices inconsistent with accepted standards that lead to payment, without proof of intent | Routinely billing the highest E/M level | Education, audits, recovery, contract action |
| Waste | Overuse or inefficiency, usually not criminal | Unnecessary repeat tests | Policy, utilization management, edits |

The main federal laws are the **False Claims Act**, which lets the government, and whistleblowers on its behalf, recover up to three times the loss from anyone who knowingly submits false claims, and the **Anti-Kickback Statute**, which forbids paying for referrals of federal program business. Each payer has a **special investigations unit** (SIU) for suspected fraud.

The cases can be large. In June 2025, the Justice Department and HHS's Inspector General announced their [largest national health care fraud takedown](https://oig.hhs.gov/newsroom/media-materials/2025-national-health-care-fraud-takedown/): 324 defendants charged in schemes involving more than \$14.6 billion in alleged intended losses. "Intended loss" means the amount billed, not the amount actually paid.

## 3. Before payment: prepayment programs

Stopping a wrong payment is far cheaper than recovering it later, so payers push as many checks as they can before payment:

- **Coding and clinical edits**, the NCCI and payer edits from part 13, plus licensed edit sets from payment integrity vendors.
- **Prepayment review** of high-risk claims: the claim is held and records requested before payment. Common for very expensive claims, unusual patterns and providers under investigation.
- **Predictive models** that score each claim for risk of being wrong or fraudulent, so the riskiest go to review. Medicare has run a **Fraud Prevention System** that screens claims this way since 2011.

The trade-off is with providers' cash and goodwill. Holding a legitimate claim for weeks to request records is costly for the provider, so payers try to target prepayment review narrowly.

## 4. After payment: recovery programs

| Program | What it finds | How |
|---|---|---|
| Data mining | Payments that break a rule the edits missed | Run new or improved rules over paid claims, sometimes years back within the contract's look-back limit |
| Clinical and DRG validation | Inpatient stays coded higher than the record supports | Coders and clinicians review records, especially for secondary diagnoses that raised the DRG |
| Coordination of benefits recovery | Claims paid as primary when another plan was primary | Match against other insurers' enrollment, then recover from the provider or the other plan |
| Subrogation | Injuries caused by someone else, such as a car accident | Recover the medical costs from the responsible party's insurer |
| Overpayment and credit balance review | Duplicate payments, payment after a retroactive termination | Find and recover, often with the provider's own refund |

Traditional Medicare uses **Recovery Audit Contractors** (RACs), paid a share of what they recover, for post-payment review, and **Unified Program Integrity Contractors** (UPICs) for fraud investigation. Commercial payers use their own teams plus vendors, often paid on contingency.

Recovery usually happens by **offset**: the payer subtracts the overpayment from the provider's next payments, with a letter explaining which claim was adjusted and why. Providers can dispute it, and contracts limit how far back the payer can look.

## 5. Risk adjustment audits

Medicare Advantage adds a payment integrity problem that runs the other way, from the government to the plan. As part 11 mentioned, CMS pays MA plans more for members with more documented conditions. If a plan reports diagnoses its members' records don't support, it's overpaid.

CMS checks this with **Risk Adjustment Data Validation** (RADV) audits, which sample members, request their records and recalculate payment. In May 2025, CMS said it would audit every eligible MA contract every year, a large expansion from the small samples it had audited before. Some of the largest False Claims Act cases of recent years have been about MA risk adjustment.

## 6. Where the data and AI fit

Payment integrity is pattern-finding over very large datasets, so it was one of the first places payers used statistical models:

- **Outlier detection**: a provider who bills a code far more often than peers in the same specialty and region.
- **Network analysis**: links between providers, patients, addresses and bank accounts that reveal fraud rings.
- **Record review**: language models that read medical records and highlight whether they support the billed codes, so a reviewer spends minutes, not an hour.

The risks are the same as in part 10: a model's flag isn't proof, and legitimate providers who are unusual, a specialist with sicker patients, for example, can look like outliers. Good programs use models to decide what a person looks at, not what gets recovered.

## 7. Limits

- **Recovery is slow and incomplete.** Money paid out is hard to get back, especially from providers who close or are under investigation.
- **The provider pays for false positives.** Every legitimate claim held or audited costs the provider staff time and cash.
- **Incentives can distort.** Contingency-fee vendors earn more by recovering more, which pushes toward aggressive audits that providers then appeal, often successfully.
- **Documentation decides.** Most improper payments are about what the record says, so documentation quality matters as much as billing honesty.

## Remember

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Improper payment | A payment that shouldn't have been made or was the wrong amount; 6.55% of Medicare FFS in FY2025 |
| Fraud / abuse / waste | Deliberate deception / improper practice without proven intent / overuse |
| False Claims Act | The law letting the government recover triple damages for knowingly false claims |
| SIU | A payer's special investigations unit |
| Prepayment review | Checking a claim before paying it |
| Data mining | Rules run over paid claims to find errors |
| Subrogation | Recovering costs from whoever caused an injury |
| RAC | Medicare's contingency-paid recovery auditors |
| RADV | CMS audits of Medicare Advantage risk adjustment diagnoses |
