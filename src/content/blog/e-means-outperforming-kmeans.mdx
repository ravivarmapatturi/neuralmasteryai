# e-means, Explained: k-Means That Learns From Small Batches

Vector search, the engine behind RAG and image search, starts by sorting millions or billions of embeddings into groups so a query only has to look inside a few of them. The tool that builds those groups is **k-means**. At that scale the slow part is not the idea, it is the number of times k-means has to read the whole dataset: usually around 25.

In November 2026 a team from Meta FAIR and École des Ponts published **e-means** ([paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09501), [project page](https://e-means.github.io/)). It reaches the quality that standard k-means gets after 25 passes in about **1.2 to 3 passes**. This post builds it up one piece at a time, with a figure for each piece you can play with.

## k-means in one paragraph

You want $k$ groups. Each group has a **centroid**, the point that represents it. k-means repeats two moves: give every point to its nearest centroid, then move every centroid to the average of the points it was given. Each round, the average squared distance from points to their centroid (the **loss**) goes down. The classic version is **Lloyd's algorithm** (1957), and it does each round over the whole dataset.

That is the problem. With a billion points, every single move of every centroid waits for a billion distance calculations. If the starting centroids were bad, you spend a full pass of the data just to find out.

## Why not just use small batches?

Deep learning learns from small batches, so the obvious fix is to move the centroids after every batch. This was tried long ago (MacQueen in 1967, Sculley in 2010). In those versions, a centroid is the running average of **every point ever given to it**. Every batch counts the same, forever.

The trouble is the early batches. They were assigned when the centroids were still in poor places, so many of those points went to the wrong group. A plain running average never forgets them, and the steps a centroid takes get smaller and smaller as its count grows, so it slows down before it has finished moving.

## The e-means update: keep a sum and a count, and let both fade

For every cluster $j$, e-means stores two numbers instead of a centroid:

- $S_j$, a running **sum** of the points given to the cluster, and
- $N_j$, a running **count** of how many points that was.

The centroid is never stored. It is read off as the sum divided by the count: $c_j = S_j / N_j$.

When a batch arrives, each point goes to its nearest centroid. For each cluster, add up the points it got in this batch ($s_j$) and count them ($n_j$). Then blend the old numbers with the new ones using a **step** $\alpha$ between 0 and 1:

$$S_j \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,S_j + \alpha\, s_j \qquad N_j \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,N_j + \alpha\, n_j$$

That is the whole update. Multiplying the old numbers by $(1-\alpha)$ every batch is what makes old batches fade: a batch from $a$ updates ago is weighted by $(1-\alpha)^a$. This kind of fading average is called an **exponential moving average** (EMA), which is where the "e" in e-means comes from.

Why keep a sum and a count instead of just nudging the centroid? Because the count tells the update how much evidence the batch brought. Try it below: with the usual number of points the centroid moves about $\alpha$ of the way to the batch's average; with more points it moves further; with **zero** points the sum and the count shrink by the same factor, so their ratio, the centroid, does not move at all. A cluster that happens to miss one batch is left alone instead of being dragged somewhere.

### The same step with numbers

Take two clusters in 2D. Cluster 1 sits at $(0, 0)$ with $S_1 = (0, 0)$ and $N_1 = 2$. Cluster 2 sits at $(10, 10)$ with $S_2 = (20, 20)$ and $N_2 = 2$. A batch of four points arrives: $(1, 1)$, $(2, 0)$, $(9, 11)$, $(10, 8)$, and the step is $\alpha = 0.2$.

The first two points are nearest cluster 1, so $s_1 = (3, 1)$ and $n_1 = 2$. The other two are nearest cluster 2, so $s_2 = (19, 19)$ and $n_2 = 2$.

$$S_1 = 0.8\,(0, 0) + 0.2\,(3, 1) = (0.6,\ 0.2) \qquad N_1 = 0.8 \cdot 2 + 0.2 \cdot 2 = 2$$

$$c_1 = (0.6,\ 0.2) / 2 = (0.3,\ 0.1)$$

The batch's average for cluster 1 was $(1.5, 0.5)$, and the centroid moved exactly 20% of the way there. Cluster 2 does the same: $S_2 = 0.8\,(20, 20) + 0.2\,(19, 19) = (19.8, 19.8)$, $N_2 = 2$, so $c_2 = (9.9, 9.9)$, 20% of the way from $(10, 10)$ to its batch average $(9.5, 9.5)$.

### Lloyd's algorithm is the extreme case

Set the batch to the whole dataset and $\alpha = 1$. Then $(1-\alpha) = 0$ wipes out the old sum and count, $S_j$ becomes the sum of all the cluster's points, $N_j$ their number, and $S_j / N_j$ is their average. That is exactly one round of Lloyd's algorithm. The paper's point is that this extreme is a poor choice: smaller batches with a smaller step make far more progress per pass over the data.

## Big moves first, small moves at the end

Early on, centroids are far from where they belong and should move boldly. Near the end they should settle. So e-means does not keep $\alpha$ fixed. It starts at a peak value $\alpha_0$ and lowers it along a cosine curve to one tenth of that by the last update ([paper, eq. 5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09501)):

$$\alpha(t) = \alpha_0 \left(0.1 + 0.9 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}\left(1 + \cos\tfrac{\pi t}{M}\right)\right)$$

Here $t$ counts updates and $M$ is the total number of updates in the run. Drag the marker to see the step at any point of training.

## Fixing clusters that go empty

Sometimes a centroid ends up somewhere no points are nearest to it. It then gets nothing, never moves, and is wasted. The paper's ablation finds that handling this is the single most important part of the recipe: without it the training loss is about 3% worse (Table 1).

Every five batches, e-means looks for clusters whose count has dropped below a tenth of the average count. For each one it picks a busy **donor** cluster, with bigger clusters more likely to be chosen, and copies the donor's centroid twice with a tiny nudge in opposite directions. The donor's count is split between the two copies. One copy stays with the donor, the other takes over the empty slot, and the next few batches pull them apart so each ends up with about half of the old cluster. Step through it:

## The finishing step

After the last pass, each centroid is replaced by the plain average of the points it was given during that last pass. The assignments are already known, so this costs almost nothing, and it can only lower the training loss. For applications that care about new data rather than the training set, the paper also describes a second, slower-fading set of sums and counts kept on the side.

## Choosing the batch size and the step without guessing

Older mini-batch methods left you to guess the batch size $B$ and the step. The authors ran a large sweep and fitted two formulas that pick both from the size of the problem: $n$ points, $k$ clusters and $T$ passes ([paper, section 3.3 and Table 2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09501)):

$$B = 0.035\, k^{0.13}\, n^{0.88}\, T^{0.81} \quad \text{(rounded to a power of two)}$$

$$\alpha_0 = \min\!\left(0.824\, k^{-0.46}\, B^{0.90}\, n^{-0.45}\, T^{-0.11},\ 1\right)$$

Plugging in one million points, 16,384 clusters and 3 passes gives $B = 65{,}536$ and $\alpha_0 \approx 0.36$ (our arithmetic from the published coefficients). Above two million points the authors divide the batch formula by 8. They fitted these on one dataset and found they carried over to others, though they note the formulas are tuned for high-dimensional embeddings and are not ideal for very small, low-dimensional data.

## Try it: Lloyd against e-means in NumPy

The code below builds 4,000 points in 8 groups, runs Lloyd for 25 passes and e-means for 2 passes with batches of 256, and prints the loss along the way. It leaves out the empty-cluster fix to stay short.

<RunnableCode code={`import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# 8 groups of 500 points each in 2D
centres = rng.uniform(-10, 10, size=(8, 2))
X = np.vstack([c + rng.normal(0, 1.0, size=(500, 2)) for c in centres])
n, k = len(X), 8

def loss(X, C):
    d = ((X[:, None, :] - C[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1)
    return d.min(1).mean()

def assign(X, C):
    return ((X[:, None, :] - C[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(-1).argmin(1)

start = X[rng.choice(n, k, replace=False)]

# Lloyd: every update looks at all n points
C = start.copy()
for epoch in range(1, 26):
    a = assign(X, C)
    C = np.array([X[a == j].mean(0) if np.any(a == j) else C[j] for j in range(k)])
    if epoch in (1, 2, 3, 25):
        print(f"Lloyd    after {epoch:2d} full passes: loss {loss(X, C):.3f}")

# e-means: running sum S and count N per cluster, faded by (1 - alpha) each batch
B, T, alpha0 = 256, 2, 0.4
M = T * (n // B)
S = start * 1e-10
N = np.full(k, 1e-10)
t = 0
for epoch in range(1, T + 1):
    order = rng.permutation(n)
    for i in range(n // B):
        batch = X[order[i * B:(i + 1) * B]]
        alpha = alpha0 * (0.1 + 0.9 * 0.5 * (1 + np.cos(np.pi * t / M)))
        a = assign(batch, S / N[:, None])
        for j in range(k):
            pts = batch[a == j]
            S[j] = (1 - alpha) * S[j] + alpha * pts.sum(0)
            N[j] = (1 - alpha) * N[j] + alpha * len(pts)
        t += 1
    print(f"e-means  after {epoch:2d} pass(es):      loss {loss(X, S / N[:, None]):.3f}")

# Finish: each centroid becomes the plain average of the points it owns now
a = assign(X, S / N[:, None])
final = np.array([X[a == j].mean(0) if np.any(a == j) else (S / N[:, None])[j] for j in range(k)])
print(f"e-means  after finishing step:    loss {loss(X, final):.3f}")
`} />

Two details match the paper: the starting count is $10^{-10}$, so the starting centroids carry almost no weight once real batches arrive, and the starting centroids are just random points from the data. When we ran this on five random datasets, e-means after one pass was within about 1% of Lloyd's loss after 25 passes, and on one of them it finished lower. On 4,000 points Lloyd is fast anyway; the saving matters when one pass over the data takes hours.

## What the authors measured

All of these are the authors' numbers, from the [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.09501) and the [project page](https://e-means.github.io/):

- e-means beats the loss of Lloyd's algorithm after 25 passes in **1.4 to 2.6 passes** (abstract). On the 11 embedding datasets of the VIBE benchmark (text, code, image and multimodal, 200 to 3,072 dimensions) each needs **1.6 to 2.4 passes**.
- On H100 GPUs it is **10 to 45 times faster** in wall-clock time than Flash-KMeans, and on CPU **8 to 15 times faster** than Faiss.
- On DINO10B, a new dataset of 10 billion 1,024-dimensional image embeddings, it trains about 16.7 million centroids in **2.5 passes**, taking 779 GPU hours with exact nearest-centroid search or 22.8 GPU hours with an approximate (IVF) search (Table 3).

## When e-means is the wrong tool

It speeds up k-means; it does not change what k-means can find. Groups still have to be roughly round blobs, because the method minimises straight-line squared distance. Rings, spirals and curved shapes need [spectral clustering](/docs/machine-learning/gmm-spectral-clustering) or [DBSCAN](/docs/machine-learning/dbscan-hdbscan). You still have to choose $k$ yourself. And it is designed for a fixed dataset, such as building a search index; the authors contrast it with neural networks whose embeddings keep changing during training.

## What you can do now

You can explain e-means in four sentences: keep a sum and a count per cluster, fade both a little every batch, start with big steps and end with small ones, and refill empty clusters by splitting busy ones. You can also predict how a centroid will move from one batch, and you know where the speed-up comes from: many small, cheap moves per pass instead of one expensive move.
