# Audio Fundamentals

<ELI5>

A microphone turns sound into a wiggly line. A computer can only store that line as a list of dots, taken thousands of times a second. A list of dots is hard to learn from, because the same word said twice gives very different dots. So we ask a different question of every few hundredths of a second: which notes are playing, and how loud? That chart of notes over time looks much the same each time the word is said, and it is what speech models actually read.

</ELI5>

## Why This Exists

Every audio task (recognising speech, classifying a sound, synthesising a voice) starts with the same problem. Raw audio is a long one-dimensional signal: 16,000 numbers per second for speech, 44,100 for music. The information a model needs, such as which vowel is being spoken, is not in any single number. It is in how the signal oscillates over a few tens of milliseconds. This page follows the standard path from microphone to model input: sampling, then the Fourier transform, then the spectrogram, then the mel scale. At the end you will be able to say exactly what shape of array Whisper reads, and why.

## Digital Audio: Sampling Rate, Bit Depth, Nyquist

- **Sampling rate** is how many times per second the amplitude is measured. 16 kHz is standard for speech, 44.1 kHz for CD-quality music.
- **Bit depth** is how many bits store each measurement. 16-bit audio has 65,536 possible levels.
- **The Nyquist limit**: a signal sampled at rate $f_s$ can only represent frequencies up to $f_s / 2$. Anything higher does not disappear. It folds back and appears as a false lower frequency, called **aliasing**.

Speech carries almost all its intelligibility below 8 kHz, so 16 kHz sampling loses little. Human hearing reaches about 20 kHz, which is why music uses 44.1 kHz.

### A Full Worked Example: A 7 kHz Tone Sampled at 10 kHz

At 10 kHz the Nyquist limit is 5 kHz, so a 7 kHz tone is out of range. The samples it produces are identical to those of a $|7000 - 10000| = 3000$ Hz tone. The Fourier transform of the sampled signal shows a clean peak at 3 kHz, and nothing in the data reveals that the real tone was higher. Sampled at 16 kHz, the same tone peaks at 7 kHz as it should. This is why recorders put an analogue low-pass filter before the sampler: once a frequency has aliased, no later processing can separate it from a real tone.

<RunnableCode code={`import numpy as np

# Aliasing: a 7 kHz tone sampled at 10 kHz (Nyquist = 5 kHz)
fs, f_true, n = 10_000, 7_000, 1_000
t = np.arange(n) / fs
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f_true * t)

spectrum = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x))
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(n, d=1 / fs)
print(f"true tone {f_true} Hz, sampled at {fs} Hz, Nyquist {fs // 2} Hz")
print(f"FFT peak appears at {freqs[spectrum.argmax()]:.0f} Hz")   # |7000 - 10000| = 3000

# Same tone at a high enough sample rate
fs2, n2 = 16_000, 1_600       # 0.1 s again, so bins are 10 Hz apart
x2 = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f_true * np.arange(n2) / fs2)
peak2 = np.fft.rfftfreq(n2, 1 / fs2)[np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x2)).argmax()]
print(f"sampled at {fs2} Hz instead: peak at {peak2:.0f} Hz")

# Try it: set f_true = 4_000. It is below 5 kHz, so the peak is reported correctly.`} />

Bit depth has its own, separate trade-off. Too few bits and the stored waveform moves in visible steps away from the true signal. Each extra bit roughly halves the quantisation error, adding about 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio: 16-bit audio reaches about 98 dB, more than enough for speech models, which are commonly fed 32-bit floats in $[-1, 1]$ after loading.

## The Fourier Transform: Time Domain to Frequency Domain

A waveform tells you when the air pressure changed, not which pitches were present. The **Fourier transform** rewrites a signal as a sum of sine waves and reports how strongly each frequency contributes. On sampled audio we use the discrete version, computed with the fast Fourier transform (FFT) in $O(n \log n)$ time. An FFT of $n$ samples at rate $f_s$ gives $n/2 + 1$ frequency bins spaced $f_s / n$ Hz apart.

## Spectrograms: The Short-Time Fourier Transform

One Fourier transform over a whole clip says which frequencies occur, but not when. For speech, the order of sounds is the message. The **short-time Fourier transform (STFT)** slides a short window along the waveform, takes an FFT of each window, and stacks the results into a **spectrogram**: time on one axis, frequency on the other, energy as colour.

<WaveformSpectrogramDiagram />

In the chart above, two steady tones look like one busy line in the waveform but appear as two flat lines in the spectrogram, and a chirp (a rising tone) becomes a diagonal. The spectrogram makes visible the structure a model needs.

**Window length is a trade-off.** A longer window separates nearby frequencies better but blurs events close in time; a shorter window does the reverse. The product of time resolution and frequency resolution cannot be made arbitrarily small, so choosing a window means choosing where the task needs precision. Speech systems settle on about 25 ms: long enough to resolve the harmonics of a voice, short enough that a consonant does not smear into its neighbours.

## Mel Spectrograms and MFCCs

STFT bins are spaced evenly in Hz, but hearing is not. The difference between 200 Hz and 300 Hz is obvious; between 7,200 Hz and 7,300 Hz it is barely audible. The **mel scale** maps frequency to perceived pitch, $m = 2595 \log_{10}(1 + f / 700)$, and a **mel spectrogram** sums the STFT bins into bands that are evenly spaced in mel. With 80 bands up to 8 kHz, the lowest band is about 22 Hz wide and the highest about 266 Hz wide. Taking the log of the band energies then matches the roughly logarithmic way we perceive loudness.

<MelScaleDiagram />

**MFCCs** (mel-frequency cepstral coefficients) go one step further: a discrete cosine transform over the log-mel bands, keeping the first 13 or so coefficients. The DCT decorrelates neighbouring bands, much as [PCA](/docs/machine-learning/pca-svd) compresses correlated features. MFCCs were the standard input for Gaussian-mixture and hidden-Markov-model recognisers. Neural models usually take log-mel spectrograms directly and learn their own compression, but MFCCs remain a good, cheap baseline for small classifiers.

### Run It Yourself: Whisper's Front End

Whisper resamples all audio to 16 kHz, splits it into 30-second chunks, and computes an STFT with a 400-sample (25 ms) window and a 160-sample (10 ms) hop. That gives 3,000 frames per chunk. Each frame is reduced to 80 log-mel bands (128 in large-v3). So the encoder's input is an array of shape 80 x 3000, whatever was said. The STFT itself has 201 frequency bins, 40 Hz apart.

<RunnableCode code={`import numpy as np

# Whisper's front end: 16 kHz audio, 25 ms window, 10 ms hop, 80 mel bins
sr, win_ms, hop_ms, n_mels, seconds = 16_000, 25, 10, 80, 30
n_fft = sr * win_ms // 1000        # 400 samples per window
hop = sr * hop_ms // 1000          # 160 samples between windows
samples = sr * seconds
frames = samples // hop            # Whisper pads so 30 s gives exactly 3000 frames
print(f"{seconds} s of audio = {samples:,} samples")
print(f"window {n_fft} samples, hop {hop} samples -> {frames} frames x {n_mels} mel bins")
print(f"frequency bin spacing: {sr / n_fft:.0f} Hz across {n_fft // 2 + 1} STFT bins")

def hz_to_mel(f):
    return 2595 * np.log10(1 + f / 700)

def mel_to_hz(m):
    return 700 * (10 ** (m / 2595) - 1)

# 80 mel bands evenly spaced in mel between 0 Hz and Nyquist
edges = mel_to_hz(np.linspace(hz_to_mel(0), hz_to_mel(sr / 2), n_mels + 2))
widths = np.diff(edges)
print(f"lowest mel band is {widths[0]:.0f} Hz wide, highest is {widths[-1]:.0f} Hz wide")

# Bit depth: each extra bit adds about 6 dB of signal-to-quantisation-noise
for bits in (8, 16, 24):
    print(f"{bits}-bit: {2**bits:,} levels, SQNR about {6.02 * bits + 1.76:.0f} dB")

# Try it: set win_ms = 50. Bins get twice as fine in frequency (20 Hz),
# but each frame now blurs 50 ms of speech together.`} />

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why not train on the raw waveform?">

It can be done: wav2vec 2.0 learns directly from waveforms with a stack of 1D convolutions whose first layers behave much like a learned filter bank. The cost is data and compute. A fixed log-mel front end builds in two things the network would otherwise have to learn, invariance to the phase of each frequency and a perceptual frequency spacing, so a model reaches good accuracy with less data. Self-supervised models such as wav2vec 2.0 pay that cost once on large unlabelled corpora. For a supervised model trained from scratch on a modest dataset, log-mel input is usually the better start.

</GoDeeper>

## Common Problems & Fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accuracy collapses on new recordings | Sample rate differs from training | Resample on load; assert the rate |
| Model fails on phone calls | 8 kHz band-limited audio | Train with band-limiting augmentation or fine-tune on telephone data |
| Features dominated by silence | Long quiet stretches | Voice activity detection before feature extraction |
| Training is unstable on log-mels | Log of near-zero energies | Add a small floor before the log; normalise per feature |
| Clicks and harsh artefacts in synthesis | Phase is discarded by the magnitude spectrogram | A neural vocoder (HiFi-GAN) instead of Griffin-Lim reconstruction |

## When *Not* to Use It

| Situation | Better choice |
| --- | --- |
| Large unlabelled corpus available | Self-supervised waveform models (wav2vec 2.0, HuBERT) |
| Phase matters, such as source separation | Complex STFT or waveform models, not magnitude-only mel |
| Music transcription needing exact pitches | Constant-Q transform, whose bins follow musical notes |
| Tiny on-device keyword spotting | MFCCs with a small CNN, cheaper than a full mel pipeline |

## Remember

| Idea | One line |
| --- | --- |
| Nyquist | Rate $f_s$ represents up to $f_s/2$; higher frequencies alias |
| Aliasing | 7 kHz at 10 kHz sampling looks exactly like 3 kHz |
| Bit depth | About 6 dB of signal-to-noise per bit |
| FFT bins | $n/2+1$ bins, $f_s/n$ Hz apart |
| STFT window | Longer gives finer frequency, coarser time |
| Mel scale | $m = 2595 \log_{10}(1 + f/700)$; narrow bands low, wide bands high |
| Whisper input | 16 kHz, 25 ms window, 10 ms hop, 80 x 3000 log-mel per 30 s |
| MFCC | DCT of log-mel; compact baseline features |
