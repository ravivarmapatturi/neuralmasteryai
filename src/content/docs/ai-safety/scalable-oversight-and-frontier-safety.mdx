# Scalable Oversight & Frontier Safety

<ELI5>

Imagine checking the homework of a student who is better at maths than you. You cannot redo every problem yourself. You can ask two students to argue opposite answers and see whose argument holds up, you can check that each step follows from the last instead of only looking at the final number, and you can let them try harder problems only after they have done well on easier ones while you watched. Those are the three ideas on this page.

</ELI5>

The alignment techniques in [Alignment & RLHF](/docs/ai-safety/alignment-and-rlhf) all assume someone can judge whether an output is good. That assumption gets harder to keep as models get more capable. This page covers the methods proposed for that edge, what each one actually relies on, and the deployment practices labs use to limit harm when the methods fall short.

## Intuition: What Do You Do When the Evaluator Isn't the Expert?

Every idea here answers one question: if checking an answer properly needs exactly the expertise the evaluator lacks, what is left to rely on? Debate relies on adversarial scrutiny instead of expertise. Process-based oversight checks the method rather than only the answer. Weak-to-strong training asks whether a strong model can learn the intent behind a weak supervisor's imperfect labels. Staged deployment and capability thresholds limit the damage instead of preventing every mistake. None of them solves the problem alone.

## Scalable Oversight

**Scalable oversight** is the problem of supervising and evaluating an AI system on tasks where the system's own competence may match or exceed the evaluator's — how do you know a model's answer to a hard research question, a complex piece of code, or a nuanced legal analysis is actually correct, if verifying it properly requires exactly the expertise the evaluator doesn't have at that depth? This isn't a hypothetical future problem — it already shows up whenever a non-expert uses an LLM to help with a task (debugging unfamiliar code, drafting in an unfamiliar domain) they can't fully independently verify.

Three approaches actively explored for this problem:

### Debate, Simulated

**Debate**'s claim is that even a judge who can't independently verify a complex claim may still be able to judge which of two competing arguments holds up better under adversarial scrutiny — similar to how a judge or jury doesn't need to be a legal expert to assess which side's argument survives cross-examination. Run a real simulation of exactly that mechanism: a judge who never checks the underlying facts, only compares each round's argument strength:

<DebateConvergenceDiagram />

The judge's confidence in the diagram is an accumulated log-odds update, round by round, from comparing argument strength alone. That is also debate's limitation: the same honest updating converges on the wrong answer just as confidently if the dishonest side's arguments look stronger.

### Run It Yourself: When Debate Helps the Judge

Model each round as a noisy reading of which side's argument is stronger, with the honest side ahead by an average "edge". With an edge of +0.5 the judge is right 69% of the time after one round and 89% after six. With +0.2 the gain is slow: 58% to 69%. With -0.2, where the dishonest side argues better, more rounds make the judge *more* wrong: 42% down to 31%.

<RunnableCode code={`import numpy as np

# A judge who cannot check facts compares argument strength each round.
# The honest debater's evidence is slightly stronger on average.
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
rounds, trials = 6, 10_000

def judge_accuracy(honest_edge, noise=1.0):
    # each round: observed strength difference = edge + noise; judge adds it to log-odds
    diffs = honest_edge + noise * rng.standard_normal((trials, rounds))
    log_odds = diffs.cumsum(axis=1)
    return (log_odds > 0).mean(axis=0)     # P(judge currently favours the honest side)

for edge in (0.5, 0.2, -0.2):
    acc = judge_accuracy(edge)
    print(f"honest edge {edge:+.1f}: P(judge right) after rounds 1..{rounds} = {np.round(acc, 3)}")

# Try it: change judge_accuracy(edge) to judge_accuracy(edge, noise=0.3), as if
# arguments were easy to compare. With edge +0.2 the judge reaches 0.944 by round 6,
# and with edge -0.2 it is wrong 95% of the time: clearer arguments amplify either side.`} />

### Process-Based Oversight, Worked

Rather than only evaluating a final answer (outcome-based), evaluate the *reasoning process* that produced it. A worked example makes the distinction concrete rather than abstract — two traces answering the same question, graded two different ways:

<ProcessVsOutcomeDiagram />

A wrong answer reached through sound reasoning is a more fixable failure than a right answer reached through reasoning that happened to work this time. The reverse is the case scalable oversight worries about most: a plausible final answer built on flawed steps. Lightman et al. (2023) trained reward models on 800,000 step-level human labels for maths solutions (PRM800K) and found that a process-supervised reward model chose correct solutions more often than an outcome-supervised one: 78% against 72% on a subset of the MATH benchmark.

**Recursive reward modelling** changes *who* grades instead of *what* is graded: AI assistants help humans evaluate outputs too complex to judge unaided, for example by summarising a long document or pointing at a suspicious step. **Sandwiching** is the experimental test for such methods: non-experts with AI help try to match expert judgements on tasks where the model is better than the non-experts but worse than the experts, so success can be measured.

### Weak-to-Strong Generalisation

Burns et al. (2023) studied a stand-in for the future problem: fine-tune a strong model (GPT-4) using labels produced by a weaker one (GPT-2-level). The strong model did better than its weak supervisor, recovering a substantial part of the gap to a strong model trained on correct labels, but not all of it, and less on reward modelling for chat preferences than on NLP classification tasks. The open question is whether a strong model learns what the weak supervisor *meant*, or copies its mistakes.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why the judge in the debate simulation is a log-odds sum">

If each round gives evidence $d_i$ about which side is right, and the rounds are independent, Bayes' rule in log-odds form says the posterior log-odds is the prior log-odds plus the sum of each round's log-likelihood ratio. Treating the observed strength difference as that log-likelihood ratio gives the cumulative sum in the code. Two assumptions carry all the weight. Independence: a debater who repeats a misleading point in every round gets counted many times. Calibration: the judge's reading of "stronger argument" must actually correlate with truth. The simulation shows that if calibration fails, more evidence only makes the judge more confidently wrong.

</GoDeeper>

## Interpretability's Role in Safety

None of the scalable-oversight approaches above fully escape depending on the model's own outputs (its arguments, its stated reasoning) — which is exactly what makes [interpretability](/docs/interpretability/roadmap) (inspecting a model's actual internal computation, not just what it says about itself) a genuinely complementary tool rather than a redundant one: a model could in principle produce a plausible-sounding chain of reasoning that doesn't reflect what actually drove its answer, and interpretability tools are among the few approaches that inspect *underneath* the output layer rather than only the output itself.

## Model Behavior Evaluation for Safety

Distinct from the general evaluation methodology in [AI Evaluation](/docs/ai-evaluation/roadmap): safety-specific behavioral evaluation deliberately probes for specific concerning behaviors — does the model comply with harmful requests under adversarial framing (overlapping with [red teaming](/docs/ai-evaluation/human-and-adversarial-evaluation#red-teaming)), does it behave consistently regardless of whether it seems to be "being tested," does it accurately represent its own confidence/uncertainty rather than always answering assertively regardless of actual reliability. This category of evaluation is what actually operationalizes alignment concerns like reward hacking and deception (see [Alignment & RLHF](/docs/ai-safety/alignment-and-rlhf)) into concrete, checkable test suites rather than leaving them as abstract concerns — with the same real detection-power limits shown on that page: a rare enough concerning behavior can hide from a fixed-size test suite the same way a rare deceptive behavior can.

## Frontier Model Safety Practices

Practices specific to the most capable models being trained and deployed, where the stakes of getting evaluation wrong are highest.

### Staged/Gradual Deployment

Releasing a new, more capable model to progressively larger audiences (internal testing, then a limited external group, then general availability) rather than immediately to everyone — giving time to discover safety issues at a smaller blast radius before wide release, the same staged-rollout principle as [canary deployment](/docs/mlops/deployment-strategies#canary-deployment) applied to model capability/safety risk rather than infrastructure risk. The statistics are the same as the deception example on the Alignment & RLHF page, with audience size in place of evaluation-suite size:

### Dangerous Capability Evaluations

Specifically testing whether a new model has crossed a capability threshold that would require additional safety measures before release (e.g. capabilities relevant to cybersecurity misuse, or providing meaningfully uplifting information for causing serious harm) — evaluation designed around specific, predefined risk thresholds rather than general capability benchmarks:

<CapabilityThresholdDiagram />

### External Red-Teaming and Safety Review

Independent, sometimes external evaluation of a frontier model before release, precisely to reduce the risk of a lab's own evaluation being insufficiently adversarial toward its own product — the same "don't grade your own homework" principle behind independent audits in other high-stakes industries.

### Frontier Safety Frameworks

Several labs publish frameworks that tie capability evaluations to required safeguards: Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (2023), OpenAI's Preparedness Framework (2023) and Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework (2024). The shared structure is an if-then rule. Each defines capability thresholds in areas such as biological, chemical, cyber and autonomous-replication risk; runs evaluations on each new model before and during deployment; and commits that a model crossing a threshold is not deployed, or not trained further, until stronger security and deployment safeguards are in place.

## Run It Yourself: How Staged Deployment Finds Rare Issues

An issue that appears once in 2,000 interactions has a 2.5% chance of showing up in 50 internal interactions, 63% in 2,000 trusted-tester interactions, and is all but certain to appear in a 50,000-interaction limited release. An issue at 1 in 200,000 has only a 22% chance of appearing even in that limited release. Staged deployment shrinks the audience exposed to common issues; it does not catch rare ones before wide release, which is why monitoring after release matters as much as testing before it.

<RunnableCode code={`# Staged deployment: chance of surfacing a rare issue at each stage,
# and how many users have been exposed by the time it is first seen.
def detection_probability(p, n):
    return 1 - (1 - p) ** n

p = 0.0005   # the issue appears in 1 of 2,000 interactions
stages = [("internal", 50), ("trusted testers", 2_000), ("limited release", 50_000), ("general availability", 2_000_000)]
for name, n in stages:
    print(f"{name:20} {n:>9,} interactions -> {detection_probability(p, n):6.1%} chance of seeing it")

# Expected interactions until the first sighting is 1/p
print(f"expected interactions before the first sighting: {1 / p:,.0f}")

# Try it: set p = 0.000005 (1 in 200,000). Even the limited release now sees it
# only about 22% of the time; such issues tend to surface first after general release.`} />

Each method moves the trust somewhere: debate trusts that truth is easier to argue, process supervision trusts step-level checks, weak-to-strong trusts generalisation, staged deployment trusts monitoring. Knowing where the trust went tells you what to test.
</NotebookCallout>

## Common Problems & Fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evaluators approve confident but wrong answers | Non-experts cannot verify the content | AI-assisted critique; debate; check sources rather than tone |
| Reasoning traces look sound but do not drive the answer | Stated reasoning is not faithful to the computation | Faithfulness tests; interpretability checks |
| Model behaves differently when it detects a test | Evaluation-awareness | Evaluations that resemble real use; monitoring after deployment |
| Rare harmful behaviour slips through every stage | Detection needs about $3/p$ interactions | Post-release monitoring, abuse reporting, fast rollback |
| Capability evaluations underestimate the model | Weak prompting or no tool access during tests | Elicit with the best available scaffolding before concluding a capability is absent |

## When *Not* to Use It

| Situation | Better choice |
| --- | --- |
| Answers can be checked automatically | Verify directly (tests, proof checkers); no oversight protocol needed |
| The evaluator is an expert in the domain | Direct expert review |
| Low-stakes, easily reversible outputs | Ordinary evaluation and monitoring |

## Remember

| Idea | One line |
| --- | --- |
| Scalable oversight | Supervising a model that may out-perform its evaluator |
| Debate | A judge compares adversarial arguments; works only if truth argues better |
| Process supervision | Grade each step; beat outcome supervision on MATH in PRM800K |
| Recursive reward modelling | AI helps humans evaluate |
| Sandwiching | Non-experts plus AI versus experts, to measure oversight methods |
