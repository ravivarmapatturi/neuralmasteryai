# Quantization & Model Compression

Every modern AI deployment faces an inescapable hardware constraint: physical memory bandwidth and VRAM capacity scale much slower than arithmetic compute capacity. A 70-billion parameter model stored in standard 16-bit floating point requires **140 GB of VRAM** for weights alone—demanding two 80 GB datacenter GPUs and costing tens of thousands of dollars per year.

**Quantization** is the mathematical discipline of mapping continuous high-precision real numbers into compact, discrete, low-bit representations (FP8, INT8, INT4):

$$\text{FP32 (4 bytes)} \longrightarrow \text{FP16 (2 bytes)} \longrightarrow \text{INT8 (1 byte)} \longrightarrow \text{INT4 (0.5 bytes)}$$

This chapter details the mathematical formulas, hardware execution mechanics, outlier channel protection strategies, and production compression workflows behind modern quantization.

<ELI5>

Imagine you have a high-resolution color photograph rendered with 16 million distinct shades. If you need to send that image over a slow satellite connection, transmitting raw 24-bit RGB coordinates for every pixel takes minutes.

Instead, you create a 256-color palette (INT8) or a 16-color palette (INT4). Every pixel in the image is assigned the nearest color number in your palette. To the human eye, the compressed image looks virtually identical to the original, but the file size is **4× to 8× smaller** and downloads almost instantly.

In neural networks, **Quantization** does the exact same thing to model weights: it replaces full-precision floating-point numbers with compact integer palette indices.

</ELI5>

## Why This Exists

When running inference in production, three systems constraints force models into quantized representations:

1. **Hardware Memory Capacity Boundaries**: An enterprise developer workstation or local edge device often has 16 GB to 24 GB of unified memory. An unquantized 70B model (140 GB) cannot load into memory at all. Compressing weights to 4-bit (AWQ/GPTQ) reduces the model footprint to **36 GB**, allowing local execution.
2. **Memory Bandwidth Bottleneck in Generation**: Autoregressive decoding reads every model weight from HBM for each generated token. At FP16 precision, an 8B model requires streaming 16 GB per token. In INT4 precision, that drops to **4 GB per token**, accelerating generation speed by up to 3× to 4× on memory-bound hardware.
3. **Power and Thermal Envelope Limits**: In embedded microcontrollers and mobile phones (Edge AI), integer arithmetic logic units (ALUs) consume significantly less energy than 32-bit floating-point units (FPUs). Operating in INT8 extends battery life and prevents device thermal throttling.

## Uniform Quantization Mathematics

The objective of uniform quantization is to map continuous real floating-point values $x \in [\alpha, \beta]$ into signed integers $q \in [q_{\min}, q_{\max}]$.

### The Quantization Formula

$$q = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{x}{S} \right\rceil + Z, q_{\min}, q_{\max}\right)$$

Where:
- $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ represents rounding to the nearest integer.
- $S \in \mathbb{R}^+$ is the **Scale Factor**, representing the real-valued distance between consecutive discrete integers.
- $Z \in \mathbb{Z}$ is the **Zero-Point**, an integer offset ensuring that real zero ($0.0$) maps exactly to a representable discrete integer without rounding error (crucial for padding tokens and zero-padded tensors).

The scale factor and zero-point are derived from the dynamic range $[\alpha, \beta]$:

$$S = \frac{\beta - \alpha}{q_{\max} - q_{\min}}$$

$$Z = \text{round}\left(\frac{-\alpha}{S}\right) + q_{\min}$$

### Symmetric vs Asymmetric Mapping

- **Symmetric Quantization ($Z = 0$)**: The real dynamic range is symmetric around zero: $[-\max(|x|), \max(|x|)]$. For signed INT8 where $q \in [-127, 127]$:
  $$S = \frac{\max(|x|)}{127}$$
  $$q = \text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor \frac{x}{S} \right\rceil, -127, 127\right)$$
- **Asymmetric Quantization ($Z \neq 0$)**: Uses independent minimum $\alpha = \min(x)$ and maximum $\beta = \max(x)$. Useful for non-negative activation distributions (e.g. post-ReLU or post-Sigmoid), but requires cross-term runtime corrections during matrix multiplication.

### De-quantization

To compute subsequent layers or emit probabilities, discrete integers are projected back into floating-point coordinates:

$$\hat{x} = S \times (q - Z)$$

## Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) vs Quantization-Aware Training (QAT)

Quantization can be applied at two distinct architectural stages:

```
Post-Training Quantization (PTQ):
[Trained FP16 Model] ───► [Calibration Dataset] ───► [INT8 Model] (Fast: < 1 hour)

Quantization-Aware Training (QAT):
[Model Training] ───► [FakeQuant Nodes (STE)] ───► [Fine-Tuning] ───► [INT8 Model] (Gold Standard)
```

### The Straight-Through Estimator (STE) in QAT

Because the rounding operator $\lfloor \cdot \rceil$ is a step function with a derivative of zero almost everywhere ($d\lfloor x \rceil / dx = 0$), standard backpropagation cannot pass gradients through quantization.

**QAT** introduces the **Straight-Through Estimator (STE)**:
- During the **forward pass**: Compute with the discrete quantized value $\hat{x}$.
- During the **backward pass**: Treat rounding as an identity operator ($d\hat{x} / dx \approx 1$), allowing gradients to flow back into high-precision master weights.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: Why activation outliers emerge in transformers > 6.7B parameters">

In 2022, Dettmers et al. discovered an unexpected phase change in transformer internal representations: once model capacity exceeds 6.7 billion parameters, **extreme activation outliers** emerge across specific hidden dimensions.

While these outlier channels constitute less than **0.1% to 1% of all hidden states**, their values reach magnitudes up to 20× to 100× larger than standard activations (e.g. $+18.4$ versus $+0.2$). 

If uniform quantization maps the entire tensor based on these extreme values, the scale factor $S = 22.1 / 127 = 0.174$ becomes coarse. Normal values around $0.05$ round to zero, obliterating non-outlier features and causing perplexity to explode. Modern quantization algorithms (LLM.int8(), AWQ, and SmoothQuant) explicitly isolate or mathematically rescale these outlier channels.

</GoDeeper>

## The 4-bit Frontier: AWQ & GPTQ

When compressing weights below 8 bits (e.g. 4-bit integer weights), naive round-to-nearest (RTN) fails. Two primary methods dominate production serving:

### 1. AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization (Lin et al., MIT)

Instead of evaluating weight magnitudes in isolation, **AWQ** recognizes that weights are only as important as the activations they multiply:
1. Runs a small calibration set (e.g. 128 Wikipedia paragraphs) through the model to measure per-channel activation scales $s_X$.
2. Identifies the top 1% salient weight channels that interact with high-magnitude activation channels.
3. Protects salient weights from quantization noise by applying an per-channel scaling transformation:
   $$W' = W \cdot \text{diag}(s), \quad X' = X \cdot \text{diag}(s)^{-1}$$
4. Quantizes $W'$ to 4 bits with near-zero perplexity loss compared to FP16.

### 2. GPTQ: Generalized Post-Training Quantization (Frantar et al.)

**GPTQ** applies second-order Taylor expansions based on the inverse Hessian matrix ($H = 2 X X^T$). As each weight column is quantized to 4 bits, GPTQ updates all remaining unquantized weights to compensate for the introduced rounding error:

$$w_{\text{remaining}} \leftarrow w_{\text{remaining}} - \frac{w_i - \hat{w}_i}{[H^{-1}]_{ii}} \cdot H^{-1}_{:, i}$$

## A Full Worked Example: Symmetric INT8 Quantization

Consider an activation slice containing 6 float32 values:
`sample_activations = [-3.25, -1.80, -0.05, 0.42, 1.95, 4.88]`.

1. **Calculate Maximum Absolute Bound**:
   $$\max(|x|) = \max(|-3.25|, |-1.80|, |-0.05|, |0.42|, |1.95|, |4.88|) = 4.88$$
2. **Compute Scale Factor $S$**:
   $$S = \frac{4.88}{127} \approx 0.038425$$
3. **Quantize to Discrete Signed Integers**:
   - $q_0 = \text{round}(-3.25 / 0.038425) = \text{round}(-84.58) = -85$
   - $q_1 = \text{round}(-1.80 / 0.038425) = \text{round}(-46.84) = -47$
   - $q_2 = \text{round}(-0.05 / 0.038425) = \text{round}(-1.30) = -1$
   - $q_3 = \text{round}(0.42 / 0.038425) = \text{round}(10.93) = 11$
   - $q_4 = \text{round}(1.95 / 0.038425) = \text{round}(50.75) = 51$
   - $q_5 = \text{round}(4.88 / 0.038425) = \text{round}(127.00) = 127$
   $$q = [-85, -47, -1, 11, 51, 127]$$
4. **Dequantize to Project Back**:
   $$\hat{x} = q \times 0.038425 = [-3.266, -1.806, -0.038, 0.423, 1.960, 4.880]$$
5. **Evaluate Error**:
   - Max absolute error: $|-3.25 - (-3.266)| = 0.01614$ (less than $S/2$).
   - Memory footprint: 24 bytes (Float32) $\rightarrow$ 6 bytes (INT8), exactly **4× memory compression**.

    """Applies symmetric INT8 quantization to an input numpy tensor."""
    max_val = np.max(np.abs(tensor))
    scale = max_val / 127.0
    
    # Quantize to signed 8-bit integers [-127, 127]
    q_tensor = np.clip(np.round(tensor / scale), -127, 127).astype(np.int8)
    
    # De-quantize back to float
    dequantized = q_tensor.astype(np.float32) * scale
    
    # Error metrics
    abs_error = np.abs(tensor - dequantized)
    max_error = np.max(abs_error)
    mean_error = np.mean(abs_error)
    
    return q_tensor, dequantized, scale, max_error, mean_error

## Common Problems & Fixes

1. **Perplexity Explosion from Unprotected Activation Outliers**: Naive 4-bit quantization destroys model output coherence.
   - *Fix*: Use AWQ or SmoothQuant to migrate dynamic range difficulty from activations into weights before applying 4-bit grids.
2. **Slow GEMV Invocations from Dequantization Overhead**: Dequantizing INT4 weights back to FP16 at runtime without fused kernels introduces latency.
   - *Fix*: Use native W4A16 or FP8 Tensor Core kernels (Marlin, ExLlamaV2, or TensorRT-LLM) that execute matrix multiplies directly on packed registers.
3. **Zero-Point Cross-Term Computation Penalties**: Asymmetric quantization slows down microcontroller integer pipelines.
   - *Fix*: Enforce symmetric quantization ($Z=0$) across all weight tensors to eliminate intermediate addition cross-terms.

<NotebookCallout color="green" title="Quantization Selection Matrix">
- **Datacenter GPUs (H100 / Blackwell)**: Use **FP8 (E4M3)** for native Tensor Core hardware throughput without calibration overhead.
- **Serving 70B+ Models on Single GPUs**: Use **INT4 (AWQ)** to fit 70B models into 36 GB VRAM with near-zero perplexity loss.
- **Ultra-Low-Power Microcontrollers (TinyML)**: Use **Symmetric INT8 (Per-Tensor or Per-Channel)** for hardware DSP acceleration with zero floating-point unit (FPU) requirement.
</NotebookCallout>

## When *Not* to Use It

| Situation | Better Choice | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Pre-training and foundational research fine-tuning | Full precision BF16 / FP32 | Low-bit discrete gradients stall optimization and cause gradient underflow during training. |
| Complex multi-step mathematical reasoning on < 3B models | FP16 unquantized | Sub-3B models have insufficient redundant capacity; aggressive 4-bit quantization degrades math reasoning. |
| Safety-critical medical diagnosis and clinical dosage calculation | FP16 with verifiable bounds | Rounding noise can subtly alter confidence calibration and token probabilities in clinical boundaries. |

## Remember

| Idea | Core Rule |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Quantization Formula** | $q = \text{clamp}(\lfloor x / S \rceil + Z, q_{\min}, q_{\max})$. |
| **Symmetric Quantization** | Set $Z=0$ to eliminate cross-term overhead in hardware GEMM kernels. |
| **Activation Outliers** | In models $> 6.7\text{B}$, 1% of channels carry 70% of reasoning; protect them with AWQ. |
| **QAT vs PTQ** | PTQ is fast calibration; QAT uses Straight-Through Estimators for training recovery. |
| **FP8 vs INT4** | FP8 is standard for datacenter inference; INT4 is standard for memory-constrained serving. |
