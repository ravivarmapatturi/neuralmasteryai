# Survival Analysis: Kaplan-Meier & Cox Proportional Hazards, In Full Depth

Some questions aren't "will it happen?" but "when?": how long until a customer cancels, a machine fails, a patient relapses, a loan defaults. These look like regression on a time, except for one complication present in almost every real dataset: for many subjects, the event hasn't happened yet when the data is analysed. Their times are **censored**. This page shows, with numbers, how badly the obvious workarounds go wrong; derives the survival and hazard functions; builds the Kaplan-Meier estimator and the log-rank test for comparing groups; derives the Cox proportional hazards model and the partial likelihood that lets it ignore the baseline hazard; and covers how to check its key assumption and how to evaluate predictions. The Cox model is fitted from scratch on a real dataset of released prisoners and checked against the standard library.

<ELI5>

A phone company wants to know how long customers usually stay. It looks at everyone who joined in the last three years. Some have left, and their stay is known. But many are still customers, and all the company knows is that they've stayed *at least* this long.

Ignoring the people still here makes customers look short-lived, because only the leavers are counted. Pretending the people still here left today is wrong too. Survival analysis uses everyone correctly: someone who's stayed two years and is still here tells you that people do make it past two years, without claiming to know when they'll leave. Everything below is that idea made exact.

</ELI5>

## 1. Censoring, and Why Naive Methods Fail

For each subject we observe a time $t_i$ and an **event indicator** $\delta_i$: $\delta_i = 1$ if the event happened at $t_i$, and $\delta_i = 0$ if the subject was still event-free at $t_i$ when observation stopped. The second case is **right-censoring**: the true event time is somewhere after $t_i$. It arises when a study ends, a subject drops out, or the data is pulled while customers are still active. (Left- and interval-censoring also exist, but right-censoring is by far the most common.)

The standard methods assume censoring is **non-informative**: whether a subject is censored at a given time doesn't depend on their risk at that time, beyond what the model's covariates explain. Customers leaving the dataset because the study ended is fine; patients dropping out *because* they feel worse is not.

Two obvious shortcuts are both wrong, in opposite directions:

- **Treat censored times as event times.** This says customers still subscribed left today, so it understates survival.
- **Drop censored subjects.** This keeps only those whose event happened within the observation window, who are disproportionately the early events, so it understates survival even more.

Section 7 measures both on simulated lifetimes whose true median is 6.93, observed over a window that censors 46% of subjects. Treating censored times as events gives a median of 5.33; dropping them gives 3.23. The Kaplan-Meier estimate, which handles censoring correctly, gives 7.11, within sampling error of the truth.

## 2. Survival and Hazard

Let $T$ be the (random) event time. Two functions describe its distribution:

- The **survival function** $S(t) = P(T \gt  t)$: the probability of still being event-free at time $t$. It starts at 1 and falls toward 0.
- The **hazard function** $h(t)$: the instantaneous event rate at time $t$ among those who've survived to $t$,

$$
h(t) = \lim_{\Delta \to 0}\frac{P(t \le T \lt  t + \Delta \mid T \ge t)}{\Delta} = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)}
$$

where $f$ is the density of $T$. Since $f(t) = -S'(t)$, the hazard is $h(t) = -\tfrac{d}{dt}\log S(t)$, and integrating gives the link between them:

$$
S(t) = \exp\!\Big(-\int_0^t h(u)\,du\Big) = \exp\big(-H(t)\big)
$$

with $H(t)$ the **cumulative hazard**. A constant hazard $\lambda$ gives the exponential distribution, $S(t) = e^{-\lambda t}$, with no memory: an old machine is as likely to fail tomorrow as a new one. Rising hazards describe wear-out, falling ones describe "infant mortality" (new customers are the most likely to cancel). The hazard is the natural quantity to model, because it only concerns subjects still at risk, which is exactly what censored data can inform.

## 3. Kaplan-Meier

The **Kaplan-Meier estimator** (Kaplan and Meier, 1958) estimates $S(t)$ without assuming any distribution. Sort the distinct event times $t_{(1)} \lt  t_{(2)} \lt  \dots$. At each, let $n_j$ be the number of subjects **at risk** (not yet had the event and not yet censored, just before $t_{(j)}$) and $d_j$ the number of events at $t_{(j)}$. Surviving past $t$ means surviving each event time up to $t$, given survival to just before it, so

$$
\hat{S}(t) = \prod_{j:\,t_{(j)} \le t}\left(1 - \frac{d_j}{n_j}\right)
$$

Each factor is the observed fraction of those at risk who got through that event time. Censored subjects contribute exactly what they're known to have done: they count in $n_j$ for every event time up to their censoring time, then leave the risk set without ever counting as an event. The curve is a step function that drops only at event times. It's the nonparametric maximum-likelihood estimate of $S$, and when nothing is censored it reduces to the ordinary fraction of subjects with $T \gt  t$. Greenwood's formula gives its variance, $\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}\big(\hat{S}(t)\big) = \hat{S}(t)^2\sum_{t_{(j)} \le t}\frac{d_j}{n_j(n_j - d_j)}$, from which confidence bands are built. The **median survival time** is where $\hat{S}(t)$ first falls to 0.5. With heavy censoring the curve may never reach 0.5, in which case the median simply isn't estimable from the data, a real limitation that's better reported than hidden.

## 4. Comparing Groups: The Log-Rank Test

Two Kaplan-Meier curves can look different by chance. The **log-rank test** asks whether the difference is real. Under the null hypothesis that both groups have the same hazard, at each event time $t_{(j)}$ the $d_j$ events should be split between the groups in proportion to how many of each are at risk. Group 1 is *expected* to have $E_{1j} = d_j\,n_{1j}/n_j$ events; it actually had $O_{1j}$. Sum the differences over all event times and standardize by their hypergeometric variance $V_j$:

$$
Z = \frac{\sum_j (O_{1j} - E_{1j})}{\sqrt{\sum_j V_j}}, \qquad V_j = \frac{d_j\,n_{1j}\,n_{2j}\,(n_j - d_j)}{n_j^2\,(n_j - 1)}
$$

$Z^2$ is approximately chi-squared with one degree of freedom. The test weights every event time equally, so it's most powerful when one group's hazard is consistently higher; when curves cross, other weightings or a Cox model with time-varying effects are better.

Section 7 applies it to the Rossi recidivism data: 432 men released from Maryland prisons in the 1970s, followed for a year, half randomly assigned to receive financial aid. After 52 weeks, 77.8% of the aided group and 69.4% of the others hadn't been re-arrested, with a log-rank $p$-value of 0.050: borderline evidence that aid helped.

## 5. The Cox Proportional Hazards Model

Kaplan-Meier describes groups; it can't handle several covariates at once or a continuous one such as age. The **Cox model** (Cox, 1972) is regression for hazards:

$$
h(t \mid \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t)\,\exp(\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x})
$$

The **baseline hazard** $h_0(t)$ is any function of time, left completely unspecified. The covariates scale it multiplicatively. Two consequences:

- **Hazard ratios.** For two subjects, $h(t \mid \mathbf{x}_a)/h(t \mid \mathbf{x}_b) = \exp\big(\boldsymbol{\beta}^T(\mathbf{x}_a - \mathbf{x}_b)\big)$, the same at every time. A one-unit increase in feature $j$ multiplies the hazard by $e^{\beta_j}$. This is the **proportional hazards** assumption, and it's what the coefficients mean.
- **The model is semi-parametric**: parametric in how covariates act, nonparametric in how risk changes over time.

**The partial likelihood.** Cox's insight was that $\boldsymbol{\beta}$ can be estimated without knowing $h_0$. At the time $t_i$ when subject $i$ has the event, consider everyone still at risk, the risk set $R(t_i) = \{k : t_k \ge t_i\}$. Given that exactly one of them has the event at that moment, the probability it's subject $i$ is its hazard over the sum of the hazards in the risk set, and the unknown $h_0(t_i)$ cancels:

$$
\frac{h_0(t_i)\,e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_i}}{\sum_{k \in R(t_i)}h_0(t_i)\,e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_k}} = \frac{e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_i}}{\sum_{k \in R(t_i)}e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_k}}
$$

Multiplying over all observed events gives the **partial likelihood**, and its logarithm is

$$
\ell(\boldsymbol{\beta}) = \sum_{i:\,\delta_i = 1}\Big[\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_i - \log\sum_{k \in R(t_i)}e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_k}\Big]
$$

Censored subjects never appear as the event in a term, but they appear in every risk set up to their censoring time, which is how their information is used. Differentiating, with $w_k = e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}_k}$:

$$
\nabla\ell = \sum_{i:\,\delta_i = 1}\Big(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}_i\Big), \qquad \bar{\mathbf{x}}_i = \frac{\sum_{k \in R(t_i)}w_k\mathbf{x}_k}{\sum_{k \in R(t_i)}w_k}
$$

At each event, the event subject's covariates minus the risk-weighted average of everyone at risk. The gradient is zero when, on average, those who have events look like a weighted draw from those at risk. The Hessian is minus a sum of weighted covariance matrices, so $\ell$ is concave and Newton's method converges quickly, which is how Section 7 fits it: in 20 Newton steps the coefficients match the lifelines library to three decimals (the remaining differences come from how tied event times are handled; Section 7 uses Breslow's approximation, lifelines uses Efron's).

On the Rossi data, financial aid has a hazard ratio of 0.685: about 32% lower re-arrest hazard at any given time, holding the other covariates fixed. Each prior conviction multiplies the hazard by 1.095, and each year of age by 0.944.

## 6. Checking Assumptions and Evaluating Predictions

**Proportional hazards.** The model assumes each covariate's effect is constant over time. That fails if, say, a treatment helps early and fades later. Checks:

- Plot $\log(-\log\hat{S}(t))$ against $\log t$ for each group: under proportional hazards the curves are parallel.
- **Schoenfeld residuals** (the event subject's covariate minus the risk-weighted mean, the terms in the gradient above) should show no trend over time; a significant correlation with time indicates a time-varying effect. lifelines' `check_assumptions` runs this test.

When the assumption fails, options include stratifying on that variable (separate baseline hazards per group), adding an interaction with time, or using a model without the assumption.

**Concordance (C-index).** Harrell's C-index generalizes ROC-AUC to censored data. Consider every pair of subjects where the earlier time is an observed event (otherwise we can't know who had the event first). The pair is **concordant** if the model gives the higher risk to the subject with the earlier event. The C-index is the fraction of such pairs that are concordant: 0.5 is random, 1 is perfect. On the Rossi data the Cox model's C-index is 0.640: modest, which is typical of behavioural outcomes. For predictions at a specific time, the time-dependent Brier score, with inverse-probability-of-censoring weights, measures calibration as well as ranking.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: parametric models, competing risks, and machine learning for survival">

**Parametric survival models** assume a distribution for $T$: exponential (constant hazard), Weibull (hazard rising or falling as a power of time), log-normal, log-logistic. They're more efficient when the assumption holds, can extrapolate beyond the observed follow-up, and give directly interpretable times. **Accelerated failure time** models write $\log T = \boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x} + \sigma\epsilon$, so a covariate stretches or shrinks time itself, an often more intuitive reading than a hazard ratio.

**Competing risks** arise when several event types can occur and one prevents the others (a patient can die of the disease or of something else). Treating the other events as censoring then overstates the probability of the event of interest; the cumulative incidence function and the Fine–Gray model handle it correctly.

**Machine learning for survival**: random survival forests (Ishwaran et al., 2008) grow trees whose splits maximize the log-rank statistic between children; gradient boosting can optimize the Cox partial likelihood (supported by XGBoost's `survival:cox` objective and scikit-survival); and neural versions such as DeepSurv replace $\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}$ with a network. They capture interactions and nonlinear effects, at the cost of the simple hazard-ratio interpretation, and are evaluated with the C-index and time-dependent Brier score like any other survival model.

</GoDeeper>

## 7. Minimal Implementation

Kaplan-Meier from the product formula; the censoring-bias comparison on simulated lifetimes with a known median; the log-rank test on the Rossi data; and the Cox model fitted by Newton's method on the partial likelihood, with the gradient and Hessian of Section 5, compared with lifelines:

```python
import numpy as np
from lifelines import CoxPHFitter, KaplanMeierFitter
from lifelines.datasets import load_rossi
from lifelines.statistics import logrank_test
from lifelines.utils import concordance_index

def kaplan_meier(times, events):
    """times: observed time per subject; events: 1 if the event was seen, 0 if censored."""
    event_times = np.unique(times[events == 1])
    s, curve = 1.0, []
    for t in event_times:
        at_risk = np.sum(times >= t)                       # still under observation just before t
        d = np.sum((times == t) & (events == 1))
        s *= 1 - d / at_risk                               # survive this event time, given survival so far
        curve.append((t, s))
    return np.array(curve)

def median_survival(curve):
    below = curve[curve[:, 1] <= 0.5]
    return below[0, 0] if len(below) else np.inf

# 1. Censoring bias, where the truth is known: exponential lifetimes with mean 10, followed for 12 time units
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
true_t = rng.exponential(10, 2000)
start = rng.uniform(0, 8, 2000)                            # people join the study at different times
follow = 12 - start                                        # and are followed until it ends
obs = np.minimum(true_t, follow); event = (true_t <= follow).astype(int)
print(f"{event.mean():.0%} of subjects had the event; the rest are censored")
print(f"true median lifetime                        {10 * np.log(2):.2f}")
print(f"median of observed times, censored as events {np.median(obs):.2f}")
print(f"median of event times, censored dropped      {np.median(obs[event == 1]):.2f}")
km = kaplan_meier(obs, event)
print(f"Kaplan-Meier median                          {median_survival(km):.2f}")
lf = KaplanMeierFitter().fit(obs, event)
print(f"largest gap vs lifelines' Kaplan-Meier curve {np.max(np.abs(km[:, 1] - lf.survival_function_at_times(km[:, 0]).values)):.1e}")

# 2. Real data: 432 released prisoners followed for 52 weeks; event = re-arrest (Rossi et al., 1980)
df = load_rossi()
fin = df["fin"] == 1                                       # received financial aid after release
res = logrank_test(df.week[fin], df.week[~fin], df.arrest[fin], df.arrest[~fin])
s_aid = KaplanMeierFitter().fit(df.week[fin], df.arrest[fin]).survival_function_at_times(52).iloc[0]
s_no = KaplanMeierFitter().fit(df.week[~fin], df.arrest[~fin]).survival_function_at_times(52).iloc[0]
print(f"\nnot re-arrested by week 52: with aid {s_aid:.3f}, without {s_no:.3f}; log-rank p = {res.p_value:.3f}")

# 3. Cox proportional hazards by Newton's method on the partial likelihood (Breslow handling of ties)
cols = ["fin", "age", "race", "wexp", "mar", "paro", "prio"]
X = df[cols].to_numpy(float); T = df.week.to_numpy(float); E = df.arrest.to_numpy()
beta = np.zeros(len(cols))
for _ in range(20):
    eta = np.exp(X @ beta)
    grad = np.zeros_like(beta); hess = np.zeros((len(cols), len(cols)))
    for i in np.flatnonzero(E):
        risk = T >= T[i]                                   # everyone still at risk when subject i had the event
        w = eta[risk]; W = w.sum(); xbar = (w @ X[risk]) / W
        grad += X[i] - xbar
        hess -= (X[risk].T * w) @ X[risk] / W - np.outer(xbar, xbar)
    beta -= np.linalg.solve(hess, grad)
cph = CoxPHFitter().fit(df[cols + ["week", "arrest"]], "week", "arrest")
print("\n           ours      lifelines   hazard ratio")
for c, b, r in zip(cols, beta, cph.params_[cols]):
    print(f"{c:6s}  {b:+.4f}   {r:+.4f}     {np.exp(b):.3f}")
print(f"C-index (higher risk score should mean earlier arrest): {concordance_index(T, -(X @ beta), E):.3f}")
```

- **Censoring handled badly biases everything (Section 1).** Against a true median of 6.93, treating censored times as events gives 5.33 and dropping them 3.23; Kaplan-Meier gives 7.11.
- **The product formula is the estimator (Section 3)**: identical to lifelines to $10^{-15}$.
- **Aid probably helped (Section 4)**: 77.8% against 69.4% not re-arrested after a year, log-rank $p = 0.050$.
- **The partial likelihood gives the Cox fit (Section 5).** Twenty Newton steps reproduce lifelines' coefficients to about three decimals, with the small differences from tie handling. Hazard ratios read directly off the exponentials: aid 0.685, each prior conviction 1.095.
- **Ranking is modest (Section 6)**: C-index 0.640.

## 8. When *Not* to Use Survival Methods

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| No censoring at all (every event observed) | Ordinary regression on time (often on $\log T$) is simpler and fine |
| You only care whether the event happens within a fixed window, and everyone is followed for that long | Binary classification of "event within the window" |
| Censoring is informative (people drop out because of their risk) | Model the dropout, or use methods for informative censoring; standard estimates are biased |
| Several event types that preclude each other | Competing-risks methods (cumulative incidence, Fine–Gray) |
| Effects that change over time | Stratified or time-varying Cox models, or models without proportional hazards |
| Strong nonlinear effects and many features | Random survival forests or gradient boosting with a Cox objective |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| Censoring | For some subjects we only know $T \gt  t_i$; assume it's non-informative |
| Naive shortcuts | Censored-as-events and dropping censored both understate survival |
| Survival and hazard | $S(t) = P(T \gt  t)$; $h = f/S$; $S(t) = e^{-H(t)}$ |
| Kaplan-Meier | $\hat{S}(t) = \prod(1 - d_j/n_j)$ over event times; censored subjects stay in risk sets |
| Log-rank | Observed minus expected events per group, summed over event times |
| Cox model | $h(t \mid \mathbf{x}) = h_0(t)e^{\boldsymbol{\beta}^T\mathbf{x}}$; $e^{\beta_j}$ is a hazard ratio |
| Partial likelihood | Each event: its hazard over the risk set's; $h_0$ cancels |
| Gradient | Event subject's $\mathbf{x}$ minus the risk-weighted average; concave, so Newton works |
| Check | Proportional hazards via log-log plots and Schoenfeld residuals |
| Evaluate | C-index: fraction of comparable pairs ranked correctly |
