# Gaussian Mixture Models & Spectral Clustering

[k-means](/docs/machine-learning/kmeans-hierarchical-clustering) assigns every point to exactly one round cluster, and [DBSCAN](/docs/machine-learning/dbscan-hdbscan) defines clusters by density. This page covers two more ideas. A **Gaussian mixture model** treats clustering as fitting a probability distribution: the data comes from a few overlapping Gaussian bumps, each point gets a *probability* of belonging to each, and the bumps can be stretched and tilted ellipses. It's fitted with the EM algorithm, derived here step by step along with the proof that it always improves. **Spectral clustering** treats clustering as cutting a graph of nearest-neighbour links in its weakest places, which the eigenvectors of a particular matrix reveal. Both get from-scratch implementations checked against scikit-learn.

<ELI5>

Imagine you hear a recording of several people talking at once in a room, and you want to know who said each word. Some words are clearly one voice. Others are muffled and could be either of two people. A good listener doesn't force a guess; they say "probably Anna, maybe Ben". A Gaussian mixture does that for data points: each cluster is a "voice" with its own typical sound, and every point gets a probability for each.

Spectral clustering is like mapping friendships at a school. Draw a line between every pair of students who are friends. Groups appear as clumps with many lines inside and few lines between them, whatever shape they have on the playground. The method finds where to cut the fewest friendships to split the school into groups. Everything below is those two ideas made exact.

</ELI5>

## 1. The Mixture Model

A Gaussian mixture says each point was generated in two steps: first pick one of $k$ components at random, component $j$ with probability $\pi_j$ (the **mixing weights**, which sum to 1); then draw the point from that component's Gaussian, with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}_j$ and covariance $\Sigma_j$. The resulting density is a weighted sum of bumps:

$$
p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)
$$

If we knew which component generated each point, this would be [QDA](/docs/machine-learning/naive-bayes-lda-qda): estimate each class's mean and covariance from its own points. A Gaussian mixture is unsupervised QDA, where the class labels are **latent** (hidden) and must be inferred.

Given a fitted model, the probability that point $\mathbf{x}_i$ came from component $j$ follows from Bayes' rule:

$$
r_{ij} = P(z_i = j \mid \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)}{\sum_{l=1}^{k}\pi_l\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_l, \Sigma_l)}
$$

These $r_{ij}$ are the **responsibilities**: a soft clustering. A point deep inside one component gets $r \approx 1$ there; a point in the overlap between two might get 0.6 and 0.4, which is an honest statement of ambiguity that k-means can't make. Watch soft memberships form as EM runs:

## 2. Fitting It: The EM Algorithm

We want the parameters $\theta = \{\pi_j, \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j\}$ that maximize the log-likelihood of the data,

$$
\ell(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\sum_{j=1}^{k}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)
$$

The log of a sum doesn't simplify, so setting derivatives to zero gives equations with no closed-form solution. But look at the derivative with respect to one mean. Differentiating $\log\sum_l(\cdot)$ gives each component's term divided by the sum, which is exactly $r_{ij}$, times the derivative of the Gaussian's log-density:

$$
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}_j} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} r_{ij}\,\Sigma_j^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_j = \frac{\sum_i r_{ij}\,\mathbf{x}_i}{\sum_i r_{ij}}
$$

The optimal mean is the responsibility-weighted average of the points. But the responsibilities themselves depend on the means. That circularity suggests alternating, and the **Expectation-Maximization** algorithm (Dempster, Laird and Rubin, 1977) does exactly that:

**E-step.** With the current parameters, compute every responsibility $r_{ij}$ by Bayes' rule.

**M-step.** With the responsibilities fixed, re-estimate the parameters as weighted maximum-likelihood estimates. With $N_j = \sum_i r_{ij}$, the "effective number" of points in component $j$:

$$
\pi_j = \frac{N_j}{n}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\mu}_j = \frac{1}{N_j}\sum_i r_{ij}\,\mathbf{x}_i, \qquad \Sigma_j = \frac{1}{N_j}\sum_i r_{ij}\,(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)^T
$$

Each is the formula for fitting one Gaussian, with every point counted fractionally according to its responsibility. Repeat until the log-likelihood stops improving.

It's the same shape as [k-means' Lloyd algorithm](/docs/machine-learning/kmeans-hierarchical-clustering): assign, then update. And it reduces to it exactly: with every $\Sigma_j = \sigma^2 I$, equal weights and $\sigma^2 \to 0$, the responsibilities become hard 0/1 assignments to the nearest mean. k-means is the hard, spherical special case.

**Why EM always improves the likelihood.** For any distribution $q_i(j)$ over components for each point, Jensen's inequality (the log of an average is at least the average of the logs) gives a lower bound:

$$
\ell(\theta) = \sum_i\log\sum_j q_i(j)\,\frac{\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)}{q_i(j)} \;\ge\; \sum_i\sum_j q_i(j)\log\frac{\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)}{q_i(j)}
$$

The bound is tight, equal to $\ell(\theta)$, exactly when $q_i(j) = r_{ij}$, the current posterior. So the E-step raises the bound until it touches the likelihood at the current parameters. The M-step then maximizes the bound over $\theta$ (with $q$ fixed, it's the weighted-estimate problem above, which has closed-form answers). The new likelihood is at least the new bound, which is at least the old bound, which equalled the old likelihood. So the log-likelihood **never decreases**. Like k-means, EM converges, and like k-means, only to a local maximum, so it's normally started from a k-means solution and restarted several times. In Section 6, the log-likelihood rises at every one of 42 iterations and ends equal to scikit-learn's to four decimals.

## 3. Shapes, Singularities and Choosing k

**Covariance types.** The covariance matrices decide the cluster shapes and the number of parameters, the same trade-off as [LDA versus QDA](/docs/machine-learning/naive-bayes-lda-qda#lda-vs-qda). In scikit-learn's terms, for $d$ features:

| `covariance_type` | Shape of each cluster | Covariance parameters |
|---|---|---|
| `spherical` | Round, own size | $k$ |
| `diag` | Axis-aligned ellipse | $kd$ |
| `tied` | Same ellipse for every cluster | $d(d+1)/2$ |
| `full` | Any ellipse, own size and orientation | $k\,d(d+1)/2$ |

`full` is the most flexible and the most likely to overfit. In Section 6, on three sheared, overlapping clusters, the full-covariance mixture recovers the groups better than k-means (ARI 0.871 against 0.808), because it can tilt its ellipses to match.

**Singularities.** As the checkpoint shows, a component can collapse onto a single point and drive the likelihood to infinity. Implementations prevent it by adding a small constant to each covariance's diagonal (`reg_covar`, $10^{-6}$ by default) or by placing a prior on the covariances.

**Choosing $k$ with BIC.** Because a mixture is a probability model, models with different $k$ can be compared on likelihood, penalized for their number of parameters $p$. The **Bayesian information criterion** is

$$
\text{BIC} = -2\,\ell(\hat{\theta}) + p\log n
$$

and smaller is better. The penalty grows with $\log n$ per parameter, so adding a component must improve the fit enough to pay for its mean, covariance and weight. In Section 6 BIC falls from 7,155 at $k = 1$ to its minimum of 6,631 at $k = 3$, the true number, then rises again. That's a tool k-means doesn't have, since inertia always prefers more clusters. **Bayesian Gaussian mixtures** go further, placing a prior on the weights that lets unneeded components shrink to zero weight (`BayesianGaussianMixture` in scikit-learn).

Mixtures also give a density, so they serve as anomaly detectors (points with low $p(\mathbf{x})$) and as generative models (sample a component, then a point).

## 4. Spectral Clustering: Cutting a Graph

Mixtures still assume elliptical clusters. Spectral clustering assumes nothing about shape. It builds a graph and looks for the weakest places to cut it.

**The graph.** Connect each point to its nearest neighbours (or to all points within a radius, or weight all pairs by a Gaussian kernel $e^{-\lVert\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\rVert^2/2\sigma^2}$). Let $W$ be the symmetric matrix of edge weights, and $D$ the diagonal matrix of each node's total weight, its **degree** $d_i = \sum_j W_{ij}$. Two points far apart in space can still be strongly connected through a chain of neighbours, which is what lets a curved cluster hold together.

**The Laplacian.** The **graph Laplacian** is $L = D - W$. Its key property is a quadratic form. For any vector $\mathbf{f}$ assigning a number $f_i$ to each node:

$$
\mathbf{f}^TL\mathbf{f} = \sum_i d_i f_i^2 - \sum_{i,j}W_{ij}f_if_j = \frac12\sum_{i,j}W_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2
$$

(expand $(f_i - f_j)^2$ and use $d_i = \sum_j W_{ij}$ to check it). It's small when strongly connected nodes get similar values. Two consequences:

- $L$ is positive semidefinite, and the constant vector has $\mathbf{f}^TL\mathbf{f} = 0$, so the smallest eigenvalue is 0.
- **The number of zero eigenvalues equals the number of connected components.** If $\mathbf{f}^TL\mathbf{f} = 0$, every edge's term must be zero, so $\mathbf{f}$ is constant within each component. The indicator vector of each component is such an $\mathbf{f}$, so there's one zero eigenvalue per component, and the corresponding eigenvectors *are* the cluster memberships.

**From cuts to eigenvectors.** When clusters aren't completely disconnected, we want the partition that cuts the least edge weight while keeping the groups balanced; otherwise the best "cut" is to snip off one point. The **normalized cut** (Shi and Malik, 2000) measures the cut weight relative to each group's total degree. Finding the exact minimum is NP-hard, but relaxing the cluster indicators from 0/1 values to real numbers turns the problem into finding the eigenvectors of a normalized Laplacian with the smallest eigenvalues. If the graph is *nearly* disconnected, those eigenvectors are nearly the indicators, and the points separate cleanly in their coordinates.

**The algorithm** (Ng, Jordan and Weiss, 2001):

1. Build the similarity graph $W$ and degrees $D$.
2. Form the normalized Laplacian $L_{\text{sym}} = I - D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}$ and take its $k$ eigenvectors with the smallest eigenvalues as columns of a matrix $U$.
3. Normalize each row of $U$ to unit length.
4. Run k-means on the rows of $U$; point $i$'s cluster is its row's cluster.

The eigenvectors give each point new coordinates in which chain-connected points sit together, so k-means, which only handles round blobs, succeeds in the new space where it failed in the original. Section 6 runs this on two half-moons. When the moons are cleanly separated, the 10-nearest-neighbour graph has two components, the Laplacian has exactly two zero eigenvalues, and spectral clustering is perfect (ARI 1.000; k-means on the raw points gets 0.274). When more noise makes the moons touch, the second eigenvalue becomes small but nonzero (0.0012), a few edges cross between the moons, and the result degrades to 0.592. Spectral clustering finds weak links, and it needs them to exist.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what spectral clustering costs, and how it relates to other methods">

The eigen-decomposition of a dense $n \times n$ matrix costs $O(n^3)$ time and $O(n^2)$ memory, so naive spectral clustering stops at around $10^4$ points. With a sparse $k$-nearest-neighbour graph, iterative eigensolvers (Lanczos, LOBPCG, which scikit-learn uses through `eigen_solver="amg"` or `"lobpcg"`) need only the few smallest eigenvectors and scale much further. The Nyström method approximates the graph from a sample of landmark points.

The choice of graph matters as much as the algorithm: the number of neighbours or the kernel width $\sigma$ decides which points are "connected", and a bad choice either fragments clusters or bridges them. That's the same role $\varepsilon$ plays in [DBSCAN](/docs/machine-learning/dbscan-hdbscan).

Spectral methods connect to much of this section. The first few Laplacian eigenvectors are also a nonlinear dimensionality reduction (Laplacian eigenmaps, Belkin and Niyogi, 2003), a close relative of the neighbourhood-preserving methods on the [ICA, t-SNE & UMAP page](/docs/machine-learning/ica-tsne-umap); UMAP initializes its layout with exactly this spectral embedding. And kernel k-means, k-means in a kernel feature space, optimizes an objective equivalent to a weighted normalized cut (Dhillon, Guan and Kulis, 2004).

</GoDeeper>

## 5. Choosing Among the Clustering Methods

| | k-means | DBSCAN / HDBSCAN | Gaussian mixture | Spectral |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cluster shape | Round, similar size | Any shape, dense regions | Ellipses of any orientation | Any shape, weakly linked groups |
| Assignment | Hard | Hard, plus noise | Soft probabilities | Hard |
| Choose $k$? | Yes | No | Yes, with BIC to help | Yes (eigenvalue gap helps) |
| Outliers | Pulled into clusters | Labelled noise | Low density; can flag | Pulled into clusters |
| Scales to large $n$ | Best | Good with an index in low dimensions | Moderate | Worst without sparse approximations |
| Gives a density | No | No | Yes | No |

## 6. Minimal Implementation

EM for a full-covariance Gaussian mixture, written from the E- and M-step formulas of Section 2 and started from k-means as scikit-learn does; BIC for $k = 1$ to 6; then spectral clustering from the normalized Laplacian on two half-moons, cleanly separated and touching:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import logsumexp
from scipy.stats import multivariate_normal
from sklearn.cluster import KMeans, SpectralClustering
from sklearn.datasets import make_blobs, make_moons
from sklearn.metrics import adjusted_rand_score
from sklearn.mixture import GaussianMixture
from sklearn.neighbors import kneighbors_graph

def em_gmm(X, k, iters=1000, reg=1e-6, seed=0):
    n, d = X.shape
    mu = KMeans(k, n_init=10, random_state=seed).fit(X).cluster_centers_   # start from k-means, as scikit-learn does
    cov = np.array([np.cov(X.T) for _ in range(k)])
    pi = np.full(k, 1 / k)
    lls = []
    for _ in range(iters):
        # E-step: responsibilities r_ij = P(component j | x_i), by Bayes' rule in log space
        logp = np.column_stack([np.log(pi[j]) + multivariate_normal(mu[j], cov[j]).logpdf(X) for j in range(k)])
        ll = logsumexp(logp, axis=1)
        r = np.exp(logp - ll[:, None])
        lls.append(ll.sum())
        # M-step: weighted maximum-likelihood estimates
        Nk = r.sum(0)
        pi = Nk / n
        mu = (r.T @ X) / Nk[:, None]
        for j in range(k):
            D = X - mu[j]
            cov[j] = (r[:, j, None] * D).T @ D / Nk[j] + reg * np.eye(d)
        if len(lls) > 1 and lls[-1] - lls[-2] < 1e-8:
            break
    return pi, mu, cov, lls

# Three elongated, overlapping clusters
X, y = make_blobs(n_samples=900, centers=[[0, 0], [4, 1], [1, 4]], random_state=1)
X = X @ np.array([[1.0, 0.6], [0.0, 0.8]])                      # shear them into ellipses
pi, mu, cov, lls = em_gmm(X, 3)
ref = GaussianMixture(3, covariance_type="full", random_state=0, tol=1e-8, max_iter=500).fit(X)
print(f"EM iterations {len(lls)}, log-likelihood never decreased: {bool(np.all(np.diff(lls) >= -1e-9))}")
print(f"mean log-likelihood per point: ours {lls[-1] / len(X):.4f}, scikit-learn {ref.score(X):.4f}")
print(f"ARI vs truth: GMM {adjusted_rand_score(y, ref.predict(X)):.3f}, "
      f"k-means {adjusted_rand_score(y, KMeans(3, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(X)):.3f}")
for k in range(1, 7):
    g = GaussianMixture(k, covariance_type="full", n_init=3, random_state=0).fit(X)
    print(f"k={k}  BIC {g.bic(X):8.1f}")

# Spectral clustering from scratch on two moons, cleanly separated (noise 0.06) and touching (noise 0.1)
import warnings
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore", message="Graph is not fully connected")   # expected for the separated moons
for noise in [0.06, 0.1]:
    Xm, ym = make_moons(n_samples=400, noise=noise, random_state=0)
    W = kneighbors_graph(Xm, 10, include_self=False)
    W = 0.5 * (W + W.T).toarray()                                    # symmetric k-NN similarity graph
    deg = W.sum(1)
    L_sym = np.eye(len(Xm)) - W / np.sqrt(np.outer(deg, deg))        # normalized Laplacian I - D^-1/2 W D^-1/2
    vals, vecs = np.linalg.eigh(L_sym)
    U = vecs[:, :2]
    U = U / np.linalg.norm(U, axis=1, keepdims=True)                 # row-normalize (Ng, Jordan and Weiss)
    ours = KMeans(2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(U)
    sk = SpectralClustering(2, affinity="nearest_neighbors", n_neighbors=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xm)
    km = KMeans(2, n_init=10, random_state=0).fit_predict(Xm)
    print(f"noise {noise}: smallest eigenvalues {np.round(vals[:3], 4)}   ARI spectral ours {adjusted_rand_score(ym, ours):.3f}, "
          f"scikit-learn {adjusted_rand_score(ym, sk):.3f}, k-means {adjusted_rand_score(ym, km):.3f}")
```

Output (scikit-learn 1.7, SciPy 1.16):

```

mean log-likelihood per point: ours -3.6196, scikit-learn -3.6196
ARI vs truth: GMM 0.871, k-means 0.808
k=1  BIC   7155.4
k=2  BIC   6814.5
k=3  BIC   6631.1
k=4  BIC   6677.3
k=5  BIC   6710.9
k=6  BIC   6754.4
noise 0.06: smallest eigenvalues [-0.      0.      0.0015]   ARI spectral ours 1.000, scikit-learn 1.000, k-means 0.274
```

- **EM behaves exactly as derived.** The log-likelihood rose at every step for 42 iterations, and the final value matches scikit-learn's to four decimal places.
- **Ellipses beat circles on ellipses.** The full-covariance mixture recovers the sheared clusters better than k-means (ARI 0.871 against 0.808).
- **BIC finds $k$.** Its minimum is at $k = 3$, the number of clusters the data was generated with.
- **The zero eigenvalues count the components.** With cleanly separated moons, the graph has two pieces, two eigenvalues are zero (printed as $-0$ and $0$ from rounding), and spectral clustering is perfect. With touching moons the second eigenvalue lifts to 0.0012 and accuracy drops: ours 0.592, scikit-learn's 0.408 (it uses a different eigensolver and label-assignment step), both still better than k-means.

## 7. When *Not* to Use These

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Clusters are non-elliptical (curved, nested) and you'd use a mixture | Spectral clustering or [HDBSCAN](/docs/machine-learning/dbscan-hdbscan) |
| Many features with a full-covariance mixture and modest $n$ | `diag` or `tied` covariances, or reduce dimensions first; full covariances need $k\,d(d+1)/2$ parameters |
| Large $n$ with spectral clustering | k-means, mini-batch methods, or a sparse graph with an iterative eigensolver |
| Noise points that shouldn't be assigned anywhere | HDBSCAN, which labels them |
| Clusters connected by bridges of points | Density-based methods, which can treat the bridge as noise; spectral clustering will cut in the wrong place |
| You need hard, simple, explainable groups at scale | k-means |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| Mixture | $p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x} \mid \boldsymbol{\mu}_j, \Sigma_j)$; unsupervised QDA |
| Responsibilities | $r_{ij} = P(\text{component } j \mid \mathbf{x}_i)$ by Bayes' rule: soft clustering |
| E-step | Compute responsibilities from current parameters |
| M-step | Weighted means, covariances and weights, with $N_j = \sum_i r_{ij}$ |
| Why EM works | E-step makes a Jensen lower bound tight; M-step maximizes it; likelihood never drops |
| k-means | Hard EM with identical spherical covariances |
| Singularities | A component on one point has unbounded likelihood; regularize the covariance |
| Choosing $k$ | BIC $= -2\ell + p\log n$; smallest wins |
| Laplacian | $L = D - W$; $\mathbf{f}^TL\mathbf{f} = \tfrac12\sum W_{ij}(f_i - f_j)^2$ |
| Zero eigenvalues | One per connected component; their eigenvectors are the memberships |
| Spectral clustering | Eigenvectors of the normalized Laplacian, then k-means in that space |
