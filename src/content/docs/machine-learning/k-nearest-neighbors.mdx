# K-Nearest Neighbors, In Full Depth

Every model so far, from [linear regression](/docs/machine-learning/linear-regression) to [SVMs](/docs/machine-learning/support-vector-machines), learns parameters during training and then predicts from those parameters alone. k-nearest neighbours learns nothing. It stores the training set and, for each new point, looks up the most similar stored examples and lets them vote. This page shows why something that simple works, with a classic result bounding its error at twice the best achievable; how the one hyperparameter $k$ trades bias for variance; why the choice of distance, and especially feature scaling, matters more than anything else; why it breaks down as the number of features grows, measured rather than asserted; and how neighbour search is made fast enough to use.

<ELI5>

You've moved to a new town and want to guess whether a particular house is expensive. You don't know any formula for house prices. But you can look at the five houses most like it (same street, similar size, similar age) and check what they sold for. If most of them were expensive, this one probably is too.

That's the whole algorithm: to label something new, find the $k$ most similar things you've already seen and go with the majority. Everything below is about making "most similar" mean the right thing, and choosing how many neighbours to ask.

</ELI5>

## 1. The Algorithm

Given a training set and a query point $\mathbf{x}$:

1. Compute the distance from $\mathbf{x}$ to every training point.
2. Take the $k$ closest, the set $N_k(\mathbf{x})$.
3. Classification: predict the most common class among them. Regression: predict their average target.

Written as a formula, the class-probability estimate for class $c$ is just the fraction of neighbours in that class,

$$
\hat{P}(y = c \mid \mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \,\in\, N_k(\mathbf{x})}\mathbb{1}[y_i = c]
$$

and the regression prediction is $\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \tfrac1k\sum_{i \in N_k(\mathbf{x})} y_i$. That's a local average: the model assumes the answer changes smoothly, so points near $\mathbf{x}$ have answers near the answer at $\mathbf{x}$. It makes no other assumption about the shape of the relationship, which is what **non-parametric** means here. The boundary can be any shape the data supports:

**Training is free; prediction is not.** `fit` only stores the data, so k-NN is called a **lazy learner**. All the work happens at prediction time: a brute-force query computes $n$ distances in $d$ dimensions, $O(nd)$ per prediction, and the whole training set must be kept in memory. Every other model in this section is the reverse: expensive to train, cheap to use. Section 6 covers how the search is sped up.

## 2. Why It Works: Twice the Best Possible Error

The **Bayes error** $R^*$ is the lowest error any classifier can achieve on a problem, the error of always predicting the most probable class given $\mathbf{x}$ (see [Naive Bayes](/docs/machine-learning/naive-bayes-lda-qda) for the Bayes classifier). Cover and Hart (1967) proved that as the training set grows without limit, the error of the 1-nearest-neighbour rule $R_{1\text{NN}}$ satisfies

$$
R^* \;\le\; R_{1\text{NN}} \;\le\; 2R^*(1 - R^*) \;\le\; 2R^*
$$

The argument for two classes fits in a few lines. Let $p = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$ at the query point. The Bayes classifier predicts the more likely class, so its error at $\mathbf{x}$ is $\min(p, 1-p)$. With infinitely many training points, the nearest neighbour of $\mathbf{x}$ is arbitrarily close to it, so its label is an independent draw with the same probability $p$. The 1-NN rule is wrong when the query's true label and the neighbour's label differ:

$$
P(\text{error at } \mathbf{x}) = p(1-p) + (1-p)p = 2p(1-p)
$$

and since $\max(p, 1-p) \le 1$, $2p(1-p) = 2\min(p, 1-p)\max(p, 1-p) \le 2\min(p, 1-p)$: at most twice the Bayes error at every point. Averaging over $\mathbf{x}$ (with a little more care, using Jensen's inequality) gives the bound.

Two readings of the result. Half the information about the class is in the nearest neighbour: a rule with no training and no parameters is within a factor of two of the best possible classifier. And the factor of two comes from noise: 1-NN copies its neighbour's label including the neighbour's bad luck. Averaging over more neighbours removes that, and if $k$ grows with $n$ but slower than $n$ ($k \to \infty$ and $k/n \to 0$), k-NN's error converges to the Bayes error itself (Stone, 1977). It's **universally consistent**: given enough data, it learns any decision boundary.

The catch is "given enough data", and Section 5 shows how much that can be.

## 3. Choosing k

$k$ is the bias-variance dial of the [overview](/docs/machine-learning/overview) in its most direct form:

- **Small $k$**: each prediction depends on very few points. The boundary follows the data closely (low bias) and changes a lot from one sample to another (high variance). At $k = 1$, training accuracy is always 100%, since every training point is its own nearest neighbour, which says nothing about new data.
- **Large $k$**: each prediction averages many points, some of them far from the query. The boundary is smooth (low variance) but blurs real structure (high bias). At $k = n$ every prediction is the overall majority class.

For regression the trade can be written exactly. If $y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$, and the training inputs are fixed, the expected squared error of the k-NN prediction at $\mathbf{x}_0$ is

$$
\mathbb{E}\big[(y_0 - \hat{f}(\mathbf{x}_0))^2\big] = \sigma^2 + \Big(f(\mathbf{x}_0) - \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i \in N_k}f(\mathbf{x}_i)\Big)^2 + \frac{\sigma^2}{k}
$$

The first term is unavoidable noise. The second is the bias: how far the average of $f$ over the neighbours is from $f$ at the query, which grows as $k$ grows and the neighbours get further away. The third is the variance of an average of $k$ noisy values, which shrinks as $k$ grows. $k$ is chosen by cross-validation, usually on a roughly logarithmic grid. On the wine data in Section 7, cross-validated accuracy rises from 0.955 at $k = 1$ to 0.967 at $k = 15$, then collapses to 0.753 at $k = 101$, where each prediction averages more than two-thirds of the training set.

Two refinements are common. For binary classification an odd $k$ avoids tied votes. And **distance-weighted** voting gives each neighbour a vote proportional to $1/\text{distance}$, so the nearest neighbours count most, which makes results less sensitive to $k$ (`weights="distance"` in scikit-learn).

## 4. What "Near" Means

### Distance Metrics

Everything k-NN does depends on the distance. The common choices are the Minkowski family,

$$
d_p(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{z}) = \Big(\sum_{j=1}^{d}\lvert x_j - z_j\rvert^p\Big)^{1/p}
$$

with $p = 2$ the **Euclidean** distance (scikit-learn's default) and $p = 1$ the **Manhattan** distance, which grows linearly rather than quadratically with each feature's difference, so a single feature with an outlying value can't dominate as easily. For embeddings and text vectors, where the direction of a vector carries the meaning and its length mostly reflects something incidental (like document length), **cosine distance**, $1 - \tfrac{\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{z}}{\lVert\mathbf{x}\rVert\lVert\mathbf{z}\rVert}$, is the standard choice; it's what retrieval in [RAG](/docs/llms-genai/rag#the-rag-pipeline-end-to-end) uses. For categorical features, Hamming distance (the number of features that differ) or Gower's distance for mixed types.

**Scaling decides everything.** Euclidean distance treats a difference of 1 the same in every feature, whatever the units. On the wine data, the proline measurement has a standard deviation of 314 and the hue measurement 0.12. On raw features, distances are almost entirely proline differences, and the "nearest" neighbours are just the wines with the closest proline level. Cross-validated accuracy is 0.663; after standardizing each feature to mean 0 and variance 1, it's 0.961 (Section 7). Unlike a [decision tree](/docs/machine-learning/decision-tree#strengths-and-weaknesses), which compares one feature at a time with a threshold and doesn't care about units, k-NN must always have its features put on comparable scales, with the scaler fitted inside the cross-validation folds.

Standardizing gives every feature equal weight, which is itself a choice. If some features are irrelevant, they still add noise to every distance. **Metric learning** methods learn a weighting, or a full linear transformation, of the features so that same-class points end up close and different-class points far apart; Neighbourhood Components Analysis (Goldberger et al., 2004) is available in scikit-learn as `NeighborhoodComponentsAnalysis`.

## 5. Why It Breaks in High Dimensions

### The Curse of Dimensionality

k-NN's assumption is that there are training points near the query. In high dimensions there aren't, and two calculations show why.

**Neighbourhoods stop being local.** Spread points uniformly in a $d$-dimensional unit cube, and ask how big a sub-cube around a query must be to contain a fraction $r$ of the data. Its volume must be $r$, so its edge length is

$$
e_d(r) = r^{1/d}
$$

To capture 1% of the data, the edge is 0.10 in 2 dimensions, 0.63 in 10 dimensions and 0.95 in 100 dimensions. In 10 dimensions, the "local" neighbourhood holding 1% of the data spans 63% of the range of every feature. The neighbours aren't near in any useful sense, and the local-average assumption fails (the example is from Hastie, Tibshirani and Friedman).

**Distances concentrate.** In high dimensions, the distances from a query to all the points become nearly equal. For points drawn independently with i.i.d. coordinates, the squared distance is a sum of $d$ independent terms, so its mean grows like $d$ while its standard deviation grows like $\sqrt{d}$; relative to the mean, the spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$. Beyer et al. (1999) proved that under broad conditions the ratio of the nearest to the farthest distance tends to 1. Section 7 measures it on 1,000 uniform random points: the nearest point is 1% as far as the farthest in 2 dimensions, 22% in 10, 72% in 100 and 90% in 1000. When the nearest and farthest neighbours are almost the same distance away, "nearest" carries little information.

Real data is usually kinder than uniform noise: images, text and measurements tend to lie near a much lower-dimensional surface inside their high-dimensional space, and distances along that surface stay meaningful. That's why k-NN on good **embeddings** works well (the embedding model has done the work of finding the low-dimensional structure) while k-NN on raw pixels or thousands of raw features usually doesn't. The standard fixes are to reduce dimensionality first ([PCA](/docs/machine-learning/pca-svd)), select features, or learn the metric.

## 6. Making Neighbour Search Fast

Brute force costs $O(nd)$ per query. For a million training points and many queries, that's too slow, and there are two families of fix.

**Exact search with space-partitioning trees.** A **k-d tree** splits the data recursively along one coordinate at a time; a **ball tree** groups points into nested hyperspheres. At query time, whole branches can be skipped when their bounding region is provably further away than the best neighbour found so far, giving roughly $O(\log n)$ queries in low dimensions. The pruning stops working as $d$ grows (the same distance concentration as Section 5: every region is about equally far), and above roughly 20 dimensions they're often no faster than brute force. scikit-learn's `algorithm="auto"` picks between brute force, k-d trees and ball trees from the data's shape.

**Approximate nearest-neighbour (ANN) search.** Give up the guarantee of finding the exact $k$ nearest, and accept "almost always the right ones, much faster". Graph-based indexes such as HNSW (Malkov and Yashunin, 2018) link each point to a few near neighbours across several layers and search by walking the graph greedily; quantization-based indexes such as those in FAISS compress vectors and search clusters. These answer queries over hundreds of millions of embeddings in milliseconds, which is what makes semantic search and retrieval-augmented generation practical. [Vector Databases](/docs/databases/vector/overview) covers them.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: k-NN as kernel smoothing, and what it shares with the RBF SVM">

The k-NN regression prediction is an average of training targets with weight $1/k$ on the $k$ nearest points and 0 on the rest. Replace that hard cut-off with a smooth weight that decays with distance, $K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i) = \exp(-\lVert\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_i\rVert^2/2h^2)$, and normalize:

$$
\hat{f}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\sum_i K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)\,y_i}{\sum_i K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}_i)}
$$

That's the **Nadaraya–Watson kernel regression** estimator. The bandwidth $h$ plays the role of $k$: small $h$ means only very close points matter. k-NN is the variant with an *adaptive* bandwidth, wide where data is sparse and narrow where it's dense, because "the $k$ nearest" covers more space where there are fewer points.

The [RBF SVM](/docs/machine-learning/support-vector-machines#the-kernel-trick) predicts with the same ingredients, a weighted sum of Gaussian bumps centred on training points, but learns the weights $\alpha_i$ by maximizing the margin and keeps only the support vectors. k-NN uses all the points with fixed weights. The comparison shows what each buys: k-NN needs no training and adapts its smoothing to the local density; the SVM pays for training with a much smaller model and a boundary optimized for separation.

</GoDeeper>

## 7. Minimal Implementation

A vectorized k-NN classifier: all query-to-training distances from one matrix product, using $\lVert\mathbf{a} - \mathbf{b}\rVert^2 = \lVert\mathbf{a}\rVert^2 + \lVert\mathbf{b}\rVert^2 - 2\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}$, then the $k$ smallest per row with `argpartition` (linear time, no full sort). It runs on the wine dataset (178 wines, 13 chemical measurements, 3 grape cultivars), then checks scaling, the choice of $k$, and the curse of dimensionality:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.datasets import load_wine
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, StratifiedKFold
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler

def knn_predict(X_train, y_train, X_query, k):
    # squared distances from every query to every training point, without a Python loop
    d2 = (X_query**2).sum(1)[:, None] + (X_train**2).sum(1)[None, :] - 2 * X_query @ X_train.T
    nearest = np.argpartition(d2, k - 1, axis=1)[:, :k]      # indices of the k smallest, unordered
    votes = y_train[nearest]
    return np.array([np.bincount(v).argmax() for v in votes])

X, y = load_wine(return_X_y=True)                             # 178 wines, 13 chemical measurements, 3 cultivars
cv = StratifiedKFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
print("feature standard deviations: smallest", np.round(X.std(0).min(), 2), " largest", np.round(X.std(0).max(), 1))

# From scratch vs scikit-learn, with standardization fitted inside each fold
ours, theirs = [], []
for tr, te in cv.split(X, y):
    sc = StandardScaler().fit(X[tr])
    ours.append(np.mean(knn_predict(sc.transform(X[tr]), y[tr], sc.transform(X[te]), 5) == y[te]))
    m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(5)).fit(X[tr], y[tr])
    theirs.append(m.score(X[te], y[te]))
print(f"k=5, standardized: ours {np.mean(ours):.3f}, scikit-learn {np.mean(theirs):.3f}")
print(f"k=5, raw features: {cross_val_score(KNeighborsClassifier(5), X, y, cv=cv).mean():.3f}")

# Choosing k: training accuracy vs cross-validated accuracy
for k in [1, 3, 5, 15, 51, 101]:
    m = make_pipeline(StandardScaler(), KNeighborsClassifier(k))
    print(f"k={k:<4} training accuracy {m.fit(X, y).score(X, y):.3f}   cross-validated {cross_val_score(m, X, y, cv=cv).mean():.3f}")

# The curse of dimensionality: how distances concentrate as dimensions grow
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for d in [2, 10, 100, 1000]:
    P = rng.random((1000, d)); q = rng.random(d)
    dist = np.sqrt(((P - q) ** 2).sum(1))
    print(f"d={d:<5} nearest/farthest distance ratio {dist.min() / dist.max():.2f}   "
          f"edge of a cube holding 1% of the data {0.01 ** (1 / d):.2f}")
```

- **The implementation matches scikit-learn** (0.961 both).
- **Scaling is the single biggest decision (Section 4)**: 0.663 on raw features, 0.961 standardized, with nothing else changed.
- **$k$ behaves as Section 3 predicts.** Training accuracy is a perfect 1.000 at $k = 1$ and means nothing. Cross-validated accuracy peaks around $k = 15$ and collapses at $k = 101$, where each fold's 142 training wines are mostly being averaged together.
- **The curse is real (Section 5).** By 100 dimensions, the nearest of 1,000 random points is 72% as far away as the farthest, and a neighbourhood holding 1% of the data spans 95% of every axis.

## 8. When *Not* to Use k-NN

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Many raw features (hundreds or more) | Reduce dimensions first ([PCA](/docs/machine-learning/pca-svd)), use a learned embedding, or use a model that ignores irrelevant features (trees, L1-regularized linear models) |
| Large training set and tight prediction latency or memory | A parametric model, or an [approximate index](/docs/databases/vector/overview) over embeddings |
| Features on incomparable scales or of mixed types you can't sensibly combine into one distance | Tree ensembles ([XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost](/docs/machine-learning/xgboost-lightgbm-catboost)) |
| Many irrelevant features | Feature selection or metric learning first; every irrelevant feature adds noise to every distance |
| Predictions outside the training range | Any model that extrapolates; k-NN returns an average of the edge points |
| You need a compact model or an explanation in terms of features | Linear models or a small tree |

k-NN is at its best as a quick, assumption-free baseline on small, low-dimensional data, and as the retrieval step on top of good embeddings, where the hard part of "similar" has already been learned.

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| Algorithm | Find the $k$ nearest training points; vote (classification) or average (regression) |
| Lazy | No training; $O(nd)$ per brute-force query; the whole training set is the model |
| Cover and Hart | As $n \to \infty$, 1-NN error is at most $2R^*(1-R^*)$, under twice the Bayes error |
| Consistency | With $k \to \infty$ and $k/n \to 0$, error converges to the Bayes error |
| Choosing $k$ | Bias grows with $k$, variance falls like $\sigma^2/k$; pick by cross-validation |
| $k = 1$ | 100% training accuracy, always; it means nothing |
| Distance | Euclidean by default; Manhattan for robustness; cosine for embeddings |
| Scaling | Decides everything: 0.663 raw vs 0.961 standardized on the wine data |
| Curse | A 1% neighbourhood spans $0.01^{1/d}$ of each axis: 0.63 at $d = 10$; distances concentrate |
| Speed | k-d and ball trees in low dimensions; HNSW and other ANN indexes for embeddings |
