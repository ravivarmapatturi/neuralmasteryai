# Time Series Forecasting: ARIMA, SARIMA & Prophet, In Full Depth

Every method in the [supervised learning](/docs/machine-learning/supervised-learning) pages assumes the training examples are independent: shuffle the rows and nothing changes. A time series breaks that on purpose. The order is the information: this month's value is related to last month's and to the same month last year, and the goal is to predict values that come *after* everything seen so far. This page builds forecasting from that fact: decomposing a series into trend and seasonality, making it stationary by differencing, the autoregressive and moving-average models and their seasonal extension, Prophet's alternative, using ordinary machine-learning models on lagged values, and how to evaluate a forecast without leaking the future into it. Everything runs on monthly carbon dioxide measurements from Mauna Loa, 1958 to 2001.

<ELI5>

To guess tomorrow's temperature, the most useful facts are today's temperature (tomorrow is usually similar), the season (July is warmer than January), and whether this year has been running warm. A forecaster combines those three things: the recent past, the regular yearly pattern, and the long-term direction.

Time series models make that combination exact. And there's one rule you can't break: when you test a forecaster, you may only use what was known at the time. Testing a weather forecaster with tomorrow's newspaper in their pocket proves nothing. Everything below is those ideas made exact.

</ELI5>

## 1. What Changes When Order Matters

Write the series as $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_T$, one value per time step. Three things are different from the independent-examples setting:

- **The examples are dependent.** $y_t$ is correlated with $y_{t-1}$, $y_{t-12}$ and so on. That correlation is what makes forecasting possible, and it's what models exploit.
- **The target is in the future.** A forecast for $y_{T+h}$, $h$ steps ahead, can use only $y_1, \dots, y_T$ and whatever other inputs will be known at time $T$.
- **The distribution can drift.** The mean and spread may change over time, so the past is only a guide to the future to the extent the process stays similar.

The dependence is measured by the **autocorrelation function** (ACF): the correlation between the series and itself shifted by $k$ steps, $\rho_k = \operatorname{corr}(y_t, y_{t-k})$, for each lag $k$. A strongly trending series has an ACF that decays very slowly; a monthly series with a yearly pattern has spikes at lags 12, 24 and so on. The **partial autocorrelation** (PACF) at lag $k$ is the correlation that remains after accounting for lags 1 to $k-1$, which isolates the direct effect of lag $k$.

## 2. Trend, Seasonality and Noise

The first look at any series is a **decomposition** into three parts:

$$
y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t
$$

the **trend** $T_t$ (the slow, long-run level), the **seasonal** component $S_t$ (a pattern repeating with a fixed period, such as 12 months or 7 days), and the **remainder** $R_t$. When the seasonal swing grows with the level (sales whose December peak grows as the business grows), the decomposition is multiplicative, which is the same as additive on $\log y_t$. **STL** (seasonal-trend decomposition using LOESS; Cleveland et al., 1990) estimates the parts with repeated local smoothing and is robust to outliers:

<ThemedImage alt="STL decomposition of monthly CO2: a rising observed series, a smooth upward trend, a regular yearly seasonal wave of about plus or minus 3 ppm, and a small residual" sources={{light: decompLight, dark: decompDark}} />

For the CO2 data the picture is unusually clean: a trend rising from 316 to 371 parts per million, accelerating slightly; a yearly cycle of about ±3 ppm (plants in the Northern Hemisphere absorb carbon in summer and release it in winter); and a small remainder. Classical forecasting models are, largely, ways of modelling these three parts, which they do in two different styles: ARIMA by transforming the trend and seasonality away and modelling what's left (Sections 3 to 5), Prophet by modelling each part explicitly (Section 6).

## 3. Stationarity and Differencing

Most classical models assume the series is **stationary**: its mean, variance and autocorrelations don't change over time. A trending series isn't: its mean keeps rising. The standard fix is **differencing**, replacing the series by its changes,

$$
\Delta y_t = y_t - y_{t-1}
$$

A linear trend becomes a constant; a quadratic trend becomes linear and needs a second difference. Seasonality is removed by a **seasonal difference** at the period $s$, $y_t - y_{t-s}$: this January minus last January. Differencing loses nothing essential: the original series is recovered by adding the changes back up, which is what the "I" (integrated) in ARIMA refers to.

Whether a series needs differencing can be tested. The **augmented Dickey–Fuller test** has the null hypothesis that the series has a unit root (it's a random-walk-like, non-stationary process). In Section 9, the raw CO2 series gives $p = 0.999$, consistent with non-stationarity; after one difference, $p \lt  0.001$. Watch differencing flatten a trend:

## 4. Autoregression and Moving Averages

**Autoregressive model, AR($p$).** Predict the (stationary) series from its own last $p$ values:

$$
y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \phi_2 y_{t-2} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \epsilon_t
$$

with $\epsilon_t$ white noise. This is [linear regression](/docs/machine-learning/linear-regression) whose features are lagged copies of the target, and it can be fitted exactly that way, by least squares on a matrix of lags. Section 9 does so for an AR(3) on the CO2 changes and gets coefficients identical to statsmodels' to four decimals. An AR(1) with $\lvert\phi_1\rvert \lt  1$ is stationary and reverts to its mean; with $\phi_1 = 1$ it's a random walk, the non-stationary case differencing removes.

**Moving-average model, MA($q$).** Predict from the last $q$ *forecast errors* (shocks) rather than the last values:

$$
y_t = c + \epsilon_t + \theta_1\epsilon_{t-1} + \dots + \theta_q\epsilon_{t-q}
$$

A shock affects the next $q$ values and then disappears entirely, which suits effects that linger briefly. The errors aren't observed directly, so MA models are fitted by maximum likelihood, not least squares.

**ARIMA($p, d, q$)** (Box and Jenkins, 1970) combines them: difference $d$ times, then fit AR($p$) and MA($q$) terms together on the result:

$$
\Delta^d y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i\,\Delta^d y_{t-i} + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j\,\epsilon_{t-j} + \epsilon_t
$$

The ACF and PACF guide the orders: a pure AR($p$) has a PACF that cuts off after lag $p$ and an ACF that decays; a pure MA($q$) has the reverse. In practice the orders are usually chosen by searching a small range and comparing AIC or BIC, or forecast error on held-out time periods (Hyndman and Khandakar's automatic ARIMA does this).

## 5. Seasonality: SARIMA

ARIMA has no way to say "this month resembles the same month last year" except through very long lags. **Seasonal ARIMA**, written ARIMA$(p,d,q)(P,D,Q)_s$, adds a second set of the same three ideas operating at multiples of the season length $s$: $D$ seasonal differences, $P$ seasonal autoregressive terms (on $y_{t-s}, y_{t-2s}, \dots$) and $Q$ seasonal moving-average terms. The two parts multiply, so the model captures short-term dependence and the yearly pattern together.

A classic choice for monthly data with trend and seasonality is ARIMA$(1,1,1)(0,1,1)_{12}$ or the closely related "airline model": one ordinary and one seasonal difference, then short-term terms and a seasonal MA term. In Section 9 it's the most accurate model, with forecast errors less than half those of the seasonal-naive baseline. Non-seasonal ARIMA$(1,1,1)$ on the same data does worse than that baseline, because it has no way to represent the yearly cycle.

## 6. Prophet: Modelling the Parts Directly

Prophet (Taylor and Letham, 2018), developed at Meta, takes the decomposition view instead of the ARIMA view. It fits a regression on functions of time:

$$
y(t) = g(t) + s(t) + h(t) + \epsilon_t
$$

- $g(t)$, the **trend**, is piecewise linear (or logistic, for growth that saturates), with many potential **changepoints** where the slope may change, regularized so that only a few are used.
- $s(t)$, **seasonality**, is a Fourier series, $\sum_{n=1}^{N}\big(a_n\cos\tfrac{2\pi nt}{P} + b_n\sin\tfrac{2\pi nt}{P}\big)$, for each period $P$ (weekly, yearly), so several seasonal cycles can be combined smoothly.
- $h(t)$ adds effects for **holidays** and known events, with dates supplied by the user.

Because it doesn't depend on the order of observations the way ARIMA does, Prophet handles missing values and irregular spacing easily, and its components can be plotted and checked by people who know the business. It was designed for daily business series with strong human-calendar effects. Its limits are the flip side: with no autoregressive terms, it ignores short-term dependence in the remainder, and independent evaluations have often found it less accurate than well-chosen ARIMA, exponential smoothing or simple baselines on general benchmark collections. (Prophet isn't installed in this page's environment, so it isn't run in Section 9.)

**Exponential smoothing** (ETS) deserves a mention alongside both: forecasts are weighted averages of past observations with weights decaying exponentially into the past, with separate smoothed components for level, trend and season (Holt–Winters). ETS models are fast, strong on many business series, and a standard benchmark.

## 7. Machine Learning on Lag Features

Any regression model can forecast if the problem is rewritten as supervised learning: for each time $t$, the features are values known at $t$ (lags $y_{t-1}, y_{t-2}, y_{t-12}, \dots$, rolling means, calendar features, known future inputs such as planned promotions) and the target is $y_{t+h}$. Gradient-boosted trees are the usual choice; they handle many related series, nonlinear effects and covariates well, and models of this kind won the M5 forecasting competition (Makridakis et al., 2022).

Two details decide whether it works:

- **Model what trees can represent.** Trees can't extrapolate: they predict values within the range they've seen. A trending level will eventually exceed every training value. Predict the *change* (or the ratio to a recent level) instead, then add it back. In Section 9 the boosting model predicts month-to-month changes from six lags and is almost exactly as accurate as SARIMA.
- **Multi-step forecasts.** To forecast 12 months ahead, either train one model per horizon (**direct**), or forecast one step, feed the prediction back in as a lag and repeat (**recursive**, which Section 9 uses). Recursive forecasts compound their own errors; direct ones need more models.

For many related series (every product in every store), **global models** trained across all series at once usually beat one model per series, because they share what's common. Deep learning versions include DeepAR, N-BEATS and the Temporal Fusion Transformer (Lim et al., 2021), which combines recurrent layers, attention over long histories, learned feature selection and quantile outputs; see [Sequence Models](/docs/deep-learning/sequence-models) and [Attention & Transformers](/docs/deep-learning/attention-transformers). Since 2023, **pretrained time-series foundation models** such as Chronos and TimesFM produce zero-shot forecasts for series they weren't trained on; on standard benchmarks they're competitive with tuned classical models, and they're worth trying as a baseline, though a seasonal-naive and a well-specified ARIMA or ETS remain essential comparisons.

## 8. Evaluating a Forecast Honestly

**Split by time, never at random.** A random split lets a model see the future of its test points. Section 9 measures how misleading that can be: the same gradient-boosted model of CO2 levels from the month index scores 0.44 ppm with shuffled folds and 6.56 ppm with time-ordered folds. When the model predicts stationary changes instead, the two kinds of fold agree (0.291 and 0.295), because the changes don't trend. Shuffled folds can look fine or catastrophically optimistic depending on the setup, so the only safe rule is never to use them for forecasting. This is the time-series form of [data leakage](/docs/machine-learning/ml-workflow-fundamentals#data-leakage), and it also applies to features: any feature must be computable from data available at forecast time.

**Rolling-origin evaluation** (time-series cross-validation): choose several forecast origins; at each, train on everything before it and forecast the next $h$ steps; average the errors. That tests the model as it would actually be used and shows how stable it is. scikit-learn's `TimeSeriesSplit` implements the expanding-window version.

**Scale-free error: MASE.** Comparing errors across series of different scales needs a relative measure. The **mean absolute scaled error** (Hyndman and Koehler, 2006) divides the forecast's mean absolute error by the in-sample error of the seasonal-naive forecast ("same as last season"):

$$
\text{MASE} = \frac{\frac{1}{h}\sum_{j=1}^{h}\lvert y_{T+j} - \hat{y}_{T+j}\rvert}{\frac{1}{T-s}\sum_{t=s+1}^{T}\lvert y_t - y_{t-s}\rvert}
$$

Below 1 means the forecast beats what seasonal-naive achieved in-sample. Unlike percentage errors, it's defined when values are zero and doesn't favour under-forecasting. On a trending series like CO2, the seasonal-naive forecast's *out-of-sample* MASE is above 1 (1.355 in Section 9), because over a 12-month horizon it misses the trend for the whole year ahead.

**Always report a baseline.** Naive (last value), seasonal naive and drift forecasts are surprisingly hard to beat, and a model that can't beat them isn't worth deploying.

**Forecast intervals.** A point forecast hides how uncertain it is, and uncertainty grows with the horizon, because errors accumulate. ARIMA models give intervals from their error model; ML models need quantile losses or conformal methods (see [Uncertainty Estimation](/docs/machine-learning/uncertainty-estimation-and-conformal-prediction), with the caveat that standard conformal prediction assumes exchangeability, which time series violate). For CO2, a SARIMA model fitted to data through 1995 and forecasting 1996 to 2001 gives a 90% interval that widens with the horizon and contains 97% of the 72 actual months:

<ThemedImage alt="CO2 from 1988 to 2001: training data to 1995, then a SARIMA forecast for 1996 to 2001 following the actual values closely, inside a shaded 90% interval that widens over time" sources={{light: forecastLight, dark: forecastDark}} />

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why forecast errors grow with the horizon">

Take the simplest non-stationary model, a random walk, $y_t = y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$. The best forecast of $y_{T+h}$ is $y_T$, and the error is the sum of the $h$ future shocks: $y_{T+h} - y_T = \epsilon_{T+1} + \dots + \epsilon_{T+h}$. Independent shocks' variances add, so the error variance is $h\sigma^2$ and the interval width grows like $\sqrt{h}$.

For a stationary AR(1), $y_t = \phi y_{t-1} + \epsilon_t$ with $\lvert\phi\rvert \lt  1$, the $h$-step forecast is $\phi^h y_T$, which decays toward the mean, and the error variance is $\sigma^2(1 + \phi^2 + \dots + \phi^{2(h-1)})$, which levels off at the series' unconditional variance $\sigma^2/(1-\phi^2)$. Far ahead, a stationary model's best forecast is just the mean, with the uncertainty of the series itself. Differenced (integrated) models behave like the random walk, so their intervals keep widening, which is visible in the CO2 forecast above. Model-based intervals assume the model is right; when the process itself changes, real errors exceed them, so check interval coverage on past forecasts.

</GoDeeper>

## 9. Minimal Implementation

Monthly CO2: a stationarity test before and after differencing; an AR(3) fitted by hand as regression on lags and checked against statsmodels; four forecasters (seasonal naive, ARIMA, SARIMA, and gradient boosting on lagged changes) compared over 12-month horizons from five rolling origins, scored by MASE; and the leakage comparison of shuffled and time-ordered folds:

```python
import warnings
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.model_selection import KFold, TimeSeriesSplit, cross_val_score
from statsmodels.datasets import co2
from statsmodels.tsa.ar_model import AutoReg
from statsmodels.tsa.statespace.sarimax import SARIMAX
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")

# Monthly mean CO2 at Mauna Loa, 1958-2001 (weekly data averaged by month, gaps filled)
y = co2.load_pandas().data["co2"].resample("MS").mean().ffill().to_numpy()
print(f"{len(y)} months, from {y[0]:.1f} to {y[-1]:.1f} ppm")

# Stationarity: Augmented Dickey-Fuller test before and after differencing
for name, s in [("raw series", y), ("first difference", np.diff(y)), ("plus seasonal difference (lag 12)", np.diff(y)[12:] - np.diff(y)[:-12])]:
    print(f"ADF p-value, {name:34s} {adfuller(s)[1]:.3f}")

# AR(p) is linear regression on lagged values: least squares by hand vs statsmodels
p = 3
d = np.diff(y)
Xlag = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(d) - p)] + [d[p - j - 1:len(d) - j - 1] for j in range(p)])
coef = np.linalg.lstsq(Xlag, d[p:], rcond=None)[0]
print("AR(3) on differences, least squares:", np.round(coef, 4))
print("AR(3) on differences, statsmodels:  ", np.round(AutoReg(d, lags=p).fit().params, 4))

# Rolling-origin evaluation: forecast the next 12 months from 5 different starting points
def mase_scale(train):                                     # error of the seasonal-naive forecast in-sample
    return np.mean(np.abs(train[12:] - train[:-12]))
def lag_features(series, lags=(1, 2, 3, 12, 13, 24)):
    m = max(lags)
    X = np.column_stack([series[m - l:len(series) - l] for l in lags])
    return X, series[m:]
results = {k: [] for k in ["seasonal naive", "ARIMA(1,1,1)", "SARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1,12)", "boosting on lags (recursive)"]}
for origin in range(len(y) - 5 * 24, len(y) - 12 + 1, 24):
    train, test = y[:origin], y[origin:origin + 12]
    scale = mase_scale(train)
    fc = {"seasonal naive": train[-12:],
          "ARIMA(1,1,1)": SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1), trend="c").fit(disp=False).forecast(12),
          "SARIMA(1,1,1)(0,1,1,12)": SARIMAX(train, order=(1, 1, 1), seasonal_order=(0, 1, 1, 12)).fit(disp=False).forecast(12)}
    Xtr, ytr = lag_features(np.diff(train))                # model month-to-month changes
    gb = HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0).fit(Xtr, ytr)
    hist = list(np.diff(train)); level = train[-1]; preds = []
    for _ in range(12):                                    # feed each forecast back in as the next lag
        x = np.array([[hist[-l] for l in (1, 2, 3, 12, 13, 24)]])
        step = gb.predict(x)[0]; hist.append(step); level += step; preds.append(level)
    fc["boosting on lags (recursive)"] = np.array(preds)
    for k, f in fc.items():
        results[k].append(np.mean(np.abs(test - f)) / scale)
print("12-month forecasts from 5 origins, MASE (below 1 beats the seasonal-naive forecast):")
for k, v in results.items():
    print(f"  {k:30s} mean {np.mean(v):.3f}   per origin {np.round(v, 2)}")

# Leakage: the same model scored with shuffled folds and with time-ordered folds
t_idx = np.arange(len(y))
cases = {"level from (time index, month)": (np.column_stack([t_idx, t_idx % 12]), y),
         "monthly change from its lags": lag_features(np.diff(y))}
for case, (X, target) in cases.items():
    for name, cv in [("shuffled 5-fold", KFold(5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)), ("time-ordered 5-fold", TimeSeriesSplit(5))]:
        mae = -cross_val_score(HistGradientBoostingRegressor(random_state=0), X, target, cv=cv, scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error").mean()
        print(f"{case:32s} {name:20s} MAE {mae:.3f}")
```

- **Differencing makes it stationary (Section 3).** The Dickey–Fuller $p$-value goes from 0.999 to below 0.001.
- **AR is regression on lags (Section 4).** Least squares on a lag matrix reproduces statsmodels' AR(3) exactly.
- **Seasonality must be modelled (Section 5).** SARIMA's MASE is 0.42; ARIMA without a seasonal part, 1.50, worse on average than the seasonal-naive baseline. The per-origin values show SARIMA beating both at every one of the five origins.
- **ML on lags works when it predicts changes (Section 7).** Boosting on lagged changes, forecasting recursively, reaches 0.43, essentially tied with SARIMA.
- **Shuffled folds can be wildly optimistic (Section 8)**: 0.44 against 6.56 ppm for a model of levels, while a model of stationary changes barely notices the difference.

## 10. When *Not* to Use These Methods

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Very short history (under two seasonal cycles) | Seasonal naive, simple exponential smoothing, or pooling with related series |
| Many related series (products, stores, sensors) | A global model across all series: gradient boosting on lag features, DeepAR or TFT |
| The future depends mostly on known drivers (prices, promotions, weather) | Regression on those drivers with time-aware validation, not a pure univariate model |
| Structural breaks (a pandemic, a policy change) | Re-estimate on post-break data, add intervention variables, widen intervals; history may not apply |
| Intermittent demand (many zeros) | Croston-type methods or count models |
| You need a quick, strong baseline across thousands of series | ETS or automatic ARIMA, or a pretrained forecasting model, compared against seasonal naive |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| Order matters | Examples are dependent and the target is in the future |
| ACF / PACF | Correlation with past lags; PACF removes shorter lags' effects |
| Decomposition | Trend + seasonal + remainder (multiplicative = additive on logs) |
| Stationarity | Constant mean, variance and autocorrelation; difference to get there |
| AR($p$) | Regression on the last $p$ values |
| MA($q$) | Regression on the last $q$ shocks |
| ARIMA | Difference $d$ times, then AR and MA terms |
| SARIMA | Adds seasonal differencing and seasonal AR/MA terms at lag $s$ |
| Prophet | Piecewise trend + Fourier seasonality + holidays, fitted as a regression |
| ML on lags | Predict changes, not trending levels; direct or recursive multi-step |
| Evaluation | Time-ordered splits, rolling origins, MASE, and always a naive baseline |
| Intervals | Widen with the horizon; check their coverage on past forecasts |
