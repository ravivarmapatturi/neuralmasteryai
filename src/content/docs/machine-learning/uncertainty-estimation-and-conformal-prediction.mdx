# Uncertainty Estimation & Conformal Prediction

[Model Evaluation & Metrics](/docs/machine-learning/model-evaluation-metrics) covered calibration: whether a model's probabilities are right *on average*. This page asks how sure we can be about *one particular* prediction, and how to turn a point prediction into a range that contains the true value with a stated probability. It separates the two kinds of uncertainty, covers quantile regression and ensembles (useful but without guarantees), then derives **conformal prediction**, a wrapper that turns any model into one whose intervals are guaranteed to cover the truth at the promised rate, proves the guarantee, and shows the version that adapts its width to how noisy each input is. The experiments measure coverage overall and in the noisy and quiet regions separately, and show what happens when the guarantee's one assumption is broken.

<ELI5>

A weather forecaster who says "about 20 degrees tomorrow" is giving a point prediction. A more useful forecast is "between 17 and 23 degrees, and on nine days in ten my ranges like this one are right". That second claim can be checked, and a forecaster can earn it.

Conformal prediction is a way to earn it for any model. Before using the model, test it on examples it hasn't seen and look at how far off it was. If 90% of its misses were within 3 degrees, put a 3-degree margin on every future forecast. That simple rule comes with a real guarantee, as long as tomorrow's examples are like the ones it was tested on. Everything below is that idea made exact, and improved so the margin can be narrow on calm days and wide on stormy ones.

</ELI5>

## 1. Two Kinds of Uncertainty

A prediction can be uncertain for two different reasons, and they behave differently:

- **Aleatoric uncertainty** is randomness in the target itself. Two houses with identical features sell for different prices; a customer with a given history may or may not churn. More data doesn't reduce it; it's a property of the problem. It can also vary with the input: in the experiments below, the noise grows from small at $x = 0$ to large at $x = 10$.
- **Epistemic uncertainty** is the model's own ignorance: too little data near this input, or an input unlike anything in training. More data does reduce it.

A good interval reflects both. Quantile regression (Section 2) learns aleatoric spread directly; ensembles (Section 2) mostly measure epistemic disagreement; and conformal prediction (Sections 3 to 5) calibrates whatever interval method you use so that its total coverage is right.

## 2. Quantile Regression and Ensembles

**Quantile regression.** The [Supervised Learning page](/docs/machine-learning/supervised-learning) showed that squared error makes a model predict the conditional mean and absolute error the conditional median. Generalize the absolute error by weighting under- and over-predictions differently. The **pinball loss** for quantile level $\tau$ is

$$
L_\tau(y, \hat{y}) = \begin{cases} \tau\,(y - \hat{y}) & y \ge \hat{y} \\ (1 - \tau)\,(\hat{y} - y) & y \lt  \hat{y} \end{cases}
$$

Its expected value is minimized where $\tau P(y \gt  \hat{y}) = (1-\tau) P(y \lt  \hat{y})$, which is where $P(y \lt  \hat{y}) = \tau$: at the conditional $\tau$-**quantile**. Training one model at $\tau = 0.05$ and another at $\tau = 0.95$ gives a 90% interval whose width follows the noise, narrow where the data is tight and wide where it's spread out. Linear models, gradient boosting (`loss="quantile"`) and neural networks can all be trained this way. The weakness is that the quantiles are estimates: with limited data or a misspecified model, a nominal 90% interval might cover 85% of cases, and nothing tells you so. In Section 7 the uncalibrated boosting quantiles cover 89.0% overall and only 87.1% in the low-noise region.

**Ensembles.** Train several models on different bootstrap samples, seeds or architectures, and treat their disagreement as uncertainty: where they agree, the data pinned the answer down; where they disagree, it didn't. Deep ensembles (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017) are a strong baseline for neural networks, and Monte Carlo dropout is a cheap approximation. Disagreement is a good signal of epistemic uncertainty and of inputs unlike the training data, but it isn't calibrated: "the models disagree by 2 units" doesn't translate into "90% of outcomes fall within 2 units".

Both are useful, and neither comes with a guarantee. The next section adds one.

## 3. Split Conformal Prediction

Conformal prediction (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer, 2005) wraps any model. The simplest version, **split conformal**, needs a set of labelled **calibration** examples that the model never trained on:

1. Fit any model $\hat{f}$ on training data.
2. Choose a **score function** $s(x, y)$ that's large when $y$ disagrees with the model. For regression the simplest is the absolute residual, $s(x, y) = \lvert y - \hat{f}(x)\rvert$.
3. Compute the scores $s_1, \dots, s_n$ on the $n$ calibration examples, and let $\hat{q}$ be the $k$-th smallest, with

$$
k = \big\lceil (n + 1)(1 - \alpha)\big\rceil
$$

4. For a new input, the prediction set is every $y$ whose score is at most $\hat{q}$: for the absolute residual, the interval $[\hat{f}(x) - \hat{q},\; \hat{f}(x) + \hat{q}]$.

**The guarantee.** If the calibration examples and the test example are **exchangeable** (any ordering of them is equally likely; i.i.d. data is the standard case), then

$$
P\big(Y_{\text{test}} \in C(X_{\text{test}})\big) \ge 1 - \alpha
$$

**Proof.** Consider the $n + 1$ scores together: the $n$ calibration scores and the test score $s_{n+1}$. By exchangeability, every ordering of these values is equally likely, so the rank of $s_{n+1}$ among them is uniformly distributed over $1, \dots, n + 1$ (breaking ties at random). The test point is outside the interval exactly when $s_{n+1} \gt  \hat{q}$, the $k$-th smallest calibration score, which happens only if $s_{n+1}$'s rank is $k + 1$ or higher: $n + 1 - k$ of the $n + 1$ equally likely positions. So

$$
P(s_{n+1} \gt  \hat{q}) \le \frac{n + 1 - k}{n + 1} \le \frac{n + 1 - (n+1)(1-\alpha)}{n + 1} = \alpha
$$

using $k \ge (n+1)(1-\alpha)$. That's the whole argument. It never mentions the model, the score function, or the distribution of the data. A terrible model still gets valid coverage; its intervals are just wide. The "$+1$" in $k$ is the finite-sample correction that makes the guarantee exact for any $n$, rather than approximately true for large $n$. (When $k \gt  n$, which happens for very small $n$ or $\alpha$, the interval must be infinite.)

**What the guarantee says, precisely.** The probability is over the random draw of the calibration set *and* the test point. For one fixed calibration set, the realized coverage on future data is a random quantity centred just above $1 - \alpha$, with spread of about $\sqrt{\alpha(1-\alpha)/n}$. In Section 7, repeating calibration 300 times with $n = 1{,}000$ gives a mean coverage of exactly 0.900, with the middle 90% of runs between 0.884 and 0.917. Larger calibration sets narrow that spread.

## 4. Adaptive Intervals: Conformalized Quantile Regression

Split conformal with absolute residuals adds the same $\hat{q}$ everywhere, so its intervals have **constant width**. That satisfies the guarantee, which is about coverage averaged over all inputs (**marginal coverage**), but it can be badly unbalanced: too wide where the data is quiet and too narrow where it's noisy. In Section 7, with noise that grows with $x$, the constant-width interval covers 100% of points with $x \lt  3$ and only 79.6% with $x \gt  7$, averaging out to 91.7%.

**Conformalized quantile regression** (CQR; Romano, Patterson and Candès, 2019) combines the adaptive width of quantile regression with the guarantee of conformal prediction. Fit lower and upper quantile models $\hat{q}_{\text{lo}}(x)$ and $\hat{q}_{\text{hi}}(x)$. Use the score

$$
s(x, y) = \max\big(\hat{q}_{\text{lo}}(x) - y,\;\; y - \hat{q}_{\text{hi}}(x)\big)
$$

which is negative when $y$ is inside the quantile band and measures how far outside it is otherwise. Calibrate this score exactly as before, and widen (or narrow, if $\hat{q}$ is negative) the band by $\hat{q}$: $C(x) = [\hat{q}_{\text{lo}}(x) - \hat{q},\; \hat{q}_{\text{hi}}(x) + \hat{q}]$. The same proof applies, so marginal coverage is guaranteed; and because the band follows the quantile models, coverage is much more even across inputs. In Section 7, CQR covers 90.4% overall, 90.1% in the quiet region and 91.1% in the noisy one, with intervals averaging 2.8 wide where the noise is small and 12.4 where it's large.

**Conditional coverage can't be guaranteed in general.** The ideal would be 90% coverage for every individual input, not just on average. Vovk (2012) and Barber et al. (2021) showed that no method can guarantee that without assumptions, beyond trivial infinite intervals. What can be done is coverage within chosen groups (**Mondrian conformal prediction**: calibrate separately per group, such as per region or demographic), and score functions like CQR's that make conditional coverage approximately right in practice.

## 5. Classification, Shift, and Other Variants

**Classification.** For a classifier with predicted probabilities $\hat{p}(y \mid x)$, use the score $s(x, y) = 1 - \hat{p}(y \mid x)$, one minus the probability given to the true class. Calibrate $\hat{q}$ as before; the prediction **set** for a new input is every class with $1 - \hat{p}(y \mid x) \le \hat{q}$. Confident inputs get a single class; ambiguous ones get two or three; and the set contains the true class at least $1 - \alpha$ of the time. The set size is itself a useful uncertainty signal: route inputs with large sets to a person. Adaptive prediction sets (APS) and their regularized version (RAPS; Angelopoulos et al., 2021) give smaller sets with more even coverage across easy and hard inputs.

**When exchangeability fails.** The guarantee's single assumption is exchangeability, and it's exactly what deployment tends to break: time series (the future isn't exchangeable with the past), distribution shift, feedback loops. Section 7 shows the cost: test inputs from a range the calibration data never covered drop coverage from 0.90 to 0.68, silently. Remedies include **weighted conformal prediction** (Tibshirani et al., 2019), which reweights calibration scores by an estimated likelihood ratio between test and calibration inputs when the shift is in $x$ only, and **adaptive conformal inference** (Gibbs and Candès, 2021), which adjusts $\alpha$ online from observed misses so long-run coverage stays on target even under drift. Monitoring realized coverage in production is the simplest safeguard.

**Using all the data.** Split conformal sets aside a calibration set, which costs training data. **Cross-conformal** and **jackknife+** (Barber et al., 2021) reuse every point for both training and calibration through cross-validation, with slightly weaker but still finite-sample guarantees.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: conformal prediction for language models">

The same recipe works for systems whose internals are inaccessible, which has made it popular for LLMs. Some examples of the score functions used:

- **Multiple-choice and classification tasks**: one minus the model's probability (or a sampled frequency) for each option, giving answer sets with guaranteed coverage of the right answer.
- **Open-ended generation**: sample several answers, score each by how often semantically equivalent answers recur or by a self-evaluation score, and calibrate a threshold so that the set of accepted answers contains a correct one at the target rate; Quach et al. (2024) develop this as conformal language modeling.
- **Abstention**: calibrate a confidence threshold so that, among the questions the model answers, the error rate is below a target, and route the rest to a human.

The guarantee is exactly as strong, and as weak, as the exchangeability assumption: calibration questions must be representative of the questions the system will see.

</GoDeeper>

## 6. Choosing a Method

| Need | Method |
|---|---|
| A quick interval with a guarantee for any model | Split conformal with absolute residuals |
| Intervals that adapt to input-dependent noise | Conformalized quantile regression |
| Uncertainty about inputs unlike the training data | Ensembles or other epistemic signals, used inside a conformal score |
| Classification with guaranteed coverage | Conformal prediction sets (APS or RAPS) |
| Guarantees per group (region, segment) | Mondrian (group-wise) calibration |
| Drifting data, time series | Adaptive conformal inference, plus coverage monitoring |
| Can't spare a calibration set | Jackknife+ or cross-conformal |

## 7. Minimal Implementation

Data with noise that grows with $x$ (standard deviation 0.2 at $x = 0$, 4.2 at $x = 10$). Five interval methods at a 90% target, each scored on 20,000 fresh test points for coverage and width overall and in the quiet ($x \lt  3$) and noisy ($x \gt  7$) regions; then the distribution-shift failure, and coverage across 300 independent calibration sets:

```python
import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import GradientBoostingRegressor
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def sample(n, lo=0, hi=10):
    x = rng.uniform(lo, hi, n)
    y = 2 * x + 5 + rng.normal(0, 0.2 + 0.4 * x, n)          # noise grows with x
    return x[:, None], y
X_tr, y_tr = sample(2000); X_cal, y_cal = sample(1000); X_te, y_te = sample(20000)
alpha = 0.1
k = int(np.ceil((len(y_cal) + 1) * (1 - alpha)))               # finite-sample-corrected rank

def report(name, lo, hi, x, y):
    cover = (y >= lo) & (y <= hi)
    low, high = x[:, 0] < 3, x[:, 0] > 7
    print(f"{name:34s} coverage {cover.mean():.3f}   (x<3: {cover[low].mean():.3f}, x>7: {cover[high].mean():.3f})   "
          f"mean width {np.mean(hi - lo):.2f}  (x<3: {np.mean((hi - lo)[low]):.2f}, x>7: {np.mean((hi - lo)[high]):.2f})")

# 1. Split conformal with absolute residuals: a constant-width band
lin = LinearRegression().fit(X_tr, y_tr)
q = np.sort(np.abs(y_cal - lin.predict(X_cal)))[k - 1]
p = lin.predict(X_te)
report("split conformal, |residual|", p - q, p + q, X_te, y_te)

# 2. Quantile regression alone: adaptive width, but no guarantee
ql = GradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", alpha=alpha / 2, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
qh = GradientBoostingRegressor(loss="quantile", alpha=1 - alpha / 2, random_state=0).fit(X_tr, y_tr)
report("quantile regression (uncalibrated)", ql.predict(X_te), qh.predict(X_te), X_te, y_te)

# 3. Conformalized quantile regression (CQR): calibrate the quantile band
s = np.maximum(ql.predict(X_cal) - y_cal, y_cal - qh.predict(X_cal))   # how far outside the band each point is
q_cqr = np.sort(s)[k - 1]
report("conformalized quantile regression", ql.predict(X_te) - q_cqr, qh.predict(X_te) + q_cqr, X_te, y_te)

# 4. A deliberately bad model still gets coverage, at the cost of width
bad = lambda X: np.full(len(X), y_tr.mean())                  # predicts a constant
q_bad = np.sort(np.abs(y_cal - bad(X_cal)))[k - 1]
report("split conformal, constant model", bad(X_te) - q_bad, bad(X_te) + q_bad, X_te, y_te)

# 5. Exchangeability broken: test inputs come from a range the calibration data never covered
X_sh, y_sh = sample(20000, lo=8, hi=14)
p = lin.predict(X_sh)
cover = (np.abs(y_sh - p) <= q).mean()
print(f"split conformal under distribution shift (x in 8..14): coverage {cover:.3f}, target {1 - alpha:.2f}")

# 6. The guarantee is an average over calibration sets: repeat the constant-model calibration 300 times
covs = []
for _ in range(300):
    Xc, yc = sample(1000)
    qb = np.sort(np.abs(yc - bad(Xc)))[k - 1]
    covs.append((np.abs(y_te - bad(X_te)) <= qb).mean())
print(f"constant model, 300 fresh calibration sets: mean coverage {np.mean(covs):.3f}, "
      f"middle 90% of runs {np.percentile(covs, 5):.3f} to {np.percentile(covs, 95):.3f}")
```

Output (scikit-learn 1.7):

```
split conformal, |residual|        coverage 0.917   (x<3: 1.000, x>7: 0.796)   mean width 9.00  (x<3: 9.00, x>7: 9.00)
quantile regression (uncalibrated) coverage 0.890   (x<3: 0.871, x>7: 0.906)   mean width 7.48  (x<3: 2.61, x>7: 12.16)
conformalized quantile regression  coverage 0.904   (x<3: 0.901, x>7: 0.911)   mean width 7.69  (x<3: 2.82, x>7: 12.37)
split conformal, constant model    coverage 0.892   (x<3: 0.907, x>7: 0.737)   mean width 18.98  (x<3: 18.98, x>7: 18.98)
split conformal under distribution shift (x in 8..14): coverage 0.680, target 0.90
constant model, 300 fresh calibration sets: mean coverage 0.900, middle 90% of runs 0.884 to 0.917
```

- **Marginal coverage is delivered (Section 3).** Every conformal method lands near 90% overall. The constant model's single run is at 89.2%, just under target, which is what the guarantee allows: across 300 calibration sets its average is exactly 0.900, with individual runs between 0.884 and 0.917.
- **Constant width is unbalanced (Section 4).** Split conformal with residuals covers every quiet point and only 79.6% of noisy ones.
- **Quantile regression adapts but isn't calibrated (Section 2)**: 89.0% overall, 87.1% in the quiet region.
- **CQR gets both (Section 4)**: 90.4% overall, 90.1% and 91.1% by region, with widths that follow the noise (2.8 to 12.4).
- **A useless model still gets coverage**, with intervals more than twice as wide (19.0 against 9.0).
- **Shift breaks the guarantee (Section 5)**: 68% coverage where 90% was promised.

The core of the method fits in a few lines; here's the split conformal recipe on a heteroskedastic line, running in your browser:

# Step 1: any fitted model, even a plain OLS line -- ordinary least squares.
A = np.vstack([x_train, np.ones_like(x_train)]).T
a, b = np.linalg.lstsq(A, y_train, rcond=None)[0]

def predict(x):
    return a * x + b

# Step 2 + 3: score function = absolute residual, q_hat = the
# finite-sample-corrected empirical quantile of calibration scores.
x_cal = rng.uniform(0, 10, n_cal)
y_cal = true_fn(x_cal) + rng.normal(0, 1 + 0.5 * x_cal, n_cal)
scores = np.abs(y_cal - predict(x_cal))

alpha = 0.1  # target: 90% coverage
k = int(np.ceil((n_cal + 1) * (1 - alpha)))
q_hat = np.sort(scores)[k - 1]

# Step 4: build the interval, then check coverage on FRESH test data
# the calibration step never touched.
x_test = rng.uniform(0, 10, n_test)
y_test = true_fn(x_test) + rng.normal(0, 1 + 0.5 * x_test, n_test)
covered = np.abs(y_test - predict(x_test)) <= q_hat
coverage = covered.mean()

print(f"Fitted line: y = {a:.3f}x + {b:.3f}  (true: y = 2x + 5)")
print(f"q_hat (half-width of every interval): {q_hat:.3f}")
print(f"Target coverage: {(1 - alpha) * 100:.0f}%")
print(f"Measured coverage on {n_test} fresh test points: {coverage * 100:.2f}%")

## 8. When *Not* to Rely on These Intervals

| Situation | What to do instead or as well |
|---|---|
| Test data differs from calibration data (new market, new time period) | Recalibrate on recent data, weighted or adaptive conformal, and monitor coverage |
| You need coverage for each individual, not on average | Group-wise calibration for the groups that matter; no method guarantees it per point |
| Very small calibration sets | Expect large run-to-run variation in realized coverage; collect more calibration data |
| The intervals are too wide to be useful | Improve the model or the score (CQR, better features); conformal guarantees validity, not usefulness |
| Time series forecasting | Use methods designed for dependence (adaptive conformal, block methods) |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| Aleatoric vs epistemic | Noise in the target vs the model's ignorance; only the second shrinks with data |
| Pinball loss | Minimized at the conditional $\tau$-quantile |
| Ensembles | Disagreement signals epistemic uncertainty; not calibrated |
| Split conformal | Scores on held-out data; $\hat{q}$ = the $\lceil(n+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$-th smallest |
| Guarantee | Coverage $\ge 1 - \alpha$ under exchangeability, for any model |
| Proof | The test score's rank among $n+1$ exchangeable scores is uniform |
| Marginal only | Coverage is on average over inputs and calibration draws |
| CQR | Calibrate a quantile band: adaptive width, same guarantee |
| Classification | Prediction sets from $1 - \hat{p}(y \mid x)$; set size signals difficulty |
| The assumption | Exchangeability; shift and time series break it |
