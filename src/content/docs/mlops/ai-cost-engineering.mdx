# AI Cost Engineering

[LLM Hosting & Serving Patterns](/docs/mlops/llm-hosting-and-serving-patterns) showed that a self-hosted GPU's cost per token depends mostly on how busy it is, and [Caching Strategies](/docs/mlops/caching-strategies) showed how to avoid repeating work. This page puts cost on its own footing. GPUs, accelerators and model APIs make ML systems far more expensive per request than ordinary web services, and the bill grows with usage in ways that surprise teams. It covers how to estimate training cost from first principles, how to compute the unit economics of an LLM feature and which levers move them most, why utilization dominates infrastructure cost, how routing between small and large models trades quality against cost (simulated as a cascade), and the quality-latency-cost trade-off every one of these decisions sits on.

<ELI5>

A delivery company can cut costs in a few ways. It can make sure vans don't drive around half empty. It can avoid sending a big lorry to deliver a small envelope. It can pack parcels more tightly. And it can stop delivering things nobody ordered. What it can't do is cut costs blindly, because a cheaper service that loses parcels isn't cheaper in the end.

Running AI systems is the same: keep the expensive hardware busy, send easy work to cheap models, send less text through the model, and don't recompute what you already know, all while checking that the answers stay good.

Everything below is how to measure each of those and how much each one saves.

</ELI5>

## 1. Where the Money Goes

ML systems spend in two phases with very different shapes:

<TrainingVsInferenceCostDiagram />

- **Training** is a large, bursty cost: GPU-hours for experiments and final runs, plus data preparation and storage. It's paid once per model version.
- **Inference** is a running cost that scales with usage: every prediction or generated token, every day, for as long as the product exists. For most deployed products, especially LLM features, inference is the larger share of lifetime spend.

Around both sit the costs teams forget: idle capacity reserved for peaks, storage of datasets, checkpoints and logs, data transfer between regions and clouds, evaluation runs (an LLM judge scoring thousands of outputs costs real money), and the engineers who operate it all.

The discipline of AI cost engineering is the same as for any cloud cost (often called FinOps): **measure cost per unit of value**, attribute it to the feature or team that causes it, find the biggest levers, and change them without lowering quality. The first step is being able to estimate the numbers before you spend them.

## 2. Estimating Training Cost

For transformer models there's a reliable first-principles estimate. Training takes about **6 floating-point operations per parameter per token**: 2 for the forward pass (a multiply and an add per parameter) and about 4 for the backward pass. So for $N$ parameters and $D$ training tokens:

$$C \approx 6ND \text{ FLOPs}, \qquad \text{GPU-hours} \approx \frac{6ND}{\text{peak FLOP/s} \times \text{MFU} \times 3600}$$

where **MFU** (model FLOPs utilization) is the fraction of the hardware's peak arithmetic the training run actually achieves; well-tuned large runs reach roughly 35 to 55%. For an 8-billion-parameter model trained on 15 trillion tokens on H100 GPUs:

```python
# Training compute: about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token (2 forward, 4 backward)
params, tokens = 8e9, 15e12                 # an 8B model trained on 15T tokens
flops = 6 * params * tokens
peak = 989e12                               # H100 dense BF16 peak, FLOP/s
for mfu in [0.5, 0.4, 0.3]:                 # model FLOPs utilization actually achieved
    gpu_hours = flops / (peak * mfu) / 3600
    print(f"MFU {mfu:.0%}: {gpu_hours / 1e6:4.2f}M GPU-hours, ${gpu_hours * 2.5 / 1e6:4.1f}M at $2.50/GPU-hour")
```

```text
MFU 50%: 0.40M GPU-hours, $ 1.0M at $2.50/GPU-hour
MFU 40%: 0.51M GPU-hours, $ 1.3M at $2.50/GPU-hour
MFU 30%: 0.67M GPU-hours, $ 1.7M at $2.50/GPU-hour
```

For comparison, Meta's model card for Llama 3.1 8B (trained on about 15 trillion tokens) reports 1.46 million H100 GPU-hours, two to three times this estimate. The gap is normal: the formula counts only the final run's arithmetic, not lower-utilization phases, restarts after failures, long-context stages and experiments. Use $6ND$ as a floor and plan with a multiple.

Two practical lessons. Efficiency work on the training run (mixed precision, better parallelism, fewer stalls) moves MFU, and every point of MFU is a proportional saving ([GPU/AI Infrastructure & Distributed Training](/docs/mlops/gpu-and-distributed-training)). And most teams never pretrain: fine-tuning an existing model on a few million tokens costs GPU-hours, not GPU-years, which is usually the right place to spend.

## 3. Unit Economics of an LLM Feature

For a feature built on a model API, cost per request is simply tokens times prices:

$$\text{cost} = n_{\text{in}} \cdot p_{\text{in}} + n_{\text{out}} \cdot p_{\text{out}}$$

with output tokens typically priced several times higher than input tokens. The numbers that matter are therefore **how many tokens go in and come out per request**, and they're usually far larger than the user's question, because the prompt also carries a system prompt, instructions, examples and retrieved documents.

Here's a RAG assistant: a 3,000-token system prompt, 6,000 tokens of retrieved passages, a 100-token question and a 400-token answer, at illustrative prices of \$3 per million input tokens and \$15 per million output tokens, with cached input billed at 10% (a common discount for provider prompt caching). Each row applies one more change:

```python
# Unit economics of one LLM feature. Prices are illustrative per-million-token rates; use your provider's.
PRICE_IN, PRICE_OUT, CACHED_IN_DISCOUNT = 3.00, 15.00, 0.10   # cached input billed at 10% of normal

def cost(system_tokens, retrieved_tokens, question_tokens, output_tokens, cache_system=False):
    cached = system_tokens if cache_system else 0
    fresh_in = system_tokens - cached + retrieved_tokens + question_tokens
    return (fresh_in * PRICE_IN + cached * PRICE_IN * CACHED_IN_DISCOUNT + output_tokens * PRICE_OUT) / 1e6

base = dict(system_tokens=3_000, retrieved_tokens=6_000, question_tokens=100, output_tokens=400)
variants = {
    "as built":                         base,
    "prompt caching on system prompt":  {**base, "cache_system": True},
    "+ retrieve 3 chunks, not 12":      {**base, "cache_system": True, "retrieved_tokens": 1_500},
    "+ cap answers at 200 tokens":      {**base, "cache_system": True, "retrieved_tokens": 1_500, "output_tokens": 200},
}
for name, v in variants.items():
    per = cost(**v)
    print(f"{name:<33} ${per:.4f}/request   ${per * 2_000_000:>8,.0f}/month at 2M requests")
```

```text
as built                          $0.0333/request   $  66,600/month at 2M requests
prompt caching on system prompt   $0.0252/request   $  50,400/month at 2M requests
+ retrieve 3 chunks, not 12       $0.0117/request   $  23,400/month at 2M requests
+ cap answers at 200 tokens       $0.0087/request   $  17,400/month at 2M requests
```

The user's question is 100 of 9,500 input tokens; the cost is almost entirely context. Three changes that never touch the model cut the bill by 74%: caching the stable system prompt, retrieving fewer but better passages, and asking for shorter answers. Each must be checked against quality: fewer passages can lower recall, and a length cap can cut useful answers ([LLM Evaluation & RAGOps](/docs/mlops/llm-evaluation-and-ragops) has the measurements). Often they improve it, since long, noisy contexts are harder for a model to use than short, relevant ones.

**Agents multiply all of this.** An agent that takes eight steps re-sends a growing conversation on every step, so its cost grows roughly with the square of the number of steps unless the context is trimmed or cached. Track cost per *completed task*, not per call, and set a hard budget per task.

## 4. Utilization: Paying for Idle Hardware

When you run your own hardware, the bill is for time, not work. A GPU at 10% utilization costs the same per hour as one at 90%.

<UtilizationCostDiagram />

[LLM Hosting & Serving Patterns](/docs/mlops/llm-hosting-and-serving-patterns#4-what-self-hosting-costs) computed what that means per token: the same GPU costs \$0.28 per million tokens when fully busy and \$5.56 at 5% utilization, twenty times more. Low utilization has a few common causes, each with its own fix:

- **Capacity sized for peak, running all day.** Autoscale replicas with load, scale to zero where cold starts are acceptable, and use serverless GPUs for spiky traffic ([Kubernetes](/docs/mlops/kubernetes#7-autoscaling-pods-and-nodes)).
- **Too little batching.** One request at a time leaves most of a GPU's capacity unused; dynamic or continuous batching serves many at once ([APIs & Model Serving](/docs/mlops/model-serving#6-dynamic-batching)).
- **Oversized hardware.** A small model on a large GPU wastes most of it; share GPUs with MIG partitions or several models per server, or pick a smaller GPU.
- **Training jobs stalled on data.** GPUs waiting for the data loader show high allocation and low utilization; profile and fix the input pipeline.

And pay less for the same hours: **reserved or committed-use** pricing for steady baseline load, and **spot or preemptible** instances (often 60 to 90% cheaper) for interruptible work such as training with frequent checkpoints, batch inference and evaluation.

Measure before optimizing. A high bill with low utilization is an efficiency problem with known fixes; a high bill with high utilization means the workload really is that big, and the levers are the model and the request, which the next sections cover.

## 5. Routing: The Right Model for Each Request

Most traffic is easy. If a small model handles easy requests well, sending everything to a large model wastes money on the majority to serve the minority.

<ModelRoutingSavingsDiagram />

There are two common designs. A **router** classifies each request up front (by type, length or a small classifier) and sends it to one model. A **cascade** tries the small model first and escalates to the large one when the small model's output looks unreliable, by its own confidence, a verifier, or a failed check such as invalid JSON.

This simulation shows the trade-off. 20% of 100,000 requests are hard; a small model costs a twentieth of a large one and succeeds on 93% of easy and 45% of hard requests; the large model succeeds on 96% and 90%. The small model's confidence is informative but noisy. The cascade escalates whenever confidence falls below a threshold:

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 100_000
hard = rng.random(n) < 0.2                       # 20% of requests are genuinely hard

# Per-request cost (USD) and success probability for each model, by difficulty (illustrative)
COST = {"small": 0.0006, "large": 0.0120}
P_OK = {"small": np.where(hard, 0.45, 0.93), "large": np.where(hard, 0.90, 0.96)}

small_ok = rng.random(n) < P_OK["small"]
large_ok = rng.random(n) < P_OK["large"]
# The small model's confidence is informative but imperfect: lower when it is wrong
confidence = np.clip(np.where(small_ok, rng.normal(0.85, 0.10, n), rng.normal(0.55, 0.15, n)), 0, 1)

print(f"{'strategy':<28} {'quality':>8} {'cost / 1k requests':>19}")
print(f"{'always large':<28} {large_ok.mean():8.1%} {COST['large'] * 1000:>18.2f}")
print(f"{'always small':<28} {small_ok.mean():8.1%} {COST['small'] * 1000:>18.2f}")
for threshold in [0.6, 0.7, 0.8]:
    escalate = confidence < threshold             # cascade: try small, escalate when unsure
    ok = np.where(escalate, large_ok, small_ok)
    cost = COST["small"] + escalate * COST["large"]
    print(f"{f'cascade, escalate below {threshold}':<28} {ok.mean():8.1%} {cost.mean() * 1000:>18.2f}"
          f"   ({escalate.mean():.0%} escalated)")
```

```text
strategy                      quality  cost / 1k requests
always large                    94.9%              12.00
always small                    83.5%               0.60
cascade, escalate below 0.6     93.0%               1.91   (11% escalated)
cascade, escalate below 0.7     95.9%               2.94   (19% escalated)
cascade, escalate below 0.8     96.7%               5.59   (42% escalated)
```

At a threshold of 0.7, the cascade matches the large model's quality for a quarter of its cost, escalating 19% of requests. The threshold is a dial: lower saves more and loses quality, higher spends more. Two cautions keep this honest. The simulation treats the two models' mistakes as independent, which lets the cascade slightly *beat* the large model; real models tend to fail on the same hard inputs, so expect the cascade to approach the large model's quality, not exceed it. And the whole scheme rests on the confidence signal: measure how well it separates right from wrong answers on labelled data before trusting it, exactly as you would calibrate an LLM judge ([ML & LLM Testing](/docs/mlops/ml-and-llm-testing#5-judging-quality-and-judging-the-judge)).

## 6. Making Each Call Cheaper

Beyond routing and context size, three techniques reduce the cost of running a given model:

- **Quantization** stores weights (and often the KV cache) in 8 or 4 bits instead of 16. It cuts memory, so a model fits on fewer or smaller GPUs, and usually raises throughput, a rare case where cost and latency improve together. The price is a small accuracy loss that must be measured on your task ([LLM Inference Optimization](/docs/mlops/llm-inference-optimization#quantization)).
- **Distillation** trains a small "student" model to imitate a large "teacher" on your task, often reaching most of the teacher's quality at a fraction of the cost. It costs a training run and an evaluation set, and pays off for high-volume, narrow tasks.
- **Smaller models for narrow tasks.** Classification, extraction and routing rarely need a frontier model. A fine-tuned small model, or even a classical model on embeddings, is often both cheaper and better.

And for LLM APIs specifically: **batch APIs** (asynchronous processing within a day, usually at a substantial discount) for anything not interactive, such as nightly enrichment or evaluation runs; and **structured, concise outputs**, since output tokens cost the most.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: cost attribution and guardrails">

Cost engineering only works if costs are visible where decisions are made. Tag every model call with the feature, team, customer and model version, and send token counts and cost to the same observability stack as latency ([Observability](/docs/mlops/observability)), so dashboards show cost per feature and per customer next to quality metrics. For cloud resources, consistent tags on GPU nodes, buckets and endpoints let the bill be split the same way.

Then add guardrails that stop surprises rather than report them: per-customer and per-feature rate limits and monthly budgets enforced in the gateway; a maximum number of steps and tokens per agent task; alerts on cost per request rising (often the first sign of a prompt change that bloated the context or an agent that loops); and anomaly detection on daily spend. A runaway agent or a retry loop against an expensive model can spend a month's budget in a day, and only a hard limit stops it in time.

</GoDeeper>

## 7. The Central Trade-off: Quality, Latency, Cost

Every technique on this page moves a system along three axes at once:

<QualityLatencyCostTriangleDiagram />

A smaller or more quantized model is cheaper and faster, and somewhat less capable. Larger batches cut cost per token and raise each request's latency. Retrieving less is cheaper and faster, and can lose information. A cache hit is free and fast, and only for repeated requests. Very few changes are free wins; most choose a different point on the surface.

The engineering job is choosing the point that a specific product needs. A real-time chat feature has a tight latency budget; a nightly batch job has none and should run on the cheapest, slowest option. A customer-facing medical answer has a high quality floor; an internal draft generator has a lower one. Write those requirements down as targets (a quality threshold on the evaluation set, a p95 latency budget, a cost per task), and then every optimization becomes a measurable question: does it stay within the quality and latency targets while lowering cost?

## 8. When *Not* to Optimize Cost Yet

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Still finding out whether the feature is useful | Use the most capable model and ship; optimize once usage and value are clear |
| Spend is small next to engineering time | Leave it; an engineer-week costs more than months of a small bill |
| No quality measurement in place | Build the evaluation set first; cost cuts without it trade quality blindly |
| Utilization is already high and quality is at its floor | The workload is genuinely that size; revisit pricing (commitments, providers) rather than the system |
| Regulated or safety-critical outputs | Optimize latency and cost only within a fixed, verified quality bar, and re-verify after every change |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| Two cost shapes | Training is bursty and per version; inference grows with usage and usually dominates |
| Training estimate | About $6ND$ FLOPs; GPU-hours = FLOPs over peak times MFU; real runs cost 2 to 3 times more |
| Unit economics | Cost per request is tokens times price; context, not the question, is most of it |
| Biggest LLM levers | Prompt caching, fewer retrieved tokens, shorter outputs, batch APIs |
| Utilization | Hardware is billed by time; autoscale, batch, right-size, use spot for interruptible work |
| Routing | Small model first, escalate when unsure; measure quality per route |
| Per-call savings | Quantization, distillation, small models for narrow tasks |
| Trade-off | Set quality and latency targets first, then minimize cost within them |
