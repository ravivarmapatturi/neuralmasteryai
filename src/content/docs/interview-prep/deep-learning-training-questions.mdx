# Deep Learning Training Interview Questions

These are the questions that separate people who've trained models from people who've read about it. Most start with something that went wrong in a training run. The memory and parameter questions want a number, and you can work every one out on paper.

## Reading the loss curve

<Calc id="dl-initial-loss" q="A freshly initialised 10-class classifier should predict roughly uniform probabilities. What cross-entropy loss should you see on the very first batch? (Natural log.)" answer={2.302585092994046} tolerance={0.01}>

Uniform predictions give each class probability 1/10, so the loss is −ln(1/10) = ln 10 = **2.303**. It's a free sanity check. A language model with a 50,000-token vocabulary should start near ln 50,000 ≈ 10.8. A first loss far above that means the initialisation is too large (confident wrong predictions). A first loss far below it means the labels are leaking into the inputs.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **The first loss is 40. What's wrong?** Logits are huge at initialisation, usually from weight scale or a missing normalisation, so the model is confidently wrong. Check the init and the last layer's scale.

</Calc>

<Choice id="dl-flat-loss" q="Training loss sits at 2.30 for a 10-class problem and doesn't move for 1,000 steps. What's your first debugging step?" answer={1} options={[{"text": "Add more layers.", "why": "Capacity isn't the problem when the model can't learn anything at all."}, {"text": "Try to overfit a single batch of 32 examples. If it can't, the bug is in the code, not the data.", "why": "Right. A working model and training loop can memorise one batch in a few hundred steps."}, {"text": "Collect more data.", "why": "The model isn't learning from the data it has, so more of it won't help."}, {"text": "Train for 10,000 more steps.", "why": "A loss pinned at exactly ln 10 means nothing is being learned. More steps won't change that."}]}>

Loss stuck at ln(classes) means the outputs are still uniform: gradients aren't reaching the weights, or they don't change anything. The classic causes are a learning rate of 0 or tiny, the optimiser not given the model's parameters, a forgotten `optimizer.step()` or `zero_grad()`, frozen layers, or inputs and labels that don't line up. **Overfitting one batch** separates "the code is broken" from "the problem is hard" in a couple of minutes.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **It overfits one batch but not the full data. Now what?** Now look at the data and the capacity: label noise, an input pipeline bug that shuffles labels relative to inputs, or too little model.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-val-rises" q="Training loss keeps falling. Validation loss falls until epoch 5, then rises every epoch. What's happening, and what do you do?" answer={1} options={[{"text": "Train longer and validation will come back down.", "why": "\"Double descent\" exists, but it isn't something to bank on. Keep the best checkpoint and fix the cause."}, {"text": "Overfitting from epoch 5: keep the epoch-5 checkpoint (early stopping), then add regularisation or augmentation.", "why": "Right. The model has started memorising the training set."}, {"text": "The learning rate is too low.", "why": "A low learning rate slows both curves. It doesn't make validation rise while training falls."}, {"text": "Validation data has a bug.", "why": "Possible, but this exact pattern, a validation minimum followed by a steady rise, is the signature of overfitting."}]}>

Training loss falling while validation loss rises means the model is fitting noise specific to the training set. Stop at the best validation epoch, then reduce the gap: weight decay, dropout, data augmentation, a smaller model, or more data.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Validation *accuracy* still improves while validation *loss* rises. How?** The model is getting more examples right but becoming over-confident on the ones it gets wrong. Loss punishes confidence, accuracy doesn't see it.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-oscillating" q="Loss jumps up and down by a lot from step to step and doesn't trend down. What do you change first?" answer={0} options={[{"text": "Lower the learning rate, by 3–10×.", "why": "Right. Steps that are too large overshoot the minimum and bounce around it."}, {"text": "Use a bigger model.", "why": "Capacity isn't the issue when the optimiser can't settle."}, {"text": "Raise the learning rate to escape a bad region.", "why": "Bigger steps make the bouncing worse."}, {"text": "Remove the learning-rate warmup.", "why": "Warmup reduces early instability. Removing it makes this worse."}]}>

Wild oscillation with no downward trend is the classic sign of a **learning rate that's too high**. Lower it, add warmup if you haven't, and check the batch isn't tiny (very noisy gradients look similar). A learning-rate range test, increasing the rate gradually over a few hundred steps and watching where the loss starts to blow up, finds a sensible value quickly.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **How would a batch that's too small look different?** Noisy, but still trending down on average. A learning rate that's too high usually doesn't trend down at all, or diverges.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-nan-fp16" q="Training in fp16, the loss suddenly becomes NaN after 200 steps. What's the most likely cause?" answer={3} options={[{"text": "The model is too small.", "why": "Size doesn't cause NaNs."}, {"text": "Too much weight decay.", "why": "Weight decay shrinks weights. It doesn't cause overflow."}, {"text": "The dataset contains NaN labels.", "why": "Possible, but then it would usually fail on the first batch containing one, not consistently after 200 steps of mixed-precision training."}, {"text": "Values overflowing fp16's range, which tops out around 65,504: exploding activations or gradients.", "why": "Right. fp16 has a narrow range, so one large activation or gradient becomes inf, and then NaN."}]}>

fp16's range is narrow, so a spike in activations or gradients overflows to inf, and the next operation produces NaN. Fixes, roughly in order: use **bf16** if the hardware supports it (fp32's exponent range), use dynamic **loss scaling** with fp16, **clip gradients**, lower the learning rate, and look for an unstable operation such as a softmax or log on unclamped values.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Why does loss scaling exist?** Small gradients underflow to zero in fp16. Multiplying the loss by a large factor before backward, then dividing the gradients, keeps them representable.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-eval-mode" q="Evaluating the same checkpoint twice on the same validation set gives 81.2% and then 79.6%. What did you forget?" answer={2} options={[{"text": "Turning off gradient calculation.", "why": "That saves memory and time, but it doesn't change the outputs."}, {"text": "Setting a random seed for the validation loader.", "why": "The order of the validation data shouldn't change accuracy at all."}, {"text": "Switching the model to evaluation mode, so dropout is still active.", "why": "Right. In training mode, dropout makes every forward pass random."}, {"text": "Shuffling the validation set.", "why": "Shuffling changes the order, not the predictions."}]}>

In training mode, **dropout** randomly zeroes activations and **batch norm** uses the current batch's statistics, so outputs change from run to run. Call `model.eval()` before evaluation (and wrap it in `torch.no_grad()` to save memory), then `model.train()` before training resumes.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **What does batch norm do differently in eval mode?** It uses the running mean and variance collected during training, not the statistics of the current batch.

</Choice>

## Learning rates and optimisers

<Calc id="dl-warmup" q="The learning rate warms up linearly from 0 to 3e-4 over the first 2,000 steps. What is it at step 500?" answer={7.5e-05} tolerance={0.01}>

Linear warmup: lr = 3e-4 × 500 ÷ 2,000 = **7.5e-05**. Warmup avoids huge, badly-directed updates in the first steps, before Adam's moment estimates have settled. It matters most for transformers trained with large learning rates.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Why does Adam need warmup at all?** Early on, its second-moment estimate is based on a few noisy gradients, so some parameters get very large effective steps. Warmup keeps those first updates small.

</Calc>

<Calc id="dl-cosine" q="After warmup, the learning rate follows a cosine decay from 3e-4 down to 0 over the remaining steps. What is it exactly halfway through the decay?" answer={0.00015} tolerance={0.01}>

Cosine schedule: lr = min + ½ (max − min)(1 + cos(π t ÷ T)). At t = T/2, cos(π/2) = 0, so lr = ½ × 3e-4 = **1.5e-04**. The rate falls slowly at first, fastest in the middle, and slowly again at the end.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Why decay the learning rate at all?** Large steps make fast progress early. Near the end, smaller steps let the model settle into a minimum instead of bouncing around it.

</Calc>

<Choice id="dl-adamw" q="Why do most transformer fine-tuning recipes use AdamW rather than Adam with an L2 penalty added to the loss?" answer={2} options={[{"text": "AdamW is faster per step.", "why": "The cost per step is essentially the same."}, {"text": "AdamW doesn't need a learning rate.", "why": "It needs one just like Adam."}, {"text": "With Adam, an L2 penalty gets divided by the adaptive denominator, so weights with large gradients are barely regularised. AdamW applies weight decay directly to the weights.", "why": "Right. That's the decoupling in AdamW."}, {"text": "Adam can't train transformers.", "why": "Adam trains them fine. AdamW regularises them more predictably."}]}>

In plain Adam, an L2 term becomes part of the gradient, then gets rescaled by the per-parameter adaptive denominator, so the effective regularisation varies by parameter in ways nobody intended. **AdamW** subtracts `lr × weight_decay × w` separately from the adaptive update, so decay behaves like true weight decay. In practice it generalises better and its settings transfer more reliably between models.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **What weight decay is typical?** Around 0.01–0.1 for transformers, usually not applied to biases or normalisation weights.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-batch-lr" q="You raise the batch size from 256 to 1,024 on the same SGD setup. What's a common starting point for the learning rate?" answer={2} options={[{"text": "Divide it by 4.", "why": "That goes the wrong way: smaller steps and fewer of them."}, {"text": "Keep it the same.", "why": "It'll work, but you'll get a quarter as many updates per epoch at the same step size, so training is slower."}, {"text": "Multiply it by 4 (linear scaling), with a warmup.", "why": "Right. Four times the examples per step gives a less noisy gradient, so a larger step is usually safe."}, {"text": "Learning rate doesn't depend on batch size.", "why": "The gradient's noise depends on the batch size, and so does the step size you can afford."}]}>

For SGD, the **linear scaling rule** (multiply the learning rate by the batch-size ratio, with warmup) is a good starting point up to fairly large batches. For Adam-style optimisers, square-root scaling is a common heuristic. Either way, treat it as a starting point and check with a short sweep.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **When does linear scaling stop working?** At very large batches the gradient noise is already small, so extra examples add little and the larger steps destabilise training.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-grad-clip" q="A transformer trains well but the loss spikes sharply every few thousand steps, then slowly recovers. What's a standard fix?" answer={3} options={[{"text": "Remove the warmup.", "why": "Warmup helps stability, so removing it is the wrong direction."}, {"text": "Raise the learning rate.", "why": "That makes spikes more likely."}, {"text": "Switch from AdamW to plain SGD.", "why": "A large change that also needs a whole new learning-rate tuning. Clipping is the targeted fix."}, {"text": "Clip the global gradient norm, often to 1.0.", "why": "Right. It caps the occasional huge update without changing normal steps."}]}>

Occasional loss spikes usually come from a rare batch producing a huge gradient. **Gradient-norm clipping** rescales the whole gradient when its norm exceeds a threshold, so the direction is kept but the step is bounded. If spikes continue, lower the learning rate, check for bad data in those batches, and look at the Adam epsilon and beta2 settings.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Clip by value or by norm?** By the global norm, usually. It keeps the gradient's direction, while clipping each value separately distorts it.

</Choice>

## Normalisation and initialisation

<Calc id="dl-he-init" q="With He initialisation for a ReLU layer that has 512 inputs, what is the standard deviation of the initial weights? (Three decimals.)" answer={0.0625} tolerance={0.01}>

He initialisation draws weights with variance 2 ÷ fan_in, so std = √(2 ÷ 512) = **0.0625**. The factor of 2 makes up for ReLU zeroing half its inputs, which keeps the variance of activations roughly constant from layer to layer. Xavier (Glorot) uses 1 ÷ fan_in, or 2 ÷ (fan_in + fan_out), which suits tanh.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **What happens with std 0.01 in a 50-layer ReLU net?** Activations shrink at every layer and vanish long before the output, so gradients do too. Training barely moves.

</Calc>

<Choice id="dl-batchnorm-small" q="You train a segmentation model with batch norm, and memory limits you to 2 images per GPU. Results are poor and noisy. Why, and what do you do?" answer={2} options={[{"text": "Remove all normalisation.", "why": "Deep networks usually train much worse without normalisation."}, {"text": "Use a larger learning rate.", "why": "That doesn't fix noisy normalisation statistics."}, {"text": "Batch statistics computed from 2 examples are very noisy. Use group or layer normalisation, or synchronised batch norm across GPUs.", "why": "Right. Batch norm needs reasonably large batches to estimate the mean and variance."}, {"text": "Batch norm doesn't work with images.", "why": "It was designed with image models in mind."}]}>

Batch norm normalises with the mean and variance of the current batch. With 2 examples those estimates are mostly noise, and the running statistics used at evaluation time don't match what training saw. **GroupNorm** and **LayerNorm** normalise within each example, so they don't depend on batch size. **SyncBatchNorm** computes statistics across all GPUs.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Why do transformers use LayerNorm?** Sequence lengths and batch composition vary, and normalising each token's features independently avoids depending on other examples in the batch.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-residual" q="A plain 50-layer network trains worse than an 18-layer one, even on the training set. What's the usual fix?" answer={3} options={[{"text": "More dropout.", "why": "Dropout fixes overfitting. This network can't even fit the training set."}, {"text": "More training data.", "why": "The deeper model is worse on the training set itself, so data isn't the bottleneck."}, {"text": "A larger learning rate.", "why": "The problem is that the signal degrades through many layers, not the step size."}, {"text": "Residual (skip) connections, so each block learns a change to its input.", "why": "Right. Skips give gradients a short path back through the network."}]}>

Worse *training* accuracy with more layers is the degradation problem, not overfitting. **Residual connections** (`x + f(x)`) make the identity function easy to represent and give gradients a direct path back through the network. Normalisation and careful initialisation help too. This is the ResNet result, and it's why every transformer block has two residual connections.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Why does `x + f(x)` help gradients?** The derivative of the sum includes an identity term, so the gradient always has a path that skips the block's own (possibly tiny) derivative.

</Choice>

## Parameters and memory

<Calc id="dl-linear-params" q="How many parameters are in a fully connected layer from 4,096 inputs to 4,096 outputs, with a bias?" answer={16781312} tolerance={0.0}>

Weights: 4,096 × 4,096 = 16,777,216. Biases: 4,096. Total **16,781,312**. A transformer block has several of these (the attention projections and the MLP), which is where most of a model's parameters live.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **How many in the MLP of a block with hidden size 4,096 and a 4× expansion?** Up and down projections: 2 × 4,096 × 16,384 ≈ 134M weights, before biases.

</Calc>

<Calc id="dl-conv-params" q="A 3×3 convolution takes 64 input channels to 128 output channels, with a bias. How many parameters?" answer={73856} tolerance={0.0}>

Each output channel has a 3 × 3 × 64 filter, plus a bias: 128 × (576 + 1) = **73,856**. The image size doesn't appear anywhere. A convolution's parameters are shared across every position.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **And the compute for a 56×56 output?** Each output value needs 3 × 3 × 64 multiply-adds: 56 × 56 × 128 × 576 ≈ 231M multiply-adds.

</Calc>

<Calc id="dl-weights-memory" q="How much GPU memory do the weights alone of a 7-billion-parameter model take in bf16? (In GB, where 1 GB is 10⁹ bytes.)" answer={14.0} tolerance={0.02} unit="GB">

bf16 uses 2 bytes per parameter: 7 × 10⁹ × 2 = **14 GB**. That's only the weights. Serving also needs memory for the KV cache and activations, and training needs far more.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **And in int4?** Half a byte per parameter: about 3.5 GB, plus a little for scales and zero-points.

</Calc>

<Calc id="dl-adam-memory" q="Training that 7B model with Adam in mixed precision: bf16 weights and gradients (2 bytes each), plus fp32 master weights and Adam's two moments (4 bytes each). How much memory before activations? (GB, 10⁹ bytes.)" answer={112.0} tolerance={0.02} unit="GB">

Per parameter: 2 (weights) + 2 (gradients) + 4 (master copy) + 4 (first moment) + 4 (second moment) = 16 bytes. 7 × 10⁹ × 16 = **112 GB**, before a single activation. That's why full fine-tuning a 7B model needs several GPUs or sharding (ZeRO, FSDP), and why LoRA is popular.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **What does ZeRO stage 3 / FSDP do with this?** It shards the weights, gradients and optimiser states across GPUs, so each holds about 1/N of the 112 GB, then gathers what each layer needs just in time.

</Calc>

<Choice id="dl-oom-accumulate" q="Training runs out of memory at batch size 32. You want to keep an effective batch of 32. What do you do?" answer={2} options={[{"text": "Lower the learning rate.", "why": "That doesn't change memory use."}, {"text": "Use a smaller learning-rate warmup.", "why": "Warmup doesn't affect memory."}, {"text": "Gradient accumulation: run 4 micro-batches of 8, summing the gradients, then step once.", "why": "Right. Same effective batch, a quarter of the activation memory per forward pass."}, {"text": "Train fewer epochs.", "why": "Memory is used per step, not per epoch."}]}>

Activation memory scales with the micro-batch size. **Gradient accumulation** splits the batch, accumulates gradients over several forward/backward passes, then calls `optimizer.step()` once, which is mathematically the same update (apart from batch-norm statistics). Combine it with **activation checkpointing** (recompute activations during backward) and mixed precision if you need more room.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **What does activation checkpointing cost?** Extra compute, roughly one more forward pass, in exchange for storing only a few activations per block.

</Choice>

<Calc id="dl-effective-batch" q="Each GPU processes a micro-batch of 8, you accumulate gradients over 16 micro-batches, and you train on 4 GPUs with data parallelism. What's the effective batch size?" answer={512} tolerance={0.0}>

Effective batch = micro-batch × accumulation steps × GPUs = 8 × 16 × 4 = **512**. This is the number to use when you reason about the learning rate.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **You move to 8 GPUs and want the same effective batch. What changes?** Halve the accumulation steps to 8.

</Calc>

<Calc id="dl-training-time" q="You need to train on 1 billion tokens. Each GPU processes 50,000 tokens a second, and you have 8 of them. How many minutes does one pass take, ignoring overheads?" answer={41.666666666666664} tolerance={0.02} unit="minutes">

Throughput is 8 × 50,000 = 400,000 tokens a second. 1 × 10⁹ ÷ 400,000 = 2,500 seconds = **41.7 minutes**. Real runs add data loading, evaluation, checkpointing and communication, so a budget 20–40% above this is common.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Scaling to 64 GPUs: 8× faster?** Rarely. Communication between GPUs grows, and the per-GPU batch or the global batch has to change. Measure the scaling efficiency.

</Calc>

<Choice id="dl-bf16" q="Why do most large-model training runs now use bf16 rather than fp16?" answer={1} options={[{"text": "bf16 uses less memory than fp16.", "why": "Both use 2 bytes per value."}, {"text": "bf16 has the same exponent range as fp32, so it rarely overflows and doesn't need loss scaling.", "why": "Right. It gives up mantissa precision instead, which training tolerates well."}, {"text": "bf16 is more precise than fp16.", "why": "It's less precise: 8 significant bits against fp16's 11."}, {"text": "fp16 isn't supported on GPUs.", "why": "fp16 is widely supported. bf16 needs newer hardware."}]}>

Both are 16-bit. fp16 spends its bits on precision (10 stored mantissa bits) with a small range (max about 65,504). **bf16** keeps fp32's 8-bit exponent, so its range matches fp32, and stores only 7 mantissa bits. Neural network training is far more sensitive to overflow and underflow than to low precision, so bf16 trains stably without loss scaling.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **Where does bf16's low precision hurt?** Accumulating many small values, like sums in normalisation layers or optimiser updates. That's why master weights and some reductions stay in fp32.

</Choice>

<Choice id="dl-augment-split" q="An image classifier scores 98% on validation and 81% on new photos. You find that augmented copies of the same originals appear in both the training and validation sets. What's the fix?" answer={3} options={[{"text": "Stop augmenting.", "why": "Augmentation helps training. The bug is where it was applied."}, {"text": "Use a bigger validation set.", "why": "A bigger contaminated validation set is still contaminated."}, {"text": "Use stronger augmentation.", "why": "More augmentation makes the copies more varied, but they're still copies of photos the model has seen."}, {"text": "Split by original image first, then augment only the training split.", "why": "Right. Variants of one photo belong on one side of the split."}]}>

Any group of near-duplicates (augmentations, frames from the same video, photos from the same patient) must stay on one side of the split. Split first, by the group, then augment the training set only, ideally on the fly. Check for near-duplicates across splits with perceptual hashes or embedding similarity.

**Follow-ups you'll get**

- **What other groups leak like this?** Several photos of the same product, recordings from the same speaker, sentences from the same document.

</Choice>
