# Optimizers

[Loss Functions](/docs/deep-learning/loss-functions) defined the number training makes smaller, and [backpropagation](/docs/deep-learning/neural-network-fundamentals) computes its gradient with respect to every weight. The **optimizer** decides what to do with that gradient: how far to step, in which direction, and whether to use what earlier gradients said. Plain gradient descent, "step against the gradient", from [Calculus & Optimization](/docs/mathematics-for-ai/calculus-optimization), works on paper and struggles in practice, because real loss surfaces are steep in some directions and flat in others. This page builds up the optimizers the field adopted to deal with that, each fixing a specific failure of the one before: momentum, the adaptive methods AdaGrad and RMSProp, Adam, and AdamW, today's default. It measures them on two ravines of different steepness, checks a hand-written Adam against PyTorch's, and ends with newer optimizers and how to choose.

<ELI5>

Imagine walking down into a long, narrow valley in thick fog. You can only feel the slope under your feet.

If you always step straight downhill, you mostly walk into the steep side wall, then the other side wall, zig-zagging across the valley and barely moving along it. That's plain gradient descent.

If you're a heavy ball rolling instead, the side-to-side pushes cancel out and the push along the valley adds up, so you pick up speed in the direction that matters. That's momentum.

If you're a careful hiker who takes small steps on steep ground and long strides on flat ground, you can walk along the valley quickly without being thrown into the walls. That's Adam. Everything below is those three ideas made exact.

## 1. Gradient Descent and How Much Data per Step

The base update is the same for every optimizer on this page: move the parameters $\theta$ a small step against the gradient of the loss,

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\, \nabla_\theta L(\theta)
$$

with **learning rate** $\alpha$. The variants differ first in how much data the gradient is computed on.

### Gradient Descent Variants (by batch size)

- **Batch gradient descent** uses the whole dataset for every step. The direction is exact, but each step costs a full pass over the data, which for millions of examples is far too slow.
- **Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)**, strictly, uses one example per step. Each gradient is a very noisy estimate, but steps are cheap and learning starts immediately.
- **Mini-batch gradient descent** uses a small random batch, typically 32 to a few thousand examples. The estimate is good enough, the steps are cheap, and a batch maps naturally onto GPU parallelism. It's what everyone uses, and in deep learning "SGD" almost always means this.

The noise in mini-batch gradients isn't only a cost. It helps the optimizer move off saddle points and out of sharp, narrow minima that tend to generalize poorly. Larger batches give cleaner gradients and better hardware use, and need a larger learning rate to make the same progress per example.

## 2. Why Plain Gradient Descent Struggles

Near a minimum, a loss surface looks like a bowl, and its shape is described by its curvature in each direction. The simplest bowl with two different curvatures is

$$
f(x, y) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(x^2 + k\, y^2\right)
$$

with curvature 1 along $x$ and $k$ along $y$. Its gradient is $(x,\; k y)$, so a gradient step multiplies $x$ by $(1 - \alpha)$ and $y$ by $(1 - \alpha k)$.

**The learning-rate trap.** For the step not to overshoot and diverge along $y$, you need $\lvert 1 - \alpha k \rvert \lt  1$, so $\alpha \lt  2/k$. But then along $x$ each step only shrinks it by a factor of $1 - \alpha \lt  1 - 2/k$, and with $k = 10{,}000$ that's a reduction of 0.02% per step. The steep direction sets the speed limit, and the flat direction crawls. The ratio of largest to smallest curvature is the **condition number**, and gradient descent's convergence slows roughly in proportion to it.

Real loss surfaces are worse: millions of directions, curvatures spanning many orders of magnitude, and different layers whose gradients differ in scale by factors of hundreds. Every optimizer below is a way out of this trap.

## 3. Momentum

**Momentum** (Polyak, 1964) keeps a running velocity $v$, an exponentially weighted sum of past gradients, and steps along it:

$$
v \leftarrow \beta v + \nabla L(\theta), \qquad \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha v
$$

with $\beta$ typically 0.9. In a ravine, the gradient across the valley flips sign every step, so those components cancel in $v$. The gradient along the valley points the same way every step, so those components accumulate, up to $1/(1 - \beta) = 10$ times the single-step size. The optimizer gains speed in the consistent direction and damps the oscillation.

(Some texts write $v \leftarrow \beta v + (1 - \beta)\nabla L$, an average rather than a sum; it's the same method with the learning rate rescaled by $1 - \beta$. PyTorch uses the sum.)

**Nesterov momentum** evaluates the gradient at the point the velocity is about to carry you to, rather than where you are, and corrects in advance. It is slightly better in theory and practice at almost no extra cost.

**The limitation:** momentum still uses one learning rate for every parameter, and that rate is still capped by the steepest direction. It accelerates the flat direction but doesn't remove the cap.

## 4. Adaptive Learning Rates

The other route out of the trap is to give each parameter its own learning rate, scaled by how large its gradients usually are: small steps where gradients are large, big steps where they're small.

**AdaGrad** (Duchi et al., 2011) divides each parameter's step by the square root of the sum of all its past squared gradients:

$$
G_i \leftarrow G_i + g_i^2, \qquad \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{G_i} + \epsilon}\, g_i
$$

Parameters with consistently large gradients get small steps, and rarely updated ones, such as embeddings of rare words, keep large steps, which made AdaGrad good for sparse features. Its flaw is that $G_i$ only grows, so every learning rate decays towards zero and training eventually stalls, converged or not.

**RMSProp** (Hinton, 2012, in lecture notes) replaces the sum with an exponential moving average, so old gradients are forgotten:

$$
v_i \leftarrow \gamma v_i + (1 - \gamma)\, g_i^2, \qquad \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{v_i} + \epsilon}\, g_i
$$

Now the effective learning rate tracks the *recent* gradient scale and can rise again if gradients shrink. **AdaDelta** is a close relative that also removes the global learning rate.

Dividing by $\sqrt{v_i}$, the typical gradient size, makes the step for each parameter roughly $\alpha$ times the sign of its gradient, whatever that parameter's scale. That's what lets an adaptive method take the same-sized step in a steep direction and a flat one.

## 5. Adam

### Adam and Its Descendants

**Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2014) combines the two ideas: momentum for the direction and RMSProp for the per-parameter scale. It keeps two moving averages per parameter, the **first moment** (mean gradient) and the **second moment** (mean squared gradient):

$$
\begin{aligned}
m &\leftarrow \beta_1 m + (1 - \beta_1)\, g \\
v &\leftarrow \beta_2 v + (1 - \beta_2)\, g^2
\end{aligned}
$$

with defaults $\beta_1 = 0.9$ and $\beta_2 = 0.999$.

**Bias correction.** Both averages start at zero, so for the first steps they underestimate the true values. After one step, $m = (1 - \beta_1) g = 0.1\, g$, a tenth of the gradient. Since the weights of an exponential average sum to $1 - \beta^t$ after $t$ steps, dividing by that undoes the bias:

$$
\hat{m} = \frac{m}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat{v} = \frac{v}{1 - \beta_2^t}
$$

and the update is

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\, \frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon}
$$

The ratio $\hat{m}/\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ is about $\pm 1$ when gradients are consistent and smaller when they're noisy, so $\alpha$ is close to the largest step any parameter takes. That makes Adam's learning rate unusually easy to set and transfer between problems, which is a large part of its popularity. It costs two extra numbers per parameter, $m$ and $v$, so its state takes twice the memory of the weights.

## 6. Measuring Them

Here are SGD, momentum and Adam written out in NumPy, raced on the ravine from section 2 at two condition numbers, then checked against PyTorch's own Adam. SGD and momentum get the largest stable learning rate, $1.9/k$; Adam gets one fixed learning rate of 0.1 for both.

```python
import numpy as np
import torch

# A ravine: f(x, y) = 0.5 * (x^2 + k y^2). Curvature 1 along x, k along y; minimum at (0, 0).
# SGD and momentum use the largest stable learning rate, just under 2/k.
def sgd(p, g, s, lr):
    return p - lr * g, s
def momentum(p, g, s, lr, beta=0.9):
    s["v"] = beta * s.get("v", 0) + g              # PyTorch-style momentum buffer
    return p - lr * s["v"], s
def adam(p, g, s, lr=0.1, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    s["t"] = s.get("t", 0) + 1
    s["m"] = b1 * s.get("m", 0) + (1 - b1) * g
    s["v"] = b2 * s.get("v", 0) + (1 - b2) * g ** 2
    m_hat, v_hat = s["m"] / (1 - b1 ** s["t"]), s["v"] / (1 - b2 ** s["t"])
    return p - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + eps), s

for k in (100, 10_000):
    H = np.array([1.0, k])
    grad = lambda p: H * p
    lr = 1.9 / k
    for name, step in [("SGD", lambda p, g, s: sgd(p, g, s, lr)),
                       ("Momentum", lambda p, g, s: momentum(p, g, s, lr)),
                       ("Adam", adam)]:
        p, s = np.array([10.0, 1.0]), {}
        for t in range(1, 2001):
            p, s = step(p, grad(p), s)
            if np.linalg.norm(p) < 1e-3:
                break
        result = f"converged in {t:4d} steps" if np.linalg.norm(p) < 1e-3 else f"not converged after 2000 steps, |p| = {np.linalg.norm(p):.2f}"
        print(f"curvature ratio {k:6,}  {name:9s} {result}")

# Check the hand-written Adam against PyTorch's for three steps on the same problem.
p_torch = torch.tensor([10.0, 1.0], requires_grad=True)
opt = torch.optim.Adam([p_torch], lr=0.1)
H = np.array([1.0, 100.0])
p, s = np.array([10.0, 1.0]), {}
for _ in range(3):
    opt.zero_grad(); (0.5 * (p_torch[0] ** 2 + 100 * p_torch[1] ** 2)).backward(); opt.step()
    p, s = adam(p, H * p, s)
print("ours: ", p.round(6), "  torch:", p_torch.detach().numpy().round(6))
```

```text
curvature ratio    100  SGD       converged in  481 steps
curvature ratio    100  Momentum  converged in  134 steps
curvature ratio    100  Adam      converged in  310 steps
curvature ratio 10,000  SGD       not converged after 2000 steps, |p| = 6.84
curvature ratio 10,000  Momentum  not converged after 2000 steps, |p| = 0.21
curvature ratio 10,000  Adam      converged in  310 steps
ours:  [9.700101 0.701586]   torch: [9.700101 0.701586]
```

The results are worth reading closely, because they aren't "Adam wins":

- **At condition number 100, momentum is fastest** (134 steps), then Adam (310), then SGD (481). On a clean, deterministic, mildly elongated bowl, momentum's acceleration is exactly what's needed, and Adam's fixed step size makes it circle the minimum for a while at the end.
- **At condition number 10,000, only Adam converges.** SGD's learning rate is capped at $1.9/10{,}000$ by the steep direction and it barely moves along the flat one; momentum helps tenfold but not enough. Adam takes the same 310 steps as before, because dividing by $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ removes each direction's scale. **Adam is invariant to rescaling individual parameters.**
- **The hand-written Adam matches PyTorch to six decimal places**, so the equations in section 5 are exactly what the library does.

Deep networks look much more like the second ravine than the first: gradient scales differ by orders of magnitude across layers and parameter types, and gradients are noisy. That's why adaptive methods became the default, and why well-tuned SGD with momentum still competes on some problems, such as training image classifiers, where scales are more uniform.

## 7. AdamW: Fixing Weight Decay

L2 regularization adds $\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\lVert\theta\rVert^2$ to the loss, which adds $\lambda\theta$ to the gradient. With plain SGD that's the same as shrinking every weight by a factor $(1 - \alpha\lambda)$ each step: **weight decay**. With Adam it isn't. The $\lambda\theta$ term goes into $m$ and $v$ and is divided by $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$, so weights with large gradients are barely decayed and weights with small gradients are decayed heavily, not what anyone intended.

**AdamW** (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017) **decouples** the decay: compute the Adam step from the loss gradient alone, then shrink the weights directly,

$$
\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\left(\frac{\hat{m}}{\sqrt{\hat{v}} + \epsilon} + \lambda\,\theta\right)
$$

Every weight now decays at the same relative rate. They showed AdamW generalizes better and that its best learning rate and weight decay can be tuned independently. **AdamW is the default optimizer for transformers and nearly all large-model training.** Typical settings for language models are $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.95$ (a shorter memory, which reacts faster to gradient spikes at scale), weight decay 0.1, and a learning-rate schedule with warmup and decay ([Initialization, Regularization and Scheduling](/docs/deep-learning/initialization-regularization-scheduling)).

## 8. Newer Optimizers

Adam's design is from 2014, and optimizer research is active, mostly aimed at large-model training where memory and stability dominate.

- **Lion** (Chen et al., 2023) was found by automated search. It keeps only the momentum and updates with its *sign*: every parameter moves by exactly $\pm\alpha$. It needs half of Adam's optimizer memory, and is competitive on some large vision and language runs, with a smaller learning rate and larger weight decay than AdamW.
- **8-bit optimizers** (Dettmers et al., 2021) store Adam's $m$ and $v$ in 8 bits with block-wise scaling, cutting optimizer memory by about 75% with little change in results.
- **LAMB** (You et al., 2019) scales each layer's Adam update by the ratio of the weight norm to the update norm. It kept BERT pretraining stable at batch sizes of tens of thousands.
- **Shampoo and SOAP** approximate second-order information with per-layer preconditioner matrices, rather than Adam's per-parameter diagonal. They take fewer steps, at more computation per step.
- **Muon** (Jordan et al., 2024) orthogonalizes the momentum of each 2D weight matrix before using it as the update, so the step changes all directions of the matrix equally. It set training-speed records on small language-model benchmarks and was used, with stabilizing changes, to train Moonshot AI's Kimi K2 in 2025. It's applied to the hidden weight matrices, with AdamW kept for embeddings, norms and the output layer.

None of these has displaced AdamW as the default, but Muon in particular is an active area in 2026.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: why Adam behaves like sign descent, and what that implies">

When gradients are consistent over the averaging window, $\hat{m} \approx g$ and $\sqrt{\hat{v}} \approx \lvert g \rvert$, so Adam's step is close to $\alpha \cdot \operatorname{sign}(g)$: every parameter moves by about $\alpha$, however large or small its gradient. Three consequences follow.

- **The learning rate is a step size in parameter units.** With $\alpha = 10^{-3}$, no weight moves more than about $10^{-3}$ per step. That's why Adam's learning rates transfer between models more easily than SGD's, which are tied to gradient scale.
- **Gradient magnitude information is mostly discarded.** Lion makes this explicit by using the sign directly, and does about as well, which suggests the magnitude wasn't contributing much.
- **$\epsilon$ matters for parameters with tiny gradients.** If $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ is comparable to $\epsilon$ (default $10^{-8}$), the step shrinks. In low-precision training $\epsilon$ is sometimes raised to $10^{-6}$ or so for numerical stability.

Adam's noisy-gradient behaviour also matters: when gradients are mostly noise, $\hat{m}$ averages towards zero while $\sqrt{\hat{v}}$ stays large, so the step shrinks automatically. Adam takes cautious steps where the signal is unclear.

</GoDeeper>

## 9. Choosing an Optimizer

| Situation | Choice |
|---|---|
| Almost anything: transformers, most deep networks | **AdamW**, with warmup and a decaying learning rate |
| Image classifiers where the last fraction of accuracy matters | SGD with momentum (0.9) and a tuned schedule is still competitive |
| Optimizer memory is the bottleneck | 8-bit AdamW, or Lion |
| Very large batch sizes | LAMB, or AdamW with careful warmup |
| Large language-model pretraining where step efficiency matters | Muon for hidden matrices with AdamW for the rest is worth testing |
| Convex problems such as linear or logistic regression | Plain gradient descent, L-BFGS, or the [closed-form solution](/docs/machine-learning/linear-regression#7-closed-form-vs-gradient-descent) |

## 10. When *Not* to Use Each

| Situation | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Adam with `weight_decay` as the regularizer | AdamW, which decouples weight decay correctly |
| AdaGrad for long training runs | RMSProp or Adam, whose learning rates don't decay to zero |
| SGD on badly scaled parameters (embeddings next to norms next to deep weights) | An adaptive method, which removes per-parameter scale |
| A new optimizer adopted from a paper without tuning | AdamW first; new optimizers need their own learning rate and decay settings |
| Raising the learning rate to fix slow training without warmup | Add warmup, then raise it; large early steps with empty moment estimates cause divergence |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| Gradient descent | $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha \nabla L$; mini-batches make it practical |
| Ill-conditioning | The steepest direction caps the learning rate; flat directions crawl |
| Momentum | Accumulate past gradients; oscillations cancel, consistent directions speed up |
| AdaGrad, RMSProp | Per-parameter step scaled by gradient size; RMSProp forgets old gradients |
| Adam | Momentum plus RMSProp plus bias correction; step about $\alpha$ per parameter |
| Scale invariance | Adam is unaffected by rescaling individual parameters; SGD isn't |
| AdamW | Weight decay applied to weights directly, not through the adaptive scaling; the default |
| Memory | Adam stores two extra numbers per parameter; Lion one; 8-bit variants shrink both |
| Newer | Lion, Muon, Shampoo, SOAP; AdamW remains the safe default |
