# Training Deep Networks, In Full Depth

The pages before this one picked the pieces of a training loop one at a time: [activation functions](/docs/deep-learning/activation-functions), [loss functions](/docs/deep-learning/loss-functions), [optimizers](/docs/deep-learning/optimizers) and [initialization and learning-rate schedules](/docs/deep-learning/initialization-regularization-scheduling). Every one of them works for a network with three layers. This page is about what goes wrong at fifty layers, or five hundred: why the gradient that reaches the first layer can be zero or astronomically large, derived from the chain rule, and the handful of ideas that fixed it. Those are normalization layers, residual connections, gradient clipping and mixed precision. Together they are the reason a modern language model with a hundred stacked blocks trains at all.

<ELI5>

Think of the telephone game. A message is whispered down a line of fifty people. If each person speaks a little too quietly, the last person hears nothing. If each person exaggerates a little, the last person hears nonsense. Either way the message is lost, and the longer the line, the worse it gets.

Training a deep network is the telephone game played backwards: the "how wrong were we" signal starts at the output and is passed back through every layer to reach the first one. Each layer either shrinks or grows it a little, and fifty small shrinks make nothing.

The fixes are what you'd do in the game. Make sure each person speaks at a steady volume (normalization). Run a wire from the end of the line straight to every person, so they hear the original message as well as their neighbour's version (residual connections). And if someone starts shouting, turn them down (gradient clipping). Everything below is that idea made exact.

</ELI5>

## 1. Why Depth Is Worth the Trouble

A network with one wide hidden layer can, in principle, approximate any reasonable function. That is the universal approximation theorem, and it says nothing about how wide the layer must be. For many functions that matter, a shallow network needs exponentially more units than a deep one to represent the same thing.

Depth lets a network build features out of features. In an image model, early layers detect edges, middle layers combine edges into textures and parts, and late layers combine parts into objects. In a language model, early blocks resolve which word a token is, later ones resolve what it refers to and what the sentence is doing. Each layer only has to learn a small step, and the steps compose. That reuse is why deep networks generalize better per parameter than wide shallow ones.

**The cost** is that training uses gradient descent, and gradient descent needs the gradient of the loss with respect to *every* weight, including the first layer's. To get it, the error signal has to travel back through every layer above. The next section shows exactly what happens to it on the way.

## 2. The Gradient Is a Product

Write a plain deep network as a chain of layers. Layer $l$ takes the previous activation $h_{l-1}$ and computes:

$$
h_l = \phi(W_l h_{l-1})
$$

where $W_l$ is the layer's weight matrix and $\phi$ is the activation function. The loss $L$ depends on the first layer's output $h_1$ only through every layer above it. The chain rule says the gradient arriving at layer 1 is the gradient at the top, multiplied by one **Jacobian** per layer: the matrix of how much each output of a layer moves when each input moves.

$$
\frac{\partial L}{\partial h_1} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_n} \cdot \prod_{l=2}^{n} \frac{\partial h_l}{\partial h_{l-1}}
$$

where each layer's Jacobian is

$$
\frac{\partial h_l}{\partial h_{l-1}} = \operatorname{diag}\big(\phi'(W_l h_{l-1})\big)\, W_l
$$

Each factor has two parts: the activation's slope $\phi'$ at that layer, and the weight matrix itself. Nothing about this product keeps it near 1. Suppose, roughly, that each factor scales the gradient by the same amount $a$. After $n$ layers:

$$
\left\lVert \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_1} \right\rVert \approx a^{\,n-1} \left\lVert \frac{\partial L}{\partial h_n} \right\rVert
$$

With $a = 0.8$ and 50 layers, $0.8^{49} \approx 1.8 \times 10^{-5}$. With $a = 1.2$, $1.2^{49} \approx 7.6 \times 10^{3}$. A per-layer factor that looks harmless becomes enormous or negligible through repetition. This is the whole problem; everything else on the page is a way of keeping $a$ close to 1 or of routing around the product.

### Vanishing & Exploding Gradients

**Vanishing gradients** happen when the factors are consistently below 1. The sigmoid is the classic cause: its slope $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z))$ is at most $0.25$, at $z = 0$, and near zero for large $|z|$. Even with perfect weights, each sigmoid layer multiplies the gradient by a quarter or less. Twenty layers give $0.25^{20} \approx 10^{-12}$. The first layers receive almost no learning signal, so they stay near their random initial values while the top layers learn. The network behaves like a shallow one sitting on top of a fixed random feature extractor.

**Exploding gradients** happen when the factors are consistently above 1, usually because the weights are too large. Gradients grow layer by layer until one update throws the weights so far that the loss jumps to a huge value or `NaN`. Recurrent networks are especially prone to both, because they apply *the same* weight matrix at every time step, so the product is a power of one matrix and its largest eigenvalue decides everything ([Sequence Models](/docs/deep-learning/sequence-models) covers that case).

**What fixes it, in order of history:** activations whose slope is 1 over half their range (ReLU), initialization that keeps each layer's scale near 1 (He, Xavier; derived on [the initialization page](/docs/deep-learning/initialization-regularization-scheduling)), normalization layers that reset the scale inside the network, and residual connections that add a path the product doesn't touch. The first two are about starting well. The next section is about staying well once the weights start moving.

## 3. Normalization Layers

Good initialization keeps activations at a sensible scale *at step 0*. Training then changes every weight, and the scale of each layer's inputs drifts as the layers below it change. A layer that was tuned for inputs with standard deviation 1 now sees inputs with standard deviation 7. Normalization layers solve this by measuring the scale of the activations as they flow and rescaling them every time.

All of them follow one recipe. Take a group of numbers $x_1, \dots, x_m$, compute their mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, standardize, then let the network undo it if it wants:

$$
\hat{x}_i = \frac{x_i - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y_i = \gamma\, \hat{x}_i + \beta
$$

$\epsilon$ is a small constant that prevents division by zero. $\gamma$ and $\beta$ are learned per feature, so the layer can choose any scale and shift it needs; what it can no longer do is drift there by accident. The variants differ only in **which numbers form the group**.

<NormalizationAxisDiagram />

**Batch Normalization** (Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015) groups each feature across the examples in the batch. For feature $j$, $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and spread of feature $j$ over the 64 or 256 examples in this batch. It made very deep convolutional networks train much faster and tolerate higher learning rates. Its cost is a dependency on the batch: statistics from a batch of 4 are noisy, and at inference time there may be a batch of 1. So BatchNorm keeps running averages of $\mu$ and $\sigma$ during training and uses those at inference, which means the network behaves differently in the two modes, a frequent source of bugs (forgetting `model.eval()` is the classic one).

**Layer Normalization** (Ba, Kiros and Hinton, 2016) groups all the features *of one example*. Each token's vector is normalized by its own mean and spread, so nothing depends on the batch size, sequence length, or which other examples are present, and training and inference behave identically. That independence is why Transformers use LayerNorm: they handle variable-length sequences, small batches, and generation one token at a time.

**RMSNorm** (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019) drops the mean subtraction and $\beta$ and divides by the root-mean-square only:

$$
y_i = \gamma\, \frac{x_i}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_j x_j^2 + \epsilon}}
$$

The authors found the re-centring contributed little, and removing it saves computation. Most current open language models (the Llama, Qwen, Mistral and DeepSeek families among them) use RMSNorm.

**Where it goes matters too.** The original Transformer normalized *after* adding the residual ("post-norm"). Almost all modern models normalize the input to each sub-layer *before* it ("pre-norm"), which keeps the residual path itself untouched. The next section explains why that path matters so much.

**The limitation:** normalization controls the *scale* of activations, but the gradient still has to pass through every layer's Jacobian on its way down. In very deep plain networks, even with BatchNorm, accuracy got *worse* as layers were added, and not from overfitting: training error rose too. Something else was needed.

## 4. Residual Connections

He, Zhang, Ren and Sun saw exactly that in 2015: a 56-layer plain network had higher *training* error than a 20-layer one. A deeper network can always represent the shallower one (set the extra layers to the identity), so this was an optimization failure, not a capacity limit. Their fix, the **residual block**, changes one line. Instead of a layer computing $y = F(x)$, it computes:

$$
y = x + F(x)
$$

The input skips over the layer and is added to its output. $F$ now only has to learn the *change* to make, the residual, and learning "change nothing" means pushing $F$'s weights towards zero, which is easy.

The bigger effect is on the gradient. Differentiate:

$$
\frac{\partial y}{\partial x} = I + \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}
$$

Through a stack of residual blocks, the product of Jacobians from section 2 becomes a product of $(I + J_l)$ terms. Expanding it, one of the terms is just $I$: a path from the loss to the first layer that passes through no weight matrices and no activation slopes at all. However small the $J_l$ get, the gradient arriving at layer 1 contains the top gradient unchanged. That is the "wire to every person" from the telephone game.

<AddNormDiagram />

ResNets with 152 layers won ImageNet in 2015, and a 1,001-layer version trained the following year. Every Transformer block uses two residual connections, one around attention and one around the feed-forward layer, which is why the stack of blocks in a language model is often called the **residual stream**: each block reads from it and adds its contribution back.

**The limitation of residuals** is that the stream keeps growing: every block adds to it, so its variance rises with depth. Pre-norm puts a normalization at the input to each block so the blocks see a stable scale, and many models also scale down the initial weights of each block's output layer by about $1/\sqrt{\text{depth}}$ (GPT-2 did this) so the sum doesn't start out large.

## 5. Measuring It: a 50-Layer Experiment

The claims above are measurable in a few lines. This builds four 50-layer networks, 256 units wide, runs one batch through each, and prints the norm of the gradient of the weights at layers 1, 25 and 50:

```python
import torch
import torch.nn as nn

torch.manual_seed(0)
DEPTH, WIDTH = 50, 256

class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, act, std, residual=False):
        super().__init__()
        self.linear = nn.Linear(WIDTH, WIDTH)
        nn.init.normal_(self.linear.weight, std=std)
        nn.init.zeros_(self.linear.bias)
        self.norm = nn.LayerNorm(WIDTH) if residual else nn.Identity()
        self.act, self.residual = act, residual

    def forward(self, x):
        out = self.act(self.linear(self.norm(x)))
        return x + out if self.residual else out

def grad_norms(act, std, residual=False):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    blocks = [Block(act, std, residual) for _ in range(DEPTH)]
    net = nn.Sequential(*blocks, nn.Linear(WIDTH, 1))
    loss = net(torch.randn(64, WIDTH)).pow(2).mean()
    loss.backward()
    return [blocks[i].linear.weight.grad.norm().item() for i in (0, 24, 49)]

he = (2 / WIDTH) ** 0.5
for name, args in [("sigmoid, std 0.05",            (torch.sigmoid, 0.05)),
                   ("tanh, std 0.15 (too big)",     (torch.tanh, 0.15)),
                   ("ReLU, He init",                (torch.relu, he)),
                   ("ReLU, He init, residual + LN", (torch.relu, he / DEPTH ** 0.5, True))]:
    g = grad_norms(*args)
    print(f"{name:30s} layer 1: {g[0]:9.2e}   layer 25: {g[1]:9.2e}   layer 50: {g[2]:9.2e}")
```

Output (PyTorch 2.5, CPU):

```text
sigmoid, std 0.05              layer 1:  0.00e+00   layer 25:  6.02e-19   layer 50:  5.25e-01
tanh, std 0.15 (too big)       layer 1:  3.06e+04   layer 25:  1.30e+02   layer 50:  6.25e-01
ReLU, He init                  layer 1:  4.87e-01   layer 25:  1.35e+00   layer 50:  3.49e+00
ReLU, He init, residual + LN   layer 1:  4.80e+00   layer 25:  4.03e+00   layer 50:  3.44e+00
```

Each row is one section of this page:

- **Sigmoid** vanishes completely. By layer 25 the gradient is $10^{-19}$; at layer 1 it has underflowed to exactly zero in 32-bit floats. Those layers will never learn.
- **tanh with weights too large** explodes in the other direction. Each layer multiplies the gradient by roughly $0.15 \times \sqrt{256} = 2.4$, so layer 1 sees a gradient 50,000 times larger than layer 50. One step at a learning rate that suits layer 50 would wreck layer 1.
- **ReLU with He initialization** is usable: the spread is a factor of 7 over 50 layers. This is the best a plain network gets.
- **Residual plus LayerNorm** is almost flat: 4.8, 4.0, 3.4. The branch weights were scaled down by $1/\sqrt{50}$, as described above, so the stream starts at a sensible size.

## 6. Regularization for Deep Nets

A network that trains well can also memorize. Large networks have far more parameters than training examples, and once the gradient flows they will happily fit noise. Regularization pushes them towards solutions that generalize.

**Dropout** (Srivastava et al., 2014) sets each activation to zero with probability $p$ at every training step, and scales the survivors by $1/(1-p)$ so the expected value is unchanged. No unit can rely on any particular other unit being present, so the network learns redundant, spread-out features. One way to see it: each step trains a different random sub-network, and all of them share weights, so the final network acts like an average over an enormous ensemble. At inference dropout is switched off. Typical rates are 0.1 in Transformers and up to 0.5 in older fully connected layers.

**Weight decay** shrinks every weight a little towards zero at each step. With Adam it should be applied directly to the weights, not added to the gradient, which is the difference between Adam with L2 and AdamW ([Optimizers](/docs/deep-learning/optimizers) covers why).

**Data is the strongest regularizer.** Very large language models are often trained with little or no dropout, because they see each token about once: there is nothing to memorize when no example repeats. Augmentation (crops, flips and colour jitter for images) manufactures more data where it is scarce.

Label smoothing, early stopping, stochastic depth and the rest of the toolkit are covered on [the regularization page](/docs/deep-learning/initialization-regularization-scheduling).

## 7. Keeping the Updates Stable

Healthy gradients are necessary but not sufficient. A single bad batch, an outlier, or the early chaos of training can still produce one update large enough to undo hours of progress. Three techniques keep updates in bounds.

**Gradient clipping by norm.** Before the optimizer step, compute the norm of the whole gradient vector $g$ across every parameter. If it exceeds a threshold $c$, scale it down:

$$
g \leftarrow g \cdot \min\!\left(1, \frac{c}{\lVert g \rVert}\right)
$$

The direction is unchanged; only the step length is capped. A threshold of 1.0 is the common default in language-model training. In PyTorch it is one line, placed between `loss.backward()` and `optimizer.step()`:

```python
grad_norm = torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
```

Logging the norm it returns is one of the most useful training diagnostics: steady, slow-moving norms mean healthy training; sudden spikes come before loss spikes.

**Learning-rate warmup and decay.** At step 0 the weights are random and Adam's running estimates of gradient scale are empty, so large steps are dangerous. Warmup raises the learning rate linearly from near zero over the first few hundred or thousand steps; afterwards it decays, usually along a cosine, so late training takes small careful steps.

<LrScheduleDiagram />

**Mixed precision.** Most of the arithmetic is done in 16-bit floats, which halves memory traffic and runs several times faster on tensor cores, while a 32-bit master copy of the weights receives the updates. There are two 16-bit formats, and they fail differently:

```python
import torch
for t in (torch.float32, torch.float16, torch.bfloat16):
    f = torch.finfo(t); print(f'{str(t):15s} max {f.max:.2e}  smallest normal {f.tiny:.2e}  eps {f.eps:.1e}')
print(torch.tensor(1e-8, dtype=torch.float16).item(), torch.tensor(1e-8, dtype=torch.bfloat16).item())
```

```text
torch.float32   max 3.40e+38  smallest normal 1.18e-38  eps 1.2e-07
torch.float16   max 6.55e+04  smallest normal 6.10e-05  eps 9.8e-04
torch.bfloat16  max 3.39e+38  smallest normal 1.18e-38  eps 7.8e-03
0.0 1.0011717677116394e-08
```

`float16` has more precision but a tiny range: a gradient of $10^{-8}$ becomes 0, and anything above 65,504 overflows. Training in it needs **loss scaling**: multiply the loss by a large factor before `backward()` so small gradients stay representable, then divide it back out before the update. `bfloat16` keeps float32's range with less precision, so small gradients survive and no loss scaling is needed. That is why most large-model training uses bfloat16 on hardware that supports it.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: what to look at when a deep network won't train">

Most failures show up in three numbers you can log every step: the loss, the global gradient norm (returned by `clip_grad_norm_`), and the learning rate.

- **Loss flat from the start.** Gradients are vanishing or the learning rate is far too small. Check per-layer gradient norms, as in section 5; if early layers are near zero, look at activations (sigmoid or tanh deep in the network), initialization, or a missing residual path.
- **Loss becomes `NaN` within the first steps.** Usually exploding gradients or a numerical problem: a learning rate too high without warmup, `log(0)` in a hand-written loss, or float16 overflow. Lower the learning rate, add warmup and clipping, compute the loss in float32.
- **Loss spikes late in training, then partially recovers.** Common in large Transformer runs. Gradient-norm spikes usually come first. Clipping, a lower peak learning rate, and normalizing queries and keys inside attention all reduce them; large runs also restart from a checkpoint before the spike and skip the offending batches.
- **Training loss fine, validation loss rising.** Not an optimization problem at all; this is overfitting, and section 6 applies.

A cheap first check for any new model: train on a single small batch until the loss is near zero. A network that can't memorize 32 examples has a bug, not a regularization problem.

</GoDeeper>

## 8. Putting It Together: a Modern Block

Every idea on this page appears in one Transformer block as it is written today (Llama-style, pre-norm):

$$
\begin{aligned}
h &= x + \operatorname{Attention}(\operatorname{RMSNorm}(x)) \\
y &= h + \operatorname{FFN}(\operatorname{RMSNorm}(h))
\end{aligned}
$$

Two residual connections keep a clean gradient path through the whole stack. Each sub-layer normalizes its own input, so it sees a stable scale however large the residual stream has grown. The feed-forward layer uses a smooth ReLU-family activation (SwiGLU) whose slope doesn't collapse. Training adds AdamW with weight decay, warmup then cosine decay, clipping at 1.0, and bfloat16 arithmetic. None of these is exotic; together they are what makes depth trainable.

## 9. When *Not* to Use These Techniques

| Situation | What to do instead |
|---|---|
| BatchNorm with batches of 1 to 8, or sequence models | LayerNorm, RMSNorm or GroupNorm; BatchNorm's statistics are too noisy |
| Dropout in a model trained for one pass over huge data | Usually leave it out; there is nothing to memorize, and it slows learning |
| A shallow network (2 to 4 layers) | Plain layers with He initialization; residuals and normalization add little |
| Clipping to hide a real bug | Find the cause first; a gradient norm that always hits the threshold means the threshold or learning rate is wrong |
| float16 without loss scaling | Use bfloat16, or enable automatic loss scaling (`torch.amp.GradScaler`) |
| Comparing models with and without dropout using training loss | Compare validation loss; dropout raises training loss by design |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
|---|---|
| The problem | The gradient at layer 1 is a product of one Jacobian per layer above it |
| Vanishing | Factors below 1 multiply to nothing; sigmoid's slope is at most 0.25 |
| Exploding | Factors above 1 multiply to overflow; weights too large are the usual cause |
| Normalization | Standardize a group of activations, then learn scale and shift; the variants differ in the group |
| BatchNorm vs LayerNorm | Across the batch vs across one example's features; Transformers use the latter, often as RMSNorm |
| Residuals | $y = x + F(x)$ gives the gradient an identity path through the whole stack |
| Dropout | Zero random activations during training; an implicit ensemble |
| Clipping | Cap the gradient's norm, keep its direction; log the norm every step |
| Mixed precision | bfloat16 keeps float32's range; float16 needs loss scaling |
