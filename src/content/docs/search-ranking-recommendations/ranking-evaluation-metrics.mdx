# Ranking Evaluation Metrics

<ELI5>
Imagine searching for a doctor's clinic. If a search engine gives you five addresses on a page, and the real doctor's clinic is buried down at the very bottom in fifth place while four closed pet shops appear above it, you will probably be annoyed and leave. Standard classification metrics like accuracy or precision only ask: "Did the list contain the doctor?" They do not care whether the doctor was at the top or at the bottom. Ranking metrics exist because in real life, nobody wants to dig to the bottom of the list.
</ELI5>

## Why Ranking Demands Order-Aware Metrics

Standard machine learning evaluation evaluates unordered sets: binary classification measures whether an item was predicted positive or negative, and regression measures the distance between a predicted continuous scalar and the ground truth.

In search and recommendation systems, position is everything:
1. **User Attention Decays Steeply**: Empirical eye-tracking studies (such as Joachims et al., 2005) and production click logs reveal that clicks heavily concentrate on top positions. Result #1 receives significantly more impressions and interaction opportunities than result #10.
2. **Relevance is Graded, Not Binary**: A document can be a perfect match (grade 3), somewhat helpful (grade 2), tangentially related (grade 1), or completely irrelevant (grade 0).
3. **Cutoffs Define User Experience**: Users rarely browse past the initial screen or visible slate. Systems are judged on their top-$k$ results (typically $k \in \{3, 5, 10\}$), rendering accuracy across an illustrative catalog of hundreds of thousands of remaining items irrelevant to immediate user satisfaction.

We evaluate ranking algorithms through two complementary lenses: **set-based metrics** that evaluate retrieval coverage, and **rank-aware metrics** that penalize delaying relevant results.

## Set-Based Metrics: Precision, Recall, and Hit Rate

Set-based metrics treat the top-$k$ items as an unranked bucket. They measure whether relevant items appeared, ignoring their permutation within the cutoff.

Let binary relevance $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate whether the item at position $i$ is relevant ($r_i = 1$ when graded relevance exceeds an operational threshold, e.g. $\text{grade} \ge 1$).

### Precision@k
The fraction of retrieved items in the top-$k$ that are relevant:
$$
\text{Precision@}k = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i=1}^{k} r_i
$$

- **Denominator**: Exactly $k$. If the system returns fewer than $k$ items, the denominator remains $k$ to penalize incomplete slates.
- **Limitation**: Precision ignores document order within the top-$k$. A list with relevant items at ranks 1 and 2 has the exact same Precision@3 as a list with relevant items at ranks 2 and 3.

### Recall@k
The fraction of all known relevant items in the corpus ($R_{\text{total}}$) retrieved in the top-$k$:
$$
\text{Recall@}k = \frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} \sum_{i=1}^{k} r_i
$$

- **Denominator**: $R_{\text{total}}$—the total count of known relevant documents in the evaluation collection for that query.
- **Trade-off**: In an illustrative large-scale retrieval scenario with $R_{\text{total}} = 10,000$ relevant pages across the web, Recall@10 is mathematically bounded at $10 / 10,000 = 0.001$. In candidate generation systems (such as [Semantic & Hybrid Search](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/semantic-and-hybrid-search)), Recall@$k$ (with $k \in \{100, 1000\}$) evaluates whether dense and sparse retrieval stages surface relevant items into the candidate pool for downstream ranking models.

### Hit Rate@k
A binary indicator showing whether at least one relevant item appeared in the top-$k$:
$$
\text{HitRate@}k = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^k r_i > 0 \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

Hit Rate@$k$ (also known as Success@$k$) is commonly used in navigational search, question answering, and task-oriented conversational agents where finding at least one acceptable result fulfills the user's intent.

## Rank-Aware Metrics: MRR, MAP, and NDCG

To penalize systems that bury relevant items beneath irrelevant clutter, rank-aware metrics incorporate position discounting.

### Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)
Reciprocal Rank (RR) evaluates the position of the **very first relevant result**:
$$
\text{RR} = \frac{1}{\text{rank}_1}
$$

Where $\text{rank}_1$ is the 1-indexed rank of the first relevant document. If no relevant document appears in the list, $\text{RR} = 0$.

Averaged across a benchmark set of $Q$ queries, this becomes **Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)**:
$$
\text{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \frac{1}{\text{rank}_1(q)}
$$

- If the first relevant item appears at rank 1, $\text{RR} = 1.0$.
- If it is delayed to rank 2, $\text{RR} = 0.5$.
- If it is delayed to rank 4, $\text{RR} = 0.25$.

MRR is especially well-suited for navigational queries (such as looking up a specific homepage or known item) where the user seeks a single correct entry point. Its limitation is that it evaluates only the rank of the first relevant result, ignoring all subsequent relevant items.

### Mean Average Precision (MAP)
For queries with multiple relevant answers, Average Precision ($\text{AP}$) computes the average of Precision@$i$ evaluated at every position where a relevant document occurs, normalized by the total number of known relevant items in the corpus:

$$
\text{AP@}k = \frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \text{Precision@}i \times r_i
$$

Where:
- $\text{Precision@}i = \frac{1}{i}\sum_{j=1}^i r_j$ is the precision evaluated at cutoff $i$.
- $r_i \in \{0, 1\}$ acts as a selector: precision contributes to the sum only when the item at position $i$ is relevant.
- $R_{\text{total}}$ is the total number of known relevant documents in the evaluation collection for that query. Dividing by $R_{\text{total}}$ ensures that $\text{AP@}k$ penalizes models for missing relevant items that were never retrieved within the top $k$.

Across a collection of test queries $Q$, the mean of $\text{AP}$ is **Mean Average Precision (MAP)**:
$$
\text{MAP} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \text{AP}(q)
$$

MAP rewards systems that place relevant documents at the very top, while still evaluating every relevant item retrieved.

### Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG & NDCG)
Neither MRR nor MAP natively handles **graded relevance** (e.g., distinguishing an exact 5-star match from an acceptable 3-star substitute). In 2002, Kalervo Järvelin and Jaana Kekäläinen introduced Cumulative Gain and Discounted Cumulative Gain.

#### Cumulative Gain (CG)
The unweighted sum of graded relevance scores $g_i \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ up to cutoff $k$:
$$
\text{CG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} g_i
$$

#### Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)
To penalize placing high-grade results lower down the list, each item's gain is divided by a logarithmic discount factor:
$$
\text{DCG@}k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{\text{gain}(g_i)}{\log_2(i + 1)}
$$

Two primary gain conventions exist in the literature:
- **Exponential Gain (Burges et al., 2005 / Manning et al., 2008)**: $\text{gain}(g_i) = 2^{g_i} - 1$. This heavily emphasizes surfacing perfect matches (grade 3 has gain $2^3 - 1 = 7$, compared to grade 1 with gain $2^1 - 1 = 1$). This is widely used in learning-to-rank competitions and web search benchmarks.
- **Linear Gain (Järvelin & Kekäläinen, 2002)**: $\text{gain}(g_i) = g_i$.

At rank 1 ($i = 1$), the denominator is $\log_2(1 + 1) = \log_2(2) = 1.0$, applying no discount. At rank 3, the denominator is $\log_2(4) = 2.0$, discounting the gain by 50%.

#### Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)
Because raw DCG depends on the number and grades of relevant items for a specific query, raw DCG scores cannot be averaged across queries. We normalize DCG by the **Ideal DCG (IDCG)**—the DCG score of the exact same candidate items sorted in perfect descending order of relevance:

$$
\text{NDCG@}k = \frac{\text{DCG@}k}{\text{IDCG@}k}
$$

$\text{NDCG@}k$ is bounded strictly in the range $[0.0, 1.0]$. A score of $1.0$ indicates that the top-$k$ results are ordered in ideal descending relevance order.

## Judgment Assumptions & Production Evaluation Edge Cases

When evaluating ranking systems, metrics depend heavily on judgment conventions and edge-case handling:

### 1. Fully Judged Slates vs. Unjudged Documents
In our five-item worked example below:
- **Fully Judged**: Every document has an explicit relevance judgment. Grade 0 explicitly denotes judged nonrelevant (not merely unjudged).
- **Unjudged Documents in Production**: In web search or large catalogs, human judges cannot annotate millions of items. Evaluation relies on *pooling* (judging only the union of top results from competing systems). Production benchmarks handle unjudged items through established conventions:
  - *Default Nonrelevant Convention*: Standard TREC evaluation treats unjudged documents as nonrelevant ($r_i = 0$ or grade 0). This prevents gaming but penalizes systems that retrieve valid novel items outside the pool.
  - *Preference-Based Evaluation (Bpref)*: Measures retrieval quality strictly over pairs of judged documents (Buckley & Voorhees, 2004), mitigating unjudged pooling bias.
  - *Counterfactual Propensity Scoring*: Reweights observed user clicks by inverse presentation propensities to correct for selection and position bias (Joachims et al., 2017).

### 2. Result Lists Shorter Than Cutoff k
When a system returns $m < k$ items (for example, only 2 documents returned for $k=5$):
- **Precision@k**: The denominator remains $k$ (the missing $k-m$ items count as 0 hits), directly penalizing the engine for returning an incomplete slate.
- **Recall@k**: The sum of hits among the $m$ returned items is divided by $R_{\text{total}}$.
- **DCG@k**: Missing positions simply contribute 0 gain to the summation.

### 3. Queries with Zero Relevant Items in Collection
When evaluating test collections, certain queries may have no known relevant documents ($R_{\text{total}} = 0$):
- In this scenario, Recall@k and Average Precision have undefined division ($0/0$).
- Standard benchmark practice either filters out queries with zero known relevant documents from the test collection beforehand, or defines Recall, AP, and NDCG as $0.0$ for that query to avoid numerical instability.

## Worked Mathematical Comparison

Consider an illustrative 5-item judged candidate pool for a query where the corpus contains exactly **$R_{\text{total}} = 3$** known relevant documents:
- **Doc A**: Grade 3 (Highly relevant, $r = 1$)
- **Doc B**: Grade 2 (Relevant, $r = 1$)
- **Doc C**: Grade 1 (Marginally relevant, $r = 1$)
- **Doc D**: Grade 0 (Judged nonrelevant, $r = 0$)
- **Doc E**: Grade 0 (Judged nonrelevant, $r = 0$)

Let us evaluate at cutoff **$k = 3$** across two candidate rankings:
- **Order 1 (High relevance first)**: `[Doc A, Doc D, Doc B, Doc C, Doc E]` $\to$ Grades `[3, 0, 2, 1, 0]`
- **Order 2 (Delayed relevance)**: `[Doc D, Doc A, Doc B, Doc C, Doc E]` $\to$ Grades `[0, 3, 2, 1, 0]`

| Metric | Order 1 `[A, D, B]` | Order 2 `[D, A, B]` | Mathematical Derivation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Precision@3** | $2/3 \approx 0.6667$ | $2/3 \approx 0.6667$ | 2 hits in top 3: both orderings retrieve $\{A, B\}$. Order is ignored. |
| **Recall@3** | $2/3 \approx 0.6667$ | $2/3 \approx 0.6667$ | Retrieved 2 out of $R_{\text{total}} = 3$ total relevant documents. |
| **Hit Rate@3** | $1.0$ | $1.0$ | At least one relevant document appears in the top 3. |
| **MRR** | **$1.0000$** | **$0.5000$** | Order 1: first hit at rank 1 ($1/1 = 1.0$). Order 2: first hit delayed to rank 2 ($1/2 = 0.5$). |
| **AP@3** | **$0.5556$** | **$0.3889$** | Order 1: $(1/1 + 2/3) / 3 = (5/3)/3 = 5/9 \approx 0.5556$. Order 2: $(1/2 + 2/3) / 3 = (7/6)/3 = 7/18 \approx 0.3889$. |
| **DCG@3 (exp)** | **$8.5000$** | **$5.9165$** | Order 1: $7/\log_2(2) + 0 + 3/\log_2(4) = 7 + 1.5 = 8.5$. Order 2: $0 + 7/\log_2(3) + 3/\log_2(4) = 4.4165 + 1.5 = 5.9165$. |
| **IDCG@3 (exp)** | $9.3928$ | $9.3928$ | Ideal ranking `[A, B, C]` with grades `[3, 2, 1]`: $7/\log_2(2) + 3/\log_2(3) + 1/\log_2(4) = 7 + 1.8928 + 0.5 = 9.3928$. |
| **NDCG@3 (exp)** | **$0.9049$** | **$0.6299$** | Order 1: $8.5 / 9.3928 \approx 0.9049$. Order 2: $5.9165 / 9.3928 \approx 0.6299$. |

def compute_ranking_metrics(grades, k=3, total_relevant=3):
    binary = [1 if g >= 1 else 0 for g in grades]
    
    # 1. Precision@k
    p_k = sum(binary[:k]) / k
    
    # 2. Recall@k
    r_k = sum(binary[:k]) / total_relevant if total_relevant > 0 else 0.0
    
    # 3. Hit Rate@k
    hr_k = 1.0 if sum(binary[:k]) > 0 else 0.0
    
    # 4. Reciprocal Rank (MRR for a single query)
    rr = 0.0
    for idx, b in enumerate(binary):
        if b > 0:
            rr = 1.0 / (idx + 1)
            break
            
    # 5. Average Precision @ k (AP@k) normalized by total_relevant
    hits = 0
    prec_sum = 0.0
    for idx in range(min(k, len(binary))):
        if binary[idx] > 0:
            hits += 1
            prec_sum += hits / (idx + 1)
    ap_k = prec_sum / total_relevant if total_relevant > 0 else 0.0
    
    # 6. Exponential DCG@k and NDCG@k
    def calc_dcg(g_list, cutoff):
        val = 0.0
        for i in range(min(cutoff, len(g_list))):
            val += (2.0**g_list[i] - 1.0) / math.log2(i + 2)
        return val
        
    dcg_k = calc_dcg(grades, k)
    ideal_grades = sorted(grades, reverse=True)
    idcg_k = calc_dcg(ideal_grades, k)
    ndcg_k = dcg_k / idcg_k if idcg_k > 0 else 0.0
    
    return {
        "P@k": round(p_k, 4),
        "R@k": round(r_k, 4),
        "HitRate@k": round(hr_k, 2),
        "MRR": round(rr, 4),
        "AP@k": round(ap_k, 4),
        "DCG@k": round(dcg_k, 4),
        "NDCG@k": round(ndcg_k, 4),
    }

order_1 = [3, 0, 2, 1, 0]  # [Doc A, Doc D, Doc B, Doc C, Doc E]
order_2 = [0, 3, 2, 1, 0]  # [Doc D, Doc A, Doc B, Doc C, Doc E]

## Recommendation-Specific Metrics: Beyond Relevance

In recommendation systems, optimizing purely for accuracy or NDCG produces a well-known failure mode: **the popularity trap**. The model repeatedly recommends the same top blockbuster movies or viral products to every user, achieving high click accuracy while destroying catalog discovery.

Production recommenders evaluate operational proxy metrics alongside NDCG:

### 1. Catalog Coverage (System-Level Metric)
Catalog coverage measures the fraction of unique items in the catalog that are recommended to at least one user across the entire platform over an evaluation window:
$$
\text{CatalogCoverage} = \frac{|\bigcup_{u \in U} R_u|}{|\mathcal{C}|}
$$

Where $R_u$ is the recommended slate for user $u$, and $\mathcal{C}$ is the total catalog. Low coverage indicates that the algorithm ignores the long tail of valuable inventory. (Note: in a single-query interactive calculator, measuring unique items in a single slate against a small corpus is only an illustrative micro-proxy).

### 2. Category-Diversity Proxy
Users dislike slates where all recommendations belong to the exact same narrow subcategory. A straightforward operational proxy measures the fraction of distinct categories present in the top-$k$ slate:
$$
\text{CategoryDiversityProxy}(R_u) = \frac{|\{c(d) \mid d \in R_u\}|}{k}
$$

Pairwise distance formulations can also measure intra-list diversity over item embeddings across all pairs in the slate.

### 3. Novelty Proxy
Novelty measures how non-obvious recommendations are relative to platform popularity. A standard operational proxy uses negative log probability (self-information):
$$
\text{NoveltyProxy}(R_u) = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{i \in R_u} -\log_2(P(i))
$$

Where $P(i)$ is the item's historical interaction share. Highly popular items have high $P(i)$ and low novelty; long-tail items have low $P(i)$ and high novelty bits.

### 4. Unvisited-Category Ratio vs. True Serendipity
A common exploration heuristic measures the fraction of recommended items from categories outside the user's historical interaction profile:
$$
\text{UnvisitedCategoryRatio}(R_u) = \frac{|\{d \in R_u \mid \text{category}(d) \notin \text{History}(u)\}|}{k}
$$

It is critical to distinguish an **unvisited-category ratio** from **true serendipity**:
- *Unvisited-Category Ratio* merely measures whether an item comes from an unfamiliar topic or genre. An irrelevant, unhelpful item from an unvisited category increases this ratio, but degrades user satisfaction.
- *True Serendipity* requires **both** unexpectedness (novelty relative to user history) **and** demonstrated user satisfaction (held-out relevance or positive engagement). Recommending unvisited categories without relevance checks produces random exploration rather than serendipity.

For offline top-$N$ recommendation evaluation formulas, see [Recommender Systems: Evaluating Offline](/docs/machine-learning/recommender-systems#7-evaluating-a-recommender-offline).

## Offline Evaluation vs. Online A/B Testing & Interleaving

An algorithm that achieves superior NDCG on a frozen test dataset does not automatically drive user engagement in production. **Offline test-set results do not establish online lift.**

### Offline Evaluation
- **How it works**: Evaluates candidate models against historical interaction logs or human-curated editorial relevance labels.
- **Advantages**: Fast, inexpensive, completely reproducible, and poses zero risk to live user experience.
- **Failure Modes**: Suffers from **position bias** (historical users clicked item #1 because it was displayed at the top, not because it was inherently better) and **selection bias** (we have zero logged labels for catalog items that previous systems never retrieved).

### Online A/B Testing
- **How it works**: Routes live traffic across model variants (such as an illustrative 50/50 Control vs. Treatment split), observing real business outcomes (conversion rate, retention, revenue, and session dwell time).
- **Primary Outcomes vs. Guardrails**: A healthy experiment designates one primary metric (such as session completion rate) alongside critical guardrail metrics (such as page load latency, unsubscribe rates, and return-request volume).

<NotebookCallout color="amber" title="The CTR Goodhart Trap">
Never use raw Click-Through Rate (CTR) as an unconstrained primary ranking metric. Ranking clickbait titles or sensationalist thumbnails inflates CTR in initial hours, but drives down session duration and accelerates long-term user churn.
</NotebookCallout>

### Interleaving
Running an independent-arm A/B test with statistical significance often requires extensive traffic. **Team Draft Interleaving** blends the ranked outputs of Model A and Model B into a single merged result list presented to the user:
1. Model A picks its top candidate (placed at slot 1).
2. Model B picks its top candidate not yet in the list (placed at slot 2).
3. If the user clicks slot 2, Model B is awarded a preference point.

By presenting both models' candidates within the same user session, interleaving controls for inter-user variance, achieving comparable statistical sensitivity with significantly fewer queries than traditional independent-arm A/B tests (Chapelle et al., 2012).

## When Not to Use Complex Ranking Metrics

| Situation | Recommended Metric | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Navigational & lookup queries** (`"account settings"`, `"login"`) | Reciprocal Rank (MRR) | Only the single target destination matters; graded NDCG over 10 slots adds noise. |
| **Unsorted discovery feeds** (infinite photo grid, casual social browsing) | Catalog Coverage & Dwell Time | Strict rank order matters less than discovery breadth and prolonged engagement. |
| **Regulatory compliance & e-discovery** (legal subpoena data extraction) | Recall@$k$ with high cutoff | Missing a single relevant document carries legal liability; top-1 position discounting is irrelevant. |
| **Small candidate pools with identical grades** | Precision@$k$ or Hit Rate | Graded metrics collapse to binary precision when all positive matches carry equal utility. |

## Remember

| Metric | Form | Sensitivity | Typical Use Case |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Precision@k** | $(1/k)\sum_{i=1}^k r_i$ | Unranked set inside cutoff | Quick sanity check on candidate quality. |
| **Recall@k** | $(1/R_{\text{total}})\sum_{i=1}^k r_i$ | Unranked set against corpus | Retrieval candidate generation evaluation. |
| **Hit Rate@k** | $\mathbb{I}(\sum_{i=1}^k r_i > 0)$ | Binary success in top-$k$ | Navigational search, single-item lookup. |
| **MRR** | $(1/\vert Q \vert)\sum_{q} (1/\text{rank}_1)$ | First relevant result only | Navigational queries, target entity extraction. |
| **MAP** | Mean of $\text{AP@}k$ | All relevant positions normalized by $R_{\text{total}}$ | Multi-relevant ad-hoc information retrieval. |
| **NDCG@k** | $\text{DCG@}k / \text{IDCG@}k$ | Graded relevance + log position discount | Production web search, e-commerce, LTR. |
| **Interleaving** | Shared blended slate | Relative pairwise preference | Sensitive online model comparison with reduced traffic. |
