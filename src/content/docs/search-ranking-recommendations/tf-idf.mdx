# TF-IDF from First Principles

<ELI5>
Imagine reading four short notes on a refrigerator. If you are looking for information on "machine learning", you want to find notes that mention both words. But not all words are equally informative: common words like "the" or "is" tell you nothing about the topic, while specific words like "machine" or "learning" carry the actual subject matter. TF-IDF does two simple calculations: it rewards a note for mentioning your search words frequently (Term Frequency), but discounts words that appear everywhere across all notes (Inverse Document Frequency).
</ELI5>

## Beyond Binary Boolean Matching

In the previous chapter, we built an [Inverted Index](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/text-search-bm25) to evaluate Boolean queries. While Boolean matching filters documents with sub-millisecond latency, it suffers from a fatal limitation: it cannot rank. If a query matches ten thousand documents, Boolean retrieval treats all ten thousand matches as identically relevant.

To rank search results from most relevant to least relevant, information retrieval systems require a continuous scoring function. The foundational algorithm of modern text ranking is **TF-IDF** (**Term Frequency &ndash; Inverse Document Frequency**), formalized by Karen Spärck Jones (1972) and Gerard Salton (1988).

TF-IDF evaluates relevance by balancing two core intuitions:
1. **Term Frequency ($\text{TF}$)**: A document that mentions a query term multiple times is generally more relevant than one mentioning it once.
2. **Inverse Document Frequency ($\text{IDF}$)**: A term that appears in almost every document in the corpus is uninformative for distinguishing relevance; rare, highly specific terms carry much higher discriminative evidence.

## A Worked Example: The 4-Document Toy Corpus

To understand TF-IDF from first principles, consider an illustrative toy corpus of $N = 4$ documents:

- **Document 1 (D1)**: *"machine learning is powerful"* (Length $|D_1| = 4$ words)
- **Document 2 (D2)**: *"deep learning is part of machine learning"* (Length $|D_2| = 7$ words)
- **Document 3 (D3)**: *"machine learning improves search"* (Length $|D_3| = 4$ words)
- **Document 4 (D4)**: *"cooking food is fun"* (Length $|D_4| = 4$ words)

**Target User Query**: `"machine learning"` (query terms: $q_1 = \text{"machine"}$, $q_2 = \text{"learning"}$).

Our objective is to calculate the exact TF-IDF score for each document and rank them in order of relevance.

---

### Step 1: Term Frequency (TF)

Term Frequency measures how frequently a query word occurs inside a given document. In its normalized formulation, raw count is scaled by the total word count of the document to prevent long documents from gaining an unfair advantage:

$$
\text{TF}(t, d) = \frac{\text{Count of term } t \text{ in } d}{\text{Total words in } d}
$$

Let us compute the exact term frequencies for both query terms:

| Document | Total Words | Count(`"machine"`) | $\text{TF}(\text{"machine"})$ | Count(`"learning"`) | $\text{TF}(\text{"learning"})$ | Total $\text{TF}$ Sum |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **D1** (`"machine learning is powerful"`) | 4 | 1 | $1 / 4 = 0.2500$ | 1 | $1 / 4 = 0.2500$ | $0.5000$ |
| **D2** (`"deep learning is part of machine learning"`) | 7 | 1 | $1 / 7 \approx 0.1429$ | 2 | $2 / 7 \approx 0.2857$ | $3 / 7 \approx 0.4286$ |
| **D3** (`"machine learning improves search"`) | 4 | 1 | $1 / 4 = 0.2500$ | 1 | $1 / 4 = 0.2500$ | $0.5000$ |
| **D4** (`"cooking food is fun"`) | 4 | 0 | $0 / 4 = 0.0000$ | 0 | $0 / 4 = 0.0000$ | $0.0000$ |

<NotebookCallout color="amber" title="Word Count Precision">
Notice that D2 mentions `"learning"` twice and `"machine"` once (3 query words in total). However, because D2 has 7 words, its normalized term frequency for `"machine"` is only $1/7 \approx 0.1429$, compared to $1/4 = 0.2500$ in D1 and D3.
</NotebookCallout>

---

### Step 2: Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

Term Frequency alone is insufficient: if a query contains both a common word and a rare word, TF treats both equally. **Inverse Document Frequency** measures how rare a term is across the entire catalog of $N$ documents:

$$
\text{IDF}(t) = \ln\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right)
$$

Where:
- $N$: Total number of documents in the corpus ($N = 4$).
- $\text{df}(t)$: Document frequency—the number of distinct documents containing term $t$.
- $\ln$: Natural logarithm.

Let us compute the IDF for each vocabulary term:
- **`"machine"`**: Appears in D1, D2, and D3 $\implies \text{df} = 3$.
  $$
  \text{IDF}(\text{"machine"}) = \ln\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) \approx 0.287682 \dots \approx 0.2877
  $$
- **`"learning"`**: Appears in D1, D2, and D3 $\implies \text{df} = 3$.
  $$
  \text{IDF}(\text{"learning"}) = \ln\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) \approx 0.287682 \dots \approx 0.2877
  $$

Now contrast this with a rare word like `"cooking"`, which appears in only 1 document (D4):
$$
\text{IDF}(\text{"cooking"}) = \ln\left(\frac{4}{1}\right) = \ln(4) \approx 1.3863
$$

Because `"cooking"` is rare, its IDF is nearly five times higher than that of `"machine"` ($1.3863$ vs $0.2877$). Rare terms receive higher scores because their presence is stronger evidence of specific topical relevance.

---

### Step 3: Computing TF-IDF and Query Scores

The TF-IDF weight for a single term in a document is simply:

$$
\text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t)
$$

To score a multi-term search query $q$, we sum the TF-IDF weights of all query terms:

$$
\text{Score}(d, q) = \sum_{t \in q} \text{TF-IDF}(t, d) = \sum_{t \in q} \left[\text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t)\right]
$$

Let us compute the step-by-step scores for each document on query `"machine learning"`:

#### Document 1: Length $|D_1| = 4$
- $\text{TF-IDF}(\text{"machine"}, D_1) = 0.2500 \times \ln(4/3) \approx 0.2500 \times 0.287682 \approx 0.07192$
- $\text{TF-IDF}(\text{"learning"}, D_1) = 0.2500 \times \ln(4/3) \approx 0.2500 \times 0.287682 \approx 0.07192$
- **Total Score ($D_1$)**: $0.07192 + 0.07192 = 0.5000 \times \ln(4/3) \approx \mathbf{0.1438}$ (rounded to 4 decimal places).

#### Document 3: Length $|D_3| = 4$
- $\text{TF-IDF}(\text{"machine"}, D_3) = 0.2500 \times \ln(4/3) \approx 0.07192$
- $\text{TF-IDF}(\text{"learning"}, D_3) = 0.2500 \times \ln(4/3) \approx 0.07192$
- **Total Score ($D_3$)**: $0.07192 + 0.07192 = 0.5000 \times \ln(4/3) \approx \mathbf{0.1438}$.

#### Document 2: Length $|D_2| = 7$
- $\text{TF-IDF}(\text{"machine"}, D_2) = (1/7) \times \ln(4/3) \approx 0.142857 \times 0.287682 \approx 0.04110$
- $\text{TF-IDF}(\text{"learning"}, D_2) = (2/7) \times \ln(4/3) \approx 0.285714 \times 0.287682 \approx 0.08220$
- **Total Score ($D_2$)**: $0.04110 + 0.08220 = (3/7) \times \ln(4/3) \approx \mathbf{0.1233}$.

#### Document 4: Length $|D_4| = 4$
- Neither `"machine"` nor `"learning"` appears in D4 $\implies \mathbf{0.0000}$.

### Final Ranking Summary Table

| Rank | Document ID | Document Text | TF(`"machine"`) | TF(`"learning"`) | Total TF Sum | Exact TF-IDF Score |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **#1 (Tie)** | **D1** | `"machine learning is powerful"` | $0.2500$ | $0.2500$ | $0.5000$ | $\mathbf{0.1438}$ |
| **#1 (Tie)** | **D3** | `"machine learning improves search"` | $0.2500$ | $0.2500$ | $0.5000$ | $\mathbf{0.1438}$ |
| **#3** | **D2** | `"deep learning is part of machine learning"` | $0.1429$ | $0.2857$ | $0.4286$ | $\mathbf{0.1233}$ |
| **#4** | **D4** | `"cooking food is fun"` | $0.0000$ | $0.0000$ | $0.0000$ | $\mathbf{0.0000}$ |

<NotebookCallout color="cobalt" title="Explaining the Mathematical Tie: D1 vs. D3">
Notice that D1 and D3 tie at **0.1438**. This tie is **not a rounding accident**. Both documents have an identical total length of 4 words, and each contains exactly 1 occurrence of `"machine"` and 1 occurrence of `"learning"`. Because both query terms share the same document frequency ($\text{df} = 3$), their score contributions are identical:
$$
\text{Score}(D_1) = \text{Score}(D_3) = \left(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{4}\right) \times \ln\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{2} \ln\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) \approx 0.143841 \dots
$$
Observe also that D2 contains **3 query words** (one `"machine"`, two `"learning"`), whereas D1 and D3 contain only 2 query words. Despite having more keyword matches, D2 ranks lower ($0.1233$) because its length of 7 words dilutes its normalized term frequencies ($3/7 \approx 0.4286 < 0.5000$).
</NotebookCallout>

## The Vector Space Model & Sparse Retrieval

Instead of computing one scalar score at a time, we can view words as coordinate directions in a geometric space. In a simple two-word vocabulary with "machine" on the horizontal axis and "learning" on the vertical axis, every document forms a directional arrow pointing out from the origin $(0, 0)$.

<VectorSpaceArrowsDiagram />

In this shared coordinate space, the direction of each arrow reveals its topical balance. Notice that D1 and D3 point along the exact same $45^\circ$ diagonal: both documents dedicate an equal 25% of their total words to "machine" and "learning". D2 tilts higher toward the vertical axis because it mentions "learning" twice as often as "machine". D4 sits directly at the origin because neither query word appears in it.

Each coordinate in a document's vector corresponds to one specific word from the entire vocabulary $V$:

$$
\mathbf{d} = \big[ \text{TF-IDF}(t_1, d),\, \text{TF-IDF}(t_2, d),\, \dots,\, \text{TF-IDF}(t_{|V|}, d) \big]^T
$$

$$
\mathbf{q} = \big[ \text{TF-IDF}(t_1, q),\, \text{TF-IDF}(t_2, q),\, \dots,\, \text{TF-IDF}(t_{|V|}, q) \big]^T
$$

Because any single document contains only a small handful of words out of the entire dictionary, coordinates for absent words are exactly zero. We visualize this structure as a row of boxes below:

<SparseVectorBoxesDiagram />

This is why TF-IDF is called **sparse retrieval**: the vector has one coordinate for every word in the dictionary, but every word absent from the document has a zero weight. Only words that appear in the document can hold positive weights.

### Measuring Relevance via Cosine Similarity

When comparing two arrows in vector space, should we measure the straight-line distance between their tips, or the angle between them?

If Document B repeats the exact text of Document A twice in a row, its arrow points in the identical direction but stretches twice as far. Measuring straight-line distance would falsely report them as dissimilar. Measuring the **cosine of the angle $\theta$** between their arrows resolves this:

<CosineAngleDiagram />

$$
\text{CosineSimilarity}(\mathbf{q}, \mathbf{d}) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{q}\|_2 \|\mathbf{d}\|_2} = \frac{\sum_{t \in q \cap d} \text{TF-IDF}(t, q) \cdot \text{TF-IDF}(t, d)}{\sqrt{\sum_{t \in q} \text{TF-IDF}(t, q)^2} \cdot \sqrt{\sum_{t \in d} \text{TF-IDF}(t, d)^2}}
$$

When two arrows point in the identical direction ($\theta = 0^\circ$), $\cos(0^\circ) = 1.0$, indicating maximum possible similarity. When two arrows are perpendicular ($\theta = 90^\circ$), $\cos(90^\circ) = 0.0$, indicating zero shared words.

<GoDeeper title="Theory & History: Vector Space Model & Production Variants">
The vector space formulation was introduced by Gerard Salton and colleagues (1975) to represent text geometrically. Dividing by the Euclidean norm $\|\mathbf{d}\|_2$ projects every document arrow onto the surface of a unit sphere, decoupling topical direction from document length.

In production libraries like Python's `scikit-learn` (`TfidfVectorizer`), three standard adjustments are applied:
1. **Smoothed IDF**: Adds a constant 1 to avoid division by zero when a term never appears:
   $$
   \text{IDF}_{\text{sklearn}}(t) = \ln\left(\frac{1 + N}{1 + \text{df}(t)}\right) + 1
   $$
2. **Sublinear Term Frequency**: Replaces raw counts with logarithmic dampening ($1 + \ln(\text{count})$) so ten word repetitions do not outweigh five distinct topic matches.
3. **L2 Unit Normalization**: Ensures every vector has length 1: $\mathbf{v}_{\text{norm}} = \mathbf{v} / \|\mathbf{v}\|_2$.
</GoDeeper>

## Limitations: The Semantic Vocabulary Mismatch Problem

The greatest limitation of TF-IDF is that it counts character strings rather than meanings. It has zero understanding of synonyms or intent.

Consider a reader searching for help with their car:

<VocabularyMismatchDiagram />

Even though *"how to fix a car"* directly answers *"automobile repair"*, not a single word overlaps:
- *"automobile"* $\ne$ *"car"*
- *"repair"* $\ne$ *"fix"*

Because the intersection of character tokens is empty ($q \cap d = \emptyset$), the TF-IDF score is strictly zero:

$$
\text{Score}(\text{"automobile repair"}, \text{"how to fix a car"}) = 0.0000
$$

This failure is known as the **vocabulary mismatch problem** (Furnas et al., 1987). In [Semantic & Hybrid Search](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/semantic-and-hybrid-search), we solve this by using neural embedding models that map synonyms into nearby points in continuous space.

## Run It Yourself: Pure Python TF-IDF from Scratch

Run the complete TF-IDF search engine below. The code implements the exact normalized TF and natural-log IDF formulas, matching our worked mathematical derivation to four decimal places.

<RunnableCode code={`import math

# The 4-document illustrative toy corpus
corpus = [
    {"id": "D1", "text": "machine learning is powerful"},
    {"id": "D2", "text": "deep learning is part of machine learning"},
    {"id": "D3", "text": "machine learning improves search"},
    {"id": "D4", "text": "cooking food is fun"},
]

def tokenize(text):
    return text.lower().split()

def compute_tfidf(corpus, query):
    N = len(corpus)
    tokenized_docs = [tokenize(doc["text"]) for doc in corpus]
    query_terms = tokenize(query)
    
    # 1. Compute document frequency (df) and IDF for query terms
    idf = {}
    print("--- Step A: Query Term IDF ---")
    for term in query_terms:
        df = sum(1 for d in tokenized_docs if term in d)
        idf[term] = math.log(N / df) if df > 0 else 0.0
        print(f"Term '{term}': df={df}/{N}, IDF={idf[term]:.4f}")
        
    # 2. Score documents: sum of (TF * IDF)
    print("\\n--- Step B: Document Scores & Ranking ---")
    results = []
    for doc, tokens in zip(corpus, tokenized_docs):
        doc_len = len(tokens)
        score = 0.0
        breakdown = []
        for term in query_terms:
            count = tokens.count(term)
            tf = count / doc_len if doc_len > 0 else 0.0
            term_score = tf * idf.get(term, 0.0)
            score += term_score
            breakdown.append(f"{term}: {count}/{doc_len} ({tf:.3f}) * {idf[term]:.3f} = {term_score:.4f}")
            
        results.append((doc["id"], score, doc["text"], breakdown))
        
    # Sort descending by score; if tied, sort by doc ID
    results.sort(key=lambda x: (-round(x[1], 4), x[0]))
    for rank, (doc_id, score, text, bd) in enumerate(results, 1):
        print(f"#{rank} {doc_id} -> Score: {score:.4f} | '{text}'")
        print(f"     Breakdown: {' | '.join(bd)}")

compute_tfidf(corpus, "machine learning")
`} />

## Vector Space Cosine Similarity in Pure Python

To understand how modern search libraries evaluate sparse vectors, inspect the cosine similarity implementation below:

<RunnableCode code={`import math
from collections import Counter

def vectorize(text, vocabulary, idf):
    tokens = text.lower().split()
    counts = Counter(tokens)
    length = len(tokens)
    vec = {}
    for term in vocabulary:
        if term in counts:
            tf = counts[term] / length
            vec[term] = tf * idf[term]
        else:
            vec[term] = 0.0
    return vec

def cosine_similarity(v1, v2):
    dot_product = sum(v1[t] * v2[t] for t in v1)
    norm1 = math.sqrt(sum(v1[t] ** 2 for t in v1))
    norm2 = math.sqrt(sum(v2[t] ** 2 for t in v2))
    if norm1 == 0 or norm2 == 0:
        return 0.0
    return dot_product / (norm1 * norm2)

corpus = [
    "machine learning is powerful",
    "deep learning is part of machine learning",
    "machine learning improves search",
    "cooking food is fun"
]
query = "machine learning"

# Build shared vocabulary and IDF
all_tokens = [doc.lower().split() for doc in corpus]
vocab = sorted(list(set(t for d in all_tokens for t in d)))
N = len(corpus)
idf = {t: math.log(N / sum(1 for d in all_tokens if t in d)) for t in vocab}

q_vec = vectorize(query, vocab, idf)
print("Cosine Similarity Scores against Query:")
for i, doc in enumerate(corpus, 1):
    d_vec = vectorize(doc, vocab, idf)
    sim = cosine_similarity(q_vec, d_vec)
    print(f"  D{i}: {sim:.4f} | '{doc}'")
`} />

## When Not to Use TF-IDF

| Scenario | Recommended Alternative | Core Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Production keyword search at scale** | BM25 (Best Matching 25) | Linear TF allows repeated keywords to inflate relevance; lack of saturation invites keyword stuffing. |
| **Synonym and conceptual search** (`"car fix"` $\to$ `"automobile repair"`) | Dense neural embeddings (Bi-encoder / DPR) | TF-IDF requires exact token overlap; vocabulary mismatch yields zero matching score. |
| **Multi-field structured search** (title vs. body vs. tags) | BM25F or Field-Weighted Search | TF-IDF cannot weight title matches higher than body matches without ad-hoc heuristic scaling. |
| **Short, query-like documents** (FAQ titles, catalog SKU lookups) | Exact prefix indexing or n-gram matching | Normalizing by length overly penalizes or distorts short titles with high variance. |

## Remember

| Concept | Mathematical Form | Systems Intuition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Term Frequency (TF)** | $\text{count}(t, d) / \vert d \vert$ | Measures local word density; prevents long documents from dominating purely on volume. |
| **Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)** | $\ln(N / \text{df}(t))$ | Measures global specificity; rewards rare terms and discounts ubiquitous words. |
| **TF-IDF Weight** | $\text{TF}(t, d) \times \text{IDF}(t)$ | Combines local frequency with corpus-wide discriminative power. |
| **Sparse Vector Space** | $\mathbf{d} \in \mathbb{R}^{\vert V \vert}$ | Documents represented as high-dimensional vectors with coordinates equal to zero for all absent vocabulary terms. |
| **Vocabulary Mismatch** | $q \cap d = \emptyset \implies \text{Score} = 0$ | The inability of lexical search to recognize semantic synonyms without dense vectors. |
