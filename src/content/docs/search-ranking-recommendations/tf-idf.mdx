# TF-IDF

<ELI5>
Four notes are stuck on a fridge, and you want the ones about "machine learning". A note that says those words often is probably about them. But some words, like "is" or "the", are on every note, so they tell you nothing. TF-IDF gives each note a score: more points for using your words often, and more points when those words are rare across all the notes.
</ELI5>

## Why it exists

The [inverted index](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/text-search-bm25) from the last chapter finds every document that contains your words, fast. But it cannot say which match is best. If ten thousand documents mention "machine learning", they all look equally good.

To put results in order we need a **score** for each document. TF-IDF is the first score most search engines learn, and its two ideas are still inside the newer ones like BM25.

| **D1** | "machine learning is powerful" | 4 |
| **D2** | "deep learning is part of machine learning" | 7 |

| **D4** | "cooking food is fun" | 4 |

The search is **"machine learning"**.

## Term frequency

A document that uses your word more often is probably more about it. But a long document uses every word more often just because it is long. So we divide the count by the document's length:

$$
\text{TF}(t, d) = \frac{\text{count of } t \text{ in } d}{\text{words in } d}
$$

where:
- *t* is one search word, *d* is one document;
- the top counts how many times *t* appears in *d*;
- the bottom is how many words *d* has.

| Document | "machine" | TF("machine") | "learning" | TF("learning") |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **D1** | 1 of 4 | 0.2500 | 1 of 4 | 0.2500 |
| **D2** | 1 of 7 | 0.1429 | 2 of 7 | 0.2857 |
| **D3** | 1 of 4 | 0.2500 | 1 of 4 | 0.2500 |
| **D4** | 0 of 4 | 0 | 0 of 4 | 0 |

<NotebookCallout color="amber" title="Longer is not better">
D2 says our words three times, more than D1 or D3. But it has 7 words, so each mention counts for less: "machine" is 1/7 = 0.1429 of D2, but 1/4 = 0.2500 of D1.
</NotebookCallout>

## Inverse document frequency

TF treats every word the same. But a word that is in almost every document cannot tell documents apart. IDF gives rare words more weight:

$$
\text{IDF}(t) = \ln\left(\frac{N}{\text{df}(t)}\right)
$$

where:
- *N* is the number of documents (here 4);
- df(*t*) is how many documents contain *t* at least once;
- ln is the natural log, which keeps very rare words from getting huge weights.

"machine" and "learning" are each in 3 of the 4 documents, so both get ln(4/3) ≈ **0.2877**.

"cooking" is in only 1 document, so it gets ln(4/1) ≈ **1.3863**, almost five times more. If someone searched "cooking", that word would count for a lot.

## Documents as arrows

There is a second way to see the same numbers. Give each word its own direction. With two search words, "machine" points right and "learning" points up, and each document becomes an arrow.

<VectorSpaceArrowsDiagram />

D1 and D3 point the same way: each spends a quarter of its words on "machine" and a quarter on "learning". D2 leans up, because it says "learning" twice. D4 is just a dot at the start, because it has neither word.

A real dictionary has thousands of words, so a real document's arrow has thousands of numbers. Almost all of them are zero, because a document uses only a few of the dictionary's words:

<SparseVectorBoxesDiagram />

This is why TF-IDF is called **sparse** search: we only store the few numbers that are not zero.

## Cosine similarity

Once documents are arrows, "how similar are they?" becomes "how close are their directions?". We measure the angle between two arrows, not the distance between their tips.

<CosineAngleDiagram />

/>

$$
\cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\lVert \mathbf{q} \rVert \, \lVert \mathbf{d} \rVert}
$$

where:
- **q** is the query's arrow, **d** is the document's arrow;
- **q** · **d** multiplies matching numbers and adds them up;
- ‖**q**‖ and ‖**d**‖ are the arrows' lengths, so dividing by them ignores length.

Same direction gives cos 0° = 1. No shared words gives cos 90° = 0.

<GoDeeper title="Go deeper: where TF-IDF came from, and what libraries change">
Karen Spärck Jones introduced the IDF idea in 1972. Gerard Salton and colleagues described documents as vectors in 1975, and Salton and Buckley compared many TF-IDF variants in 1988.

Libraries such as scikit-learn's `TfidfVectorizer` change three details by default:
1. **Smoothed IDF**, so a word that appears nowhere does not divide by zero: ln((1 + N) / (1 + df)) + 1.
2. **Optional log TF**, 1 + ln(count), so ten repeats do not count ten times as much.
3. **Length-1 arrows**: each vector is divided by its length, so cosine becomes a plain dot product.
</GoDeeper>

## Worked example

Now we put the pieces together for "machine learning". Each document's score is the sum over the two words of TF × IDF, and both words have IDF ≈ 0.2877.

| Rank | Document | TF("machine") + TF("learning") | × IDF | Score |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 (tie) | **D1** | 0.2500 + 0.2500 = 0.5000 | × 0.2877 | **0.1438** |
| 1 (tie) | **D3** | 0.2500 + 0.2500 = 0.5000 | × 0.2877 | **0.1438** |
| 3 | **D2** | 0.1429 + 0.2857 = 0.4286 | × 0.2877 | **0.1233** |
| 4 | **D4** | 0 + 0 = 0 | × 0.2877 | **0** |

<NotebookCallout color="cobalt" title="Why D1 and D3 tie">
The tie is exact, not rounding. Both have 4 words and use each search word once, so both score (1/4 + 1/4) × ln(4/3) ≈ 0.1438. D2 uses our words more often but is longer, so its share, 3/7 ≈ 0.4286, is smaller than 0.5000.
</NotebookCallout>

## Try it yourself

This runs the exact steps above and prints the same scores:

<RunnableCode code={`import math

# The 4-document illustrative toy corpus
corpus = [
    {"id": "D1", "text": "machine learning is powerful"},
    {"id": "D2", "text": "deep learning is part of machine learning"},
    {"id": "D3", "text": "machine learning improves search"},
    {"id": "D4", "text": "cooking food is fun"},
]

def tokenize(text):
    return text.lower().split()

def compute_tfidf(corpus, query):
    N = len(corpus)
    tokenized_docs = [tokenize(doc["text"]) for doc in corpus]
    query_terms = tokenize(query)
    
    # 1. Compute document frequency (df) and IDF for query terms
    idf = {}
    print("--- Step A: Query Term IDF ---")
    for term in query_terms:
        df = sum(1 for d in tokenized_docs if term in d)
        idf[term] = math.log(N / df) if df > 0 else 0.0
        print(f"Term '{term}': df={df}/{N}, IDF={idf[term]:.4f}")
        
    # 2. Score documents: sum of (TF * IDF)
    print("\\n--- Step B: Document Scores & Ranking ---")
    results = []
    for doc, tokens in zip(corpus, tokenized_docs):
        doc_len = len(tokens)
        score = 0.0
        breakdown = []
        for term in query_terms:
            count = tokens.count(term)
            tf = count / doc_len if doc_len > 0 else 0.0
            term_score = tf * idf.get(term, 0.0)
            score += term_score
            breakdown.append(f"{term}: {count}/{doc_len} ({tf:.3f}) * {idf[term]:.3f} = {term_score:.4f}")
            
        results.append((doc["id"], score, doc["text"], breakdown))
        
    # Sort descending by score; if tied, sort by doc ID
    results.sort(key=lambda x: (-round(x[1], 4), x[0]))
    for rank, (doc_id, score, text, bd) in enumerate(results, 1):
        print(f"#{rank} {doc_id} -> Score: {score:.4f} | '{text}'")
        print(f"     Breakdown: {' | '.join(bd)}")

compute_tfidf(corpus, "machine learning")

# Try it: change the query to "learning". D2 now beats D1, because it says "learning" twice.
`} />

And the cosine version, which compares directions instead of adding scores:

<RunnableCode code={`import math
from collections import Counter

def vectorize(text, vocabulary, idf):
    tokens = text.lower().split()
    counts = Counter(tokens)
    length = len(tokens)
    vec = {}
    for term in vocabulary:
        if term in counts:
            tf = counts[term] / length
            vec[term] = tf * idf[term]
        else:
            vec[term] = 0.0
    return vec

def cosine_similarity(v1, v2):
    dot_product = sum(v1[t] * v2[t] for t in v1)
    norm1 = math.sqrt(sum(v1[t] ** 2 for t in v1))
    norm2 = math.sqrt(sum(v2[t] ** 2 for t in v2))
    if norm1 == 0 or norm2 == 0:
        return 0.0
    return dot_product / (norm1 * norm2)

corpus = [
    "machine learning is powerful",
    "deep learning is part of machine learning",
    "machine learning improves search",
    "cooking food is fun"
]
query = "machine learning"

# Build shared vocabulary and IDF
all_tokens = [doc.lower().split() for doc in corpus]
vocab = sorted(list(set(t for d in all_tokens for t in d)))
N = len(corpus)
idf = {t: math.log(N / sum(1 for d in all_tokens if t in d)) for t in vocab}

q_vec = vectorize(query, vocab, idf)
print("Cosine Similarity Scores against Query:")
for i, doc in enumerate(corpus, 1):
    d_vec = vectorize(doc, vocab, idf)
    sim = cosine_similarity(q_vec, d_vec)
    print(f"  D{i}: {sim:.4f} | '{doc}'")

# Try it: add "machine learning machine learning" to the corpus.
# Its cosine score equals the original's: repeating a text does not change its direction.
`} />

## Common mistakes

| Mistake | Why it happens | Fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Searching "automobile repair" finds nothing about "how to fix a car" | TF-IDF matches exact words, and these share none | Add meaning-based search with [embeddings](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/semantic-and-hybrid-search) |
| A page that repeats a keyword 50 times wins | TF keeps growing with every repeat | Use [BM25](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/bm25), which flattens repeats |
| Long documents win too often | Raw counts are used without dividing by length | Divide by length (TF), or normalise the arrows to length 1 |
| "the" and "is" seem to matter | They are in the counts, and IDF is not applied | Apply IDF; it pushes words that are everywhere towards 0 |
| Scores change when one document is added | IDF depends on the whole collection | Expected; rebuild IDF when the collection changes a lot |

## When not to use it

| Situation | Better choice | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Keyword search at scale | BM25 | It stops repeated words from adding up forever |
| Synonyms and meaning ("car fix" vs "automobile repair") | Dense embeddings | TF-IDF needs exact shared words |
| Titles should count more than body text | BM25F, field weights | TF-IDF has no idea of fields |
| Very short texts (product codes, FAQ titles) | Exact or prefix matching | Length division is unstable on 2–3 words |

## Remember

| Idea | In one line |
| :--- | :--- |
| TF | How often the word is used here, divided by document length |
| IDF | ln(N / df): rare words count more, common words count almost nothing |
| Score | Add TF × IDF over the search words |
| Documents as arrows | One direction per word; most numbers are zero (sparse) |
| Cosine | Compares directions, so repeating a text does not change its score |
| Main weakness | Needs the same words; no synonyms |

Next: [BM25](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/bm25), which fixes the keyword-repeat problem with a score that levels off.
