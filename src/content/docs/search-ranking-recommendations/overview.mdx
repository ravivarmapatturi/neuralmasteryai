# Search & Recommendation Pipelines

<ELI5>
Imagine walking into a warehouse with ten million books. If you ask a librarian for books about space exploration, they do not read every single book in the warehouse while you wait at the desk. Instead, they first run to the astronomy aisle and grab fifty candidate books in thirty seconds. Next, they discard books that are checked out or damaged. Finally, they sit down, compare the remaining candidates carefully, and hand you the best five in order of relevance. Search and recommendation systems do the exact same thing in software.
</ELI5>

## Why Multi-Stage Funnels Exist

Search and recommendation engines face an identical fundamental engineering tension: catalog size versus scoring complexity. A modern platform contains vast catalogs—web pages, e-commerce products, short videos, or audio tracks—frequently scaling from millions to billions of items. Conversely, scoring a candidate item with a modern transformer or gradient-boosted decision tree requires computing dozens to hundreds of cross-features, comparing dense text embeddings, or evaluating deep neural networks.

If we evaluated millions of catalog items through a deep cross-attention model on every user request, computational demands would require millions of full neural forward passes, violating interactive response budgets by orders of magnitude. Conversely, if we relied exclusively on fast keyword matching or unranked nearest-neighbor vector scans, responses would arrive rapidly, but the ordering would fail to capture subtle user preferences, contextual affinities, and business constraints.

We resolve this tension by structuring the system as a multi-stage funnel. Early stages employ lightweight, sub-linear filtering and vector indexing to discard the vast majority of non-viable items, passing only a bounded candidate set to progressively richer, more computationally intensive scoring layers.

## The Multi-Stage Architecture

Large-scale search and recommendation platforms typically decompose request processing into a sequential funnel. While search begins with an explicit user query (such as `"wireless noise cancelling headphones"`) and recommendation begins from an implicit user or session context (such as past interaction history, time of day, and client device), both paradigms route items through five core funnel stages: Candidate Generation, Hard Filtering, Ranking, Re-Ranking, and Serving.

### 1. Candidate Generation (Retrieval)
In an illustrative production setup with millions of items, candidate generation reduces the search space down to several hundred or a few thousand candidates. The primary optimization objective is **high recall**: if a truly relevant item fails to make the candidate pool, downstream models cannot score or display it. Systems typically query complementary lightweight retrieval channels in parallel:
- **Lexical Retrieval**: Inverted indexes with [TF-IDF](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/tf-idf) and [BM25](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/bm25) score exact keyword matches, SKU lookups, and brand titles with sub-millisecond posting-list intersections (see [Text Search & Inverted Indexes](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/text-search-bm25)).
- **Dense Vector Retrieval**: Two-tower neural encoders project users and items into a shared embedding space, executing Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) search via HNSW or IVF indexes (see [Semantic & Hybrid Search](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/semantic-and-hybrid-search)).
- **Graph & Collaborative Filtering**: Item-to-item co-occurrence tables and user-interaction graphs (see [Recommendation Foundations](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/recommendation-foundations) and [Matrix Factorization & Latent Factor Embeddings](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/matrix-factorization-embeddings)).

### 2. Hard Filtering
The raw candidate pool frequently contains items that cannot be served to the requesting user. Hard filtering applies deterministic business logic and operational constraints before scoring models execute:
- Removing out-of-stock inventory or items outside the user's regional fulfillment boundary.
- Enforcing age restrictions, region licensing rights, or blocked merchant lists.
- Filtering out items the user has already purchased, consumed, or explicitly dismissed.

### 3. Ranking (Scoring)
The surviving candidates (often several hundred items in an illustrative setup) enter the ranking stage. Here, computational budgets permit expressive models that evaluate deep interaction features:
- Historical click-through rates (CTR) and conversion rates conditioned on user demographic segments and temporal contexts (see [Ranking Fundamentals & Link Analysis](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/ranking-fundamentals)).
- Cross-encoder transformer attention and gradient-boosted decision trees trained via pairwise and listwise losses (see [Learning to Rank](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/learning-to-rank)).
- Dense feature interactions through Deep Learning Recommendation Models (such as DLRM and Wide & Deep, covered in [Deep Recommenders](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/deep-recommenders)).

The ranking model outputs a continuous calibrated score for each item, representing predicted probability of engagement: $\hat{y} = P(\text{click}) \times P(\text{conversion} \mid \text{click})$. For offline evaluation of these rankings, see [Ranking Evaluation Metrics](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/ranking-evaluation-metrics).

### 4. Re-Ranking & Business Logic
The top-scoring candidates enter re-ranking, which balances raw predicted relevance against platform-level constraints:
- **Intra-List Diversity**: Preventing multiple consecutive results from the exact same merchant, brand, or creator.
- **Freshness & Exploration**: Injecting newly published items via contextual multi-armed bandits to learn their baseline engagement distribution without historical interaction logs (see [Exploration, Feedback Loops & Counterfactual Evaluation](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/exploration-feedback-loops)).
- **Fairness & Sponsored Allocation**: Blending paid partner listings into designated organic slots with explicit attribution and quality guards.

### 5. Serving & Presentation
The final top-$k$ results (typically 5 to 20 items in an illustrative response slate) are packaged into the API payload. The serving layer fetches dynamic pricing, localized thumbnail URLs, and generates cryptographic tracking tokens so client-side impressions and clicks can be joined back to offline training logs (see [Search & Recommendation System Design](/docs/search-ranking-recommendations/search-recommendation-system-design)).

### Comparing the Two Stages

- **Candidate Generation maximizes Recall over the whole catalog**: It asks, *"Did we capture the best items somewhere in this set of candidates?"* It evaluates decoupled representations (such as single-term index postings or dot products of precomputed embeddings) where per-item evaluation costs are minimal.
- **Ranking maximizes Order and Precision at the top**: It asks, *"Is item #1 better than item #2?"* It evaluates deep cross-features (user history crossed with item brand, real-time context, query-document term proximity) where scoring costs are higher per candidate.

<NotebookCallout color="amber" title="The Retriever Bottleneck Invariant">
No ranking algorithm can surface an item dropped during candidate generation. If candidate generation retrieves candidates with 85% recall, the overall system recall has an absolute upper bound of 85%, regardless of how accurate the downstream ranking model is.
</NotebookCallout>

## Theoretical Foundations: The Probability Ranking Principle

The theoretical bedrock of information retrieval and ranking is Stephen Robertson's **Probability Ranking Principle (PRP)** (1977).

<GoDeeper title="Formal Derivation of the Probability Ranking Principle">
Let $q$ denote a user query, and let $d$ denote a candidate document in catalog $\mathcal{C}$. Let binary random variable $R \in \{0, 1\}$ represent document relevance, where $R = 1$ indicates that document $d$ satisfies user $q$, and $R = 0$ indicates non-relevance.

Assume that a user examines the retrieved list sequentially from rank $1$ to rank $k$. Let $C_1$ denote the utility (reward) gained by the user when encountering a relevant document, and let $C_0$ denote the penalty (cost in time or effort) incurred when examining a non-relevant document.

For an arbitrary document $d$, its expected utility is:
$$
\mathbb{E}[U(d)] = C_1 \cdot P(R = 1 \mid d, q) + C_0 \cdot P(R = 0 \mid d, q)
$$

Because $P(R = 0 \mid d, q) = 1 - P(R = 1 \mid d, q)$, we rearrange:
$$
\mathbb{E}[U(d)] = (C_1 - C_0) \cdot P(R = 1 \mid d, q) + C_0
$$

Assuming that finding a relevant document provides strictly greater utility than finding a non-relevant document ($C_1 > C_0$), the expected utility $\mathbb{E}[U(d)]$ is a strictly increasing linear function of the posterior probability of relevance $P(R = 1 \mid d, q)$.

Therefore, total expected user utility across a ranked slate is maximized if and only if documents are ordered in strictly decreasing order of their posterior probabilities of relevance:
$$
P(R = 1 \mid d_1, q) \ge P(R = 1 \mid d_2, q) \ge \dots \ge P(R = 1 \mid d_k, q)
$$
</GoDeeper>

### Why Modern Systems Extend Beyond Pure PRP

While the PRP provides the mathematical justification for sorting by predicted relevance, modern production systems must balance constraints that the classic PRP ignores:
1. **Relevance Independence Assumption**: The PRP assumes the utility of document $d_i$ is independent of document $d_j$. In practice, if document $d_1$ and document $d_2$ are identical duplicates, finding $d_1$ fulfills the user need; $d_2$ provides zero marginal utility despite having an identical probability of relevance.
2. **Diversity and Portfolio Effects**: Presenting five near-identical top-ranked items increases the risk of complete user abandonment if the user's specific intent differed slightly from the query interpretation. Re-ranking balances individual relevance against portfolio coverage.
3. **Multi-Objective Utilities**: Modern platforms optimize composite objectives that blend user satisfaction with business sustainability: $\text{Utility} = \alpha \cdot P(\text{click}) + \beta \cdot P(\text{purchase} \mid \text{click}) - \gamma \cdot \text{ReturnRate}$.

## Mathematical Analysis of Funnel Recall Cascading

To understand why candidate generation requires high recall, let us formalize how items flow through the funnel boundaries.

Let $\mathcal{C}$ denote the full catalog. Let $\mathcal{R}_q \subset \mathcal{C}$ represent the set of all items truly relevant to query or user context $q$.

The multi-stage funnel generates a sequence of progressively smaller candidate sets:
$$
\mathcal{C} = C_0 \supset C_1 \supset C_2 \supset C_3 \supset C_4 = \text{Slate}
$$

Where:
- $C_1$: Retrieved candidates from candidate generation.
- $C_2$: Surviving candidates after hard business filtering.
- $C_3$: Top candidates selected by the ranking model.
- $C_4$: Final presentation slate produced by re-ranking.

Because each stage operates strictly as a filter on the preceding stage's output ($C_{i} \subseteq C_{i-1}$), relevance recall is monotonically non-increasing across stages:

$$
\text{Recall}(C_i) = \frac{|C_i \cap \mathcal{R}_q|}{|\mathcal{R}_q|}
$$

$$
\text{Recall}(C_1) \ge \text{Recall}(C_2) \ge \text{Recall}(C_3) \ge \text{Recall}(C_4)
$$

### The Unrecoverable Loss Invariant

If a relevant item $d^* \in \mathcal{R}_q$ is excluded during candidate generation ($d^* \notin C_1$), its downstream survival probability is strictly zero:
$$
\mathbb{P}(d^* \in C_4 \mid d^* \notin C_1) = 0
$$

This asymmetric risk profile dictates the engineering trade-off at each stage:
- **Stage 1 (Retrieval)**: Tolerates low precision to minimize false negatives (type II errors). Surfacing 900 irrelevant candidates alongside 100 relevant candidates (precision = 0.10) is acceptable because downstream stages will eliminate the irrelevant items.
- **Stage 3 (Ranking)**: Must achieve high precision at the top ranks (minimizing false positives in slots 1 through 5) because users rarely inspect past the initial screen.

## Feature Engineering and Storage Across Stages

Data representations evolve fundamentally as candidates advance through the funnel:

```
[Catalog Level]      ──► Static Precomputed Embeddings, Inverted Postings, Taxonomy
                           (Read-only, sub-linear indices, updated daily/hourly)

[Retrieval Level]    ──► Bi-Encoder Vector Dot Products, BM25 Scores, Graph Proximity
                           (Decoupled representations, <1ms lookups)

[Ranking Level]      ──► Dynamic Feature Joins: User Profiling, Real-time Interaction Logs,
                           Cross-Attention Features, GBDT Trees
                           (High dimensionality, compute intensive)

[Re-Ranking Level]   ──► Slate-Level Features: Category Dispersion, Brand Coverage, Novelty
                           (Operates over sets of items rather than isolated items)
```

### 1. Static vs. Dynamic Features
- **Static Document Features**: Precomputed properties that do not depend on the user or immediate query. Examples include document length, item category, brand ID, historical quality score, and precomputed text embeddings. These are stored directly in index postings or memory-mapped arrays.
- **Dynamic Contextual Features**: Properties that exist only at the moment of request. Examples include current query text, user device type, user geo-IP location, time of day, and weather context.
- **Real-Time Interaction Features**: Rolling aggregations capturing immediate platform feedback. Examples include the number of times an item was viewed in the last 15 minutes, or whether the user dismissed a similar item three seconds ago in the current session. These require low-latency distributed key-value stores (e.g. Redis or Dragonfly) fed by streaming event engines (e.g. Apache Flink or Kafka).

### 2. The Point-in-Time Correctness Invariant
When training offline ranking models, logged training examples must reconstruct features exactly as they existed at the moment the prediction was served. If offline training logs join an item's current 30-day conversion rate rather than its historical rate at the timestamp of the impression, the model learns to exploit future data leakage, resulting in severe performance collapse when deployed to live traffic.

## Architectural Trade-Offs: Two-Tower vs. Cross-Encoder

A central architectural pattern across search and recommendation is the division between **Two-Tower Bi-Encoders** (used in candidate generation) and **Cross-Encoders** (used in ranking):

```
Two-Tower Architecture (Candidate Generation)

Query / User Context ──► [ Tower 1: f_θ ] ──► Vector u ──┐
                                                           ├──► Dot Product ⟨u, v⟩ ──► ANN Score
Item Document        ──► [ Tower 2: g_ϕ ] ──► Vector v ──┘
                          (Precomputed)


Cross-Encoder Architecture (Ranking / Scoring)

[ Query Tokens + Item Tokens ] ──► [ Full Multi-Head Cross Attention ] ──► Calibrated P(click)
```

| Dimension | Two-Tower Bi-Encoder | Cross-Encoder Ranker |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stage in Funnel** | Candidate Generation (Retrieval) | Ranking / Scoring |
| **Interaction Mechanism** | Late interaction: dot product or cosine similarity $\langle u, v \rangle$ | Early interaction: all query tokens attend to all item tokens |
| **Document Representation** | Precomputed offline and indexed in vector databases | Jointly computed at request time per (query, document) pair |
| **Computational Complexity** | $O(d)$ vector dot product per candidate | $O((L_q + L_d)^2)$ quadratic attention over concatenated tokens |
| **Throughput & Capacity** | Millions of items via ANN indexing (HNSW, IVF-PQ) | Hundreds of items via GPU/accelerator batches |
| **Semantic Precision** | Moderate: misses fine-grained token alignments and negations | High: models complex token interactions and contextual nuances |

## Tracing an Illustrative Candidate Lifecycle

To trace how representations and volumes evolve across stage boundaries, consider an illustrative e-commerce toy scenario with a hypothetical 2,000,000-item catalog. (All candidate counts, latencies, and scores below serve solely as a concrete walkthrough of stage mechanics).

A user submits the search query `"wireless noise cancelling headphones"`. Simultaneously, the recommendation engine receives their user profile: a customer in Chicago who previously browsed audio recording gear.

Here is how candidate item **H1** (a pair of high-end noise-cancelling headphones) moves through the funnel:

```
[Entire Catalog: 2,000,000 Products (Illustrative Toy Example)]
        │
        ▼ (Stage 1: Candidate Generation via Sparse BM25 + Vector ANN)
[Candidate Pool: ~1,200 Products]
  - Item H1 is retrieved: BM25 score 14.2 (exact title match)
  - Item H3 (competitor headphones) is retrieved via vector cosine similarity 0.84
  - Cable C4 (replacement audio cable) is retrieved via keyword match
        │
        ▼ (Stage 2: Deterministic Filtering)
[Filtered Candidates: ~450 Products]
  - Item H1 passes: in-stock at Chicago regional fulfillment center
  - Cable C4 is discarded: out-of-stock across Midwest region
        │
        ▼ (Stage 3: GBDT / Cross-Encoder Ranking)
[Scored Pool: Top 50 Ordered by P(click) * P(conversion)]
  - Item H1: Score = 0.94 (Rank 1)
  - Item H3: Score = 0.88 (Rank 2)
  - Earbuds E2: Score = 0.82 (Rank 3)
  - Speaker S5: Score = 0.71 (Rank 4)
        │
        ▼ (Stage 4: Re-Ranking & Business Logic)
[Adjusted Top 10 Slate]
  - Rank 1: Item H1 (Retained top slot)
  - Rank 2: Earbuds E2 (Promoted ahead of H3 to avoid consecutive listings from same brand)
  - Rank 3: Item H3
        │
        ▼ (Stage 5: Client Serving Payload)
[Illustrative JSON response returned to client]
```

At each transition, the candidate pool contracts while feature richness expands. Candidate generation operates over static document embeddings; ranking operates over real-time feature joins; re-ranking operates over list-level properties.

## When Not to Use a Multi-Stage Pipeline

A full multi-stage architecture introduces distributed complexity, operational overhead, and serialized network boundaries. Several common product scenarios do not warrant this architecture:

| Situation | Recommended Architecture | Reason |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Small catalogs under a few thousand items** (internal documentation, small SaaS settings) | Single-stage lexical or dense retrieval directly to UI | The entire catalog can be scanned or ranked directly without an intermediate candidate generation stage. |
| **Strict FIFO or deterministic audit logs** (financial ledgers, compliance records) | Direct database index lookups (`ORDER BY timestamp DESC`) | Learned ranking introduces non-deterministic score drift and breaks compliance guarantees. |
| **Purely transactional SKU searches** (exact part number or barcode lookup) | Direct primary key hash lookup in Redis or PostgreSQL | Semantic embeddings and ranking models introduce noise and false positives for exact identifiers. |
| **Cold-start products with zero interaction logs** | Rule-based curation or editorial popularity fallback | Ranking models overfit and degenerate without verified training impressions. |

## Remember

| Architectural Stage | Illustrative Input Volume | Illustrative Output Volume | Primary Optimization Objective | Representative Technologies |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Candidate Gen** | Millions of items | Hundreds to thousands | High Recall | BM25, Two-Tower ANN (HNSW), Item2Vec |
| **2. Filtering** | Thousands of items | Hundreds of items | Availability & Compliance | Redis Bloom filters, SQL boolean predicates |
| **3. Ranking** | Hundreds of items | Tens of items | High Precision (NDCG) | GBDT (LightGBM), DLRM, Cross-Encoders |
| **4. Re-Ranking** | Tens of items | Final display slate (5–20) | Diversity, Fairness & Freshness | Contextual Bandits, Deterministic Slates |
| **5. Serving** | Final display slate | Formatted client payload | Responsive End-to-End Latency | Low-latency RPC gateway, CDN caching |
